## Hendricks v. Stalnaker (Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia, 1989)

**Facts**
- In 1984, the Hendrickses bought 2.95 acres adjacent to Stalnaker's property for a home or trailer development.
- In 1985, Stalnaker built a home on his 10-acre tract and had two water wells dowsed. One well produced poor quality water due to a former strip mine. The other was near the Hendrickses' property.
- On December 31, 1985, Mr. Hendricks met with a sanitarian to find locations for a water well and septic system. The Health Department requires 100 feet between wells and septic systems. The only suitable location for a septic system on the Hendrickses' land was near Stalnaker's property.
- On January 13, 1986, the Hendrickses contacted the sanitarian to complete their septic system permit application.
- On January 13, 1986, Stalnaker called the sanitarian and learned about the Hendrickses' proposed septic system.
- On January 14, 1986, Stalnaker contacted a well driller, obtained a permit, and began drilling the second well near the Hendrickses' property.
- On January 15, 1986, the sanitarian informed Mr. Hendricks that no septic system permit could be issued because his proposed absorption field was within 100 feet of Stalnaker's new water well.
- The well was completed on January 25, 1986, but not connected to Stalnaker's home until January 1987.
- In January 1987, Mr. Hendricks installed a septic system without a permit, leaving it inoperative pending the lawsuit.

**Procedural History**
- The Hendrickses sued Stalnaker in the Circuit Court of Lewis County, seeking to declare the well a private nuisance, abate it, and obtain damages.
- In a bifurcated trial, the jury found the well was a private nuisance, and the judge ordered its abatement. The jury found for Stalnaker on damages.
- Stalnaker appealed to the Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia.

**Issue**
- Is a water well a private nuisance if it prevents a neighbor from installing a septic system, when both uses are necessary for residential land use and neither party has a practical alternative?

**Holding**
- Defendant Stalnaker wins. The water well is not an unreasonable use of land and therefore does not constitute a private nuisance. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Private Nuisance.** A private nuisance is a substantial and unreasonable interference with the private use and enjoyment of another’s land.
- **Intentional Interference.** An interference is intentional when the actor knows or should know that the conduct is causing a substantial and unreasonable interference.
- **Unreasonable Interference (Balancing Test).** An intentional interference is unreasonable when the gravity of the harm outweighs the social value of the activity alleged to cause the harm.
    - Factors for gravity of harm include: extent and character of harm, social value of the invaded use, suitability of the invaded use to the locality, and burden on the harmed person to avoid harm.
    - Factors for utility of conduct include: social value of the primary purpose of the conduct, suitability of the conduct to the locality, and impracticability of preventing the invasion.

**Reasoning**
- The Hendrickses' inability to operate a septic system is a substantial interference with their land use.
- Stalnaker's installation of the well was intentional, but not malicious; he sought an adequate water supply and found no alternative.
- The critical question is whether the interference (the well) was unreasonable, determined by balancing the landowners' interests.
    - Both a water well and a septic system are necessary for residential use of the land.
    - Both uses burden adjacent property: a well requires a 100-foot non-interference zone, and a septic system's 100-foot safety zone may intrude on adjacent property. A septic system places a more invasive burden due to potential drainage.
    - Both parties lack inexpensive and practical alternatives for their respective uses.
    - The balancing of interests is at least equal, or slightly favors the water well, because both are necessary and neither party has a clear alternative.
- The Hendrickses did not show that the balancing of interests favored their septic system.
- The evidence does not demonstrate that the water well is an unreasonable use of land.
- When material facts are undisputed and only one inference can be drawn, the question becomes one of law for the court. Here, the trial court should have held as a matter of law that the well was not a private nuisance.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the balancing test for private nuisance, particularly when two essential land uses conflict. It connects to **Jost v. Dairyland Power Cooperative** and **Campbell v. Seaman** by showing how courts weigh the utility of the defendant's conduct against the harm to the plaintiff's use and enjoyment of land. Unlike those cases where the defendant's activity was clearly harmful (smell, fumes), here both parties' desired uses are beneficial and necessary, leading to a more even balance.
2. What if Stalnaker had drilled the well solely to spite the Hendrickses, knowing it would prevent their septic system, even if he had another viable water source? Would that change the outcome under the "malicious or indecent conduct" factor mentioned by the court?

**Takeaway:** When two necessary and reasonable land uses conflict, and neither party has a practical alternative, a court applying a nuisance balancing test may find neither use to be unreasonable.
