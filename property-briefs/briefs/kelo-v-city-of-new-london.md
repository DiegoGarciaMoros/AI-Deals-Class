## Kelo v. City of New London (Supreme Court of the United States, 2005)

**Facts**
- In 1990, New London, Connecticut, was designated a "distressed municipality" due to economic decline.
- In 1996, a naval facility employing over 1,500 people closed in the Fort Trumbull area of New London.
- In 1998, the city's unemployment rate was nearly double the state average, and its population was at its lowest since 1920.
- The New London Development Corporation (NLDC), a private nonprofit entity, was reactivated to plan economic development for the city.
- The State of Connecticut authorized bond issues to support NLDC's planning and the creation of a state park.
- Pharmaceutical company Pfizer Inc. announced in February 1998 that it would build a research facility adjacent to Fort Trumbull.
- The NLDC developed an integrated development plan for 90 acres of the Fort Trumbull area, intending to capitalize on Pfizer's arrival, create jobs, increase tax revenue, and revitalize the area.
- The plan included parcels for a waterfront hotel, residences, a Coast Guard Museum, research and office space, parking, and retail.
- In January 2000, the New London City Council approved the development plan and designated the NLDC as its development agent, authorizing it to acquire property by purchase or eminent domain.
- The NLDC purchased most of the real estate but failed to negotiate purchases with the petitioners.
- In November 2000, the NLDC initiated condemnation proceedings against the petitioners' properties.
- Petitioners owned 15 properties in the Fort Trumbull area, some occupied by owners and some investment properties, none of which were blighted or in poor condition.

**Procedural History**
- Petitioners sued the City of New London in the New London Superior Court, claiming the takings violated the Fifth Amendment's "public use" restriction.
- The Superior Court granted a permanent restraining order prohibiting the taking of properties in Parcel 4A (park or marina support) but denied relief for properties in Parcel 3 (office space).
- Both sides appealed to the Supreme Court of Connecticut.
- The Supreme Court of Connecticut held that all proposed takings were valid, finding that economic development qualified as a valid public use under both federal and state constitutions.
- The Supreme Court of Connecticut reversed the trial court's decision regarding Parcel 4A, finding its intended use sufficiently definite.
- Petitioners sought certiorari from the U.S. Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Can a city take private property for the purpose of economic development, transferring it to private parties, if the taking is part of a comprehensive plan to revitalize an economically distressed area and is expected to generate public benefits like jobs and increased tax revenue?

**Holding**
Respondent (City of New London) wins. A city's taking of private property for the purpose of economic development, as part of a comprehensive plan to revitalize an economically distressed area, satisfies the "public use" requirement of the Fifth Amendment, even if the property is transferred to private parties. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Fifth Amendment Takings Clause.** Private property shall not be taken for public use, without just compensation.
- **Public Use Doctrine (Broad Interpretation).** The concept of "public use" in the Fifth Amendment is broadly interpreted to mean "public purpose," reflecting deference to legislative judgments in this field.
- **Deference to Legislative Judgment.** Courts should afford legislatures broad latitude in determining what public needs justify the use of the takings power.
- **Public Purpose Justification.** A taking is for a public use if it serves a public purpose, even if the property is subsequently transferred to private parties.
- **Rejected: Literal "Use by the Public" Test.** The Court has long rejected any literal requirement that condemned property be put into use for the general public.
- **Rejected: "Purely Private Taking" Prohibition.** A taking for the sole purpose of transferring property from one private party to another, to confer a private benefit, is forbidden.
- **Rejected: Pretextual Public Purpose.** A taking under the mere pretext of a public purpose, when its actual purpose is to bestow a private benefit, is forbidden.
- **Rejected: Heightened Scrutiny for Economic Development Takings.** There is no basis for a new bright-line rule that economic development does not qualify as a public use, nor for requiring "reasonable certainty" that expected public benefits will accrue.

**Reasoning**
- The Court's jurisprudence has consistently defined "public use" broadly as "public purpose," reflecting deference to legislative judgments.
- The City's development plan was a "carefully considered" plan to revitalize an economically distressed city, projected to create jobs, increase tax revenue, and improve the area.
- The Court has previously upheld takings where the property was transferred to private parties for public purposes, such as eliminating blight (Berman v. Parker) or breaking up a land oligopoly (Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff).
- The fact that private parties will benefit from the development does not negate the public character of the taking, as the government's pursuit of a public purpose often benefits private parties.
- The Court rejected petitioners' argument for a bright-line rule against economic development takings, stating that promoting economic development is a traditional government function and there's no principled way to distinguish it from other recognized public purposes.
- The Court also rejected the argument for requiring "reasonable certainty" of public benefits, as this would involve courts in empirical debates over the wisdom of legislative initiatives, which is contrary to precedent.
- The Court emphasized that its role is not to second-guess the City's judgments about the efficacy of its plan or the specific lands needed, but only to determine if the takings are for a "public use."
- States are free to impose stricter "public use" requirements than the federal baseline.

**Concurrence (Justice Kennedy)**
- Agrees with the Court's opinion but emphasizes that transfers intended to confer benefits on particular, favored private entities, with only incidental or pretextual public benefits, are forbidden.
- A court should seriously review plausible accusations of impermissible favoritism, though with a presumption of government reasonableness and public purpose.
- The trial court's extensive inquiry in this case found no evidence of illicit favoritism, and the plan was a comprehensive effort to address a serious citywide depression with substantial public benefits.
- A more stringent standard of review might be appropriate for narrowly drawn categories of takings where the risk of undetected impermissible favoritism is acute, but this case does not present such circumstances.

**Dissent (Justice O’Connor)**
- Argues that the Court abandons a basic limitation on government power, making all private property vulnerable to being taken for transfer to another private owner if it might be "upgraded" for more beneficial use.
- Contends that equating incidental public benefits from private use with "public use" effectively deletes the words "for public use" from the Fifth Amendment.
- Distinguishes Berman and Midkiff by arguing that those cases involved the elimination of existing harmful property uses (blight, land oligopoly), whereas Kelo's homes were well-maintained and not harmful.
- Believes the "public purpose" interpretation of "public use" is theoretically flawed and leads to an unprincipled application, as nearly any lawful use of private property generates some incidental public benefit.
- Expresses concern that the decision will disproportionately harm politically powerless communities, allowing property to be transferred from those with fewer resources to those with more.
- Criticizes the Court's reliance on the idea that states can impose stricter limits, calling it an abdication of federal responsibility to enforce the Constitution.

**Dissent (Justice Thomas)**
- Argues that the Court replaces the "Public Use" Clause with a "Public Purpose" Clause, effectively erasing it from the Constitution.
- Believes the original meaning of "public use" requires that the government or the public have a legal right to use the property, not merely that the taking serves any public purpose.
- Points to the common law distinction between nuisance law (which requires no compensation) and eminent domain (which requires compensation) to support a narrower reading of "public use."
- Criticizes the Court's precedents (Bradley, Clark, Strickley) for adopting the "public purpose" test with little analysis of original meaning and for affording undue deference to legislative judgments.
- Argues that the "public purpose" standard is not susceptible to principled application and that the "actual use" test is easier to administer.
- Predicts that the decision will have harmful consequences, exacerbating the displacement of poor and minority communities for the benefit of powerful private interests.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a landmark decision regarding the **Takings Clause** of the Fifth Amendment, specifically the "public use" requirement. It significantly broadens the interpretation of "public use" to include economic development, even when property is transferred to private entities. It connects to **Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon** and **Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City** by further defining the limits of government power over private property, but in the context of eminent domain rather than regulatory takings. It also contrasts with cases like **Jacque v. Steenberg Homes** and **Intel Corp. v. Hamidi** which emphasize the right to exclude, as here the government is compelling exclusion for a broader public purpose.
2. A key tension is between the majority's broad, deferential interpretation of "public use" and the dissents' call for a narrower, originalist interpretation. A hypo could be: If a city wants to condemn a successful, family-owned restaurant to build a larger, chain restaurant that promises more jobs and tax revenue, would this be a valid public use under Kelo?

**Takeaway:** The Supreme Court held that economic development qualifies as a "public use" under the Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause, allowing governments to condemn private property for transfer to other private entities as part of a comprehensive revitalization plan.
