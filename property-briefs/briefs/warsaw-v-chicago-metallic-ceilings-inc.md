## Warsaw v. Chicago Metallic Ceilings Inc. (Supreme Court of California, 1984)

**Facts**
- In 1972, plaintiffs and defendant acquired contiguous, unimproved parcels of land from a common owner in Vernon, California.
- Plaintiffs constructed a large commercial building on their parcel, covering almost the entire property, with a 40-foot wide paved driveway along the northern edge for access to loading docks.
- Defendant constructed a smaller building on its parcel, leaving a 150-foot wide vacant strip of ground along the side abutting plaintiffs' property.
- Plaintiffs' 40-foot driveway was inadequate for large trucks to turn and position themselves at the loading docks without driving onto defendant's property.
- The possibility of creating an easement over defendant's property was considered and rejected during the original negotiations between the seller, plaintiffs, and defendant.
- From 1972 to 1979, trucks servicing plaintiffs' facility used a portion of the vacant ground on defendant's property to enter, turn, park, and leave the loading dock area.
- On at least two occasions during this period, plaintiffs unsuccessfully tried to acquire an easement from defendant or create mutual easements.
- In 1979, defendant planned to build a warehouse on the southerly portion of its property, including the area used by plaintiffs.
- Defendant raised a dirt pad along its southerly property line, approximately five feet from the property line, which blocked plaintiffs' use of the area.
- Plaintiffs filed an action for injunctive and declaratory relief.
- The trial court denied plaintiffs' request for a preliminary injunction.
- Defendant proceeded to erect a building on the contested area while the litigation was pending.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court found that plaintiffs had acquired a 25-foot wide prescriptive easement over defendant's property and ordered defendant to remove the portion of its building interfering with the easement. The trial court also reserved jurisdiction to award damages if defendant failed to comply.
- Defendant appealed the trial court's judgment.
- The Court of Appeal affirmed the trial court's finding of a prescriptive easement and the mandatory injunction.
- The Supreme Court of California granted a hearing to consider compensation issues.

**Issue**
Can a property owner who acquires a valid prescriptive easement be required to compensate the underlying property owner for the fair market value of the easement or the cost of removing encroaching structures?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (easement owner) wins. One who acquires a valid prescriptive easement cannot be required to compensate the underlying property owner for the fair market value of the easement, nor for the cost of removing encroaching structures if the encroachment was willful. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Elements of Prescriptive Easement.** The party claiming a prescriptive easement must show use of the property that has been open, notorious, continuous, and adverse for an uninterrupted period of five years.
- **Definite and Certain Line of Travel.** The existence of a prescriptive easement must be shown by a definite and certain line of travel for the statutory period, though slight deviations from the accustomed route will not defeat it.
- **Presumption of Adverse Use.** Continuous use of an easement over a long period of time without the landowner's interference is presumptive evidence of its existence and, in the absence of evidence of mere permissive use, is sufficient to sustain a judgment.
- **Mandatory Injunction for Easement Protection.** A court of equity may issue a mandatory injunction for the protection and preservation of an easement, including an order for removal of an obstruction already erected.
- **Balancing Hardships for Encroachments.** A court has discretion to balance the hardships and deny removal of an encroachment if it was innocently made, does not irreparably injure the plaintiff, and the cost of removal would greatly exceed the inconvenience to the plaintiff.
- **Willful Encroachment.** If an encroaching structure was willfully erected with knowledge of the claimed easement, a mandatory injunction for its removal may issue even if the cost of removal is great.
- **No Compensation for Prescriptive Easement.** The statutory procedure for acquiring an easement by prescription retains the traditional common law rule that such an easement may be obtained without incurring any liability to the underlying property owner.
- **Legislative Prerogative for Compensation.** Any decision to alter the system of acquiring an interest in land by prescription by requiring the payment of compensation is a matter for the Legislature.
- **Rejected: Equitable Power to Require Compensation.** Rejected: Courts acting in equity have the power to require payment of fair market value for a prescriptive easement.

**Reasoning**
- The trial court's findings that plaintiffs established the elements of a prescriptive easement were supported by substantial evidence.
    - The use was open and notorious, as trucks regularly used the area.
    - The use was continuous for more than five years.
    - The use was adverse, as defendant's refusal to negotiate an express easement indicated no permission was given.
    - The line of travel was sufficiently definite, despite slight variations by different truck drivers, as they all used the parcel for the same purpose.
- The mandatory injunction ordering removal of defendant's building was appropriate because defendant erected the structure after plaintiffs filed suit and while litigation was pending, indicating a willful encroachment.
    - Defendant "gambled on the outcome of the action and lost."
    - Courts have ordered removal of willful encroachments despite disproportionate hardship to the defendant.
- Requiring plaintiffs to compensate defendant for the fair market value of the easement is not authorized by statute or common law.
    - Civil Code section 1007 states that occupancy for the statutory period confers "title by prescription," which is "sufficient against all."
    - Requiring compensation would defeat the policies underlying adverse possession and prescription, which aim to reduce litigation and stabilize long-continued property uses.
    - The "lost grant" theory of prescription justifies its continuance based on functional utility in terminating controversies and stabilizing property uses.
    - Altering this system to require compensation is a matter for the Legislature, not the courts.
- Requiring plaintiffs to contribute to the cost of removing the encroaching structure is not equitable.
    - Defendant erected the structure with prior notice of plaintiffs' claim and while the suit was pending.
    - It would be inequitable to charge plaintiffs for removing a structure willfully erected by defendant.

**Dissent (Reynoso, J.)**
- The majority's decision to deny compensation for the fair market value of the easement is unjust.
- While the declaration of a prescriptive easement is an action at law, the protection of that right is an action in equity, giving courts power to impose conditions.
- Civil Code section 1007 merely codifies the general concept of prescriptive easements and does not remove the courts' traditional equitable power to ensure fairness.
- The court in equity should exercise its power to require plaintiffs to pay fair market value for the property interest acquired, especially since plaintiffs are "trespassers" acquiring valuable property rights "free of charge."
- The policy reasons cited by the majority (reducing litigation, protecting possession, favoring use over disuse) are unconvincing in this case.
    - Denying compensation does not reduce litigation; it forces owners to sue when trespass occurs.
    - While possession is protected, plaintiffs should pay for it.
    - Modern society prefers planned use over ad hoc use by a trespasser, especially in urban settings.
- The court's role is to apply a "conception of justice in accordance with the prevailing reason and conscience of the time," and the majority's decision contravenes modern notions of fairness.
- The case should be remanded to fix reasonable compensation (fair market value) to be paid by plaintiffs to defendant.

**Concurrence (Grodin, J.)**
- Agrees with the policy criticisms in the dissenting opinion regarding the lack of compensation for prescriptive easements in modern society.
- Believes that any change to this area of law should come from the Legislature, not the courts.
- Notes that the Legislature has already addressed the harshness of prescriptive easements by enacting Civil Code section 1008, which allows property owners to prevent acquisition of an easement by posting a sign. This legislative attention suggests the courts should defer to the Legislature for further changes.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the **prescriptive easement** doctrine, particularly the lack of a compensation requirement for the owner whose land is burdened. It connects to **adverse possession** (as the court discusses the underlying policies) by showing that the law favors long-standing use and stability of property interests, even if acquired without permission. It also touches on the equitable powers of courts, similar to cases like *Pile v. Pedrick* or *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands*, regarding mandatory injunctions for encroachments, but here the willfulness of the encroacher is key.
2. The tension between the majority's strict application of the statutory and common law rule for prescriptive easements and the dissent's call for equitable compensation highlights a fundamental debate in property law: should courts prioritize established legal rules and stability, or should they adapt to modern notions of fairness and prevent "unjust enrichment"? This raises a hypo: What if the defendant's encroachment was truly innocent and the cost of removal was astronomical compared to the benefit to the easement holder? Would the court still order removal without compensation?

**Takeaway:** A party who establishes a prescriptive easement is generally not required to pay compensation to the burdened landowner, and a willful encroacher may be forced to remove their structure without contribution from the easement holder.
