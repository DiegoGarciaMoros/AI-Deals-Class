## Pocono Springs Civic Association v. MacKenzie (Superior Court of Pennsylvania, 1995)

**Facts**
- On October 14, 1969, the MacKenzies purchased a vacant lot in Pocono Springs Development.
- In 1987, the MacKenzies decided to sell their vacant lot.
- An offer to purchase the lot was conditioned on its suitability for an on-lot sewage system.
- An inspection determined the lot had inadequate soil for proper percolation, and the sale was lost.
- The MacKenzies attempted to turn the lot over to the Pocono Springs Civic Association (Association), but the Association declined.
- The MacKenzies tried to persuade the Association to accept the lot as a gift for a park, but the Association again declined.
- In 1986, the MacKenzies stopped paying real estate taxes on the lot.
- In 1988, the Wayne County Tax Claim Bureau offered the property for sale due to delinquent taxes, but there were no purchasers.
- In 1990, the lot was again offered for sale by the Tax Claim Bureau, but it was not sold and was placed on its "repository" list.
- The MacKenzies signed a notarized statement expressing their desire to abandon the lot and mailed it to "all interested parties."
- The MacKenzies stopped accepting mail regarding the property and had not visited the lot or used the development's services since 1986.
- The Association sued the MacKenzies for unpaid association fees.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court granted summary judgment in favor of the Association, finding the MacKenzies had not abandoned their property and were still obligated to pay association fees.
- The MacKenzies appealed.

**Issue**
- Can owners of real property held in fee simple abandon that property to avoid paying association fees?

**Holding**
- The Association wins. Owners of real property held in fee simple cannot abandon their property under Pennsylvania law. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Abandonment of Property.** Abandoned property is that to which an owner has voluntarily relinquished all right, title, claim, and possession with the intention of terminating ownership, without vesting it in any other person, and with the intention of not reclaiming further possession or resuming ownership, possession, or enjoyment.
- **Abandonment of Real Property.** Under Pennsylvania law, real property owned in fee simple with perfect title cannot be abandoned.
- **Rejected: Intent to Abandon.** An owner's intent to abandon real property is irrelevant if the law states that such property cannot be abandoned.
- **Rejected: Non-payment of Taxes/Non-use.** Neither refusal to pay taxes nor non-use of real property constitutes abandonment of perfect title.

**Reasoning**
- The MacKenzies still hold perfect title to the lot, as evidenced by their recorded deed, and have not relinquished their rights, title, claim, and possession.
- Pennsylvania law explicitly states that perfect title to real property cannot be abandoned.
    - The doctrine of abandonment applies only to imperfect titles or inchoate rights, not to perfect titles.
    - No legal authority in Pennsylvania supports the abandonment of real property held in fee simple.
- The MacKenzies' actions, such as attempting to give the lot away, ceasing tax payments, and signing a statement of abandonment, do not legally constitute abandonment of fee simple real property.
- The MacKenzies' intent to abandon is irrelevant because the law dictates that real property cannot be abandoned, leaving no factual issue for a jury.
- The MacKenzies are bound by the covenant in their deed to pay association fees as long as they retain title.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes a bright-line rule that fee simple ownership of real property cannot be abandoned, contrasting with the abandonment of personal property or lesser interests like easements (as seen in cases involving railway lines). It reinforces the idea of the permanence of fee simple title and the obligations that come with it. This case could be compared to cases like *Eyerman v. Mercantile Trust Co.* where the court considered limitations on an owner's right to destroy property, but here the owners are trying to shed obligations rather than destroy the property.
2. What if the property became a public nuisance or environmental hazard? Would the state have any mechanism to force the MacKenzies to take action or to take title itself, even if they cannot "abandon" it?

**Takeaway:** Under Pennsylvania law, an owner cannot abandon real property held in fee simple, regardless of their intent or efforts to relinquish it, and thus remains responsible for associated obligations.
