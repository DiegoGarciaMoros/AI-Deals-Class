## Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City (Supreme Court of the United States, 1978)

**Facts**
- Over the past 50 years, states and municipalities enacted laws to preserve historic landmarks and districts.
- In 1965, New York City adopted its Landmarks Preservation Law to protect historic landmarks and neighborhoods.
- The Landmarks Preservation Commission (Commission) was established to identify and designate landmarks.
- The law requires landmark owners to keep exterior features in good repair and obtain Commission approval for alterations or new construction.
- The law also provides for transferable development rights (TDRs) for landmark owners who have not developed their property to the full extent permitted by zoning laws.
- In 1967, the Commission designated Grand Central Terminal (Terminal), owned by Penn Central Transportation Co. (Penn Central), as a landmark.
- Penn Central did not seek judicial review of the designation.
- In 1968, Penn Central leased the airspace above the Terminal to UGP Properties, Inc. (UGP) to construct a multistory office building.
- UGP and Penn Central submitted two plans (Breuer I and Breuer II Revised) to the Commission for approval to construct an office building atop the Terminal.
- The Commission denied a certificate of no exterior effect and subsequently denied a certificate of appropriateness for both plans.
- Penn Central did not seek judicial review of the denial of either certificate.
- Penn Central filed suit in New York Supreme Court, claiming the Landmarks Preservation Law constituted a "taking" without just compensation and deprived them of property without due process.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court granted injunctive and declaratory relief to Penn Central.
- The New York Supreme Court, Appellate Division, reversed, holding that the restrictions were necessary for a legitimate public purpose and Penn Central failed to prove deprivation of all reasonable beneficial use.
- The New York Court of Appeals affirmed, finding no "taking" because the law did not transfer control of the property and Penn Central failed to show denial of a reasonable return.
- Penn Central appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Does New York City's Landmarks Preservation Law, by restricting the development of Grand Central Terminal, constitute a "taking" of private property for public use without just compensation in violation of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments?

**Holding**
Defendant (New York City) wins. The application of New York City's Landmarks Preservation Law to Grand Central Terminal does not constitute a "taking" of appellants' property. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Takings Clause (Fifth Amendment).** The Fifth Amendment prohibits the government from taking private property for public use without just compensation, applicable to states via the Fourteenth Amendment.
- **Ad Hoc Factual Inquiries.** Whether a particular restriction constitutes a "taking" depends largely on the specific circumstances of the case, involving essentially ad hoc, factual inquiries.
- **Factors for a Taking.** Key factors in determining if a taking has occurred include:
    - The economic impact of the regulation on the claimant.
    - The extent to which the regulation has interfered with distinct investment-backed expectations.
    - The character of the governmental action.
- **Physical Invasion vs. Public Program.** A "taking" is more readily found when governmental interference is a physical invasion than when it arises from a public program adjusting economic benefits and burdens for the common good.
- **Diminution in Value.** Diminution in property value, standing alone, does not establish a "taking" if the regulation is reasonably related to the promotion of the general welfare.
- **Single Parcel Rule.** "Taking" jurisprudence does not divide a single parcel into discrete segments to determine if rights in a particular segment have been entirely abrogated; rather, it focuses on the parcel as a whole.
- **Legitimate Public Purpose.** Land-use regulations that promote the "health, safety, morals, or general welfare" are generally upheld, even if they prohibit the most beneficial use of the property.
- **Transferable Development Rights (TDRs).** TDRs, while not necessarily "just compensation" if a taking occurred, can mitigate financial burdens and are considered when assessing the impact of regulation.

**Reasoning**
- The Court recognized that states and cities may enact land-use restrictions to enhance the quality of life by preserving historic and aesthetic features.
- Appellants did not dispute that historic preservation is a permissible governmental goal or that the restrictions were appropriate means to achieve it.
- The Court rejected the argument that denying the ability to exploit "air rights" above the Terminal automatically constitutes a taking, emphasizing that takings jurisprudence considers the parcel as a whole, not discrete segments.
- The Court found that the Landmarks Law did not interfere with Penn Central's present use of the Terminal as a railroad station with office space and concessions, which was its primary expectation.
- The Court concluded that Penn Central could still obtain a "reasonable return" on its investment, as found by the New York courts.
- The Court noted that the Commission had not prohibited all construction above the Terminal, only the specific large-scale plans submitted, and Penn Central had not sought approval for smaller structures.
- The transferable development rights (TDRs) afforded to Penn Central, while not necessarily "just compensation" for a taking, were found to be valuable and mitigated the financial burdens of the law.
- The Court distinguished this case from *Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon* because the Landmarks Law did not make it commercially impracticable to use the property and did not have the same effect as destroying rights.
- The governmental action was characterized as a public program promoting the general welfare, not a physical invasion or appropriation for a uniquely public function.
- The law applies to a large number of structures, providing assurances against arbitrariness, and the benefits of landmark preservation accrue to all citizens, including the property owners.

**Dissent (Rehnquist)**
- The dissent argued that the landmark designation imposed a substantial cost on Penn Central with little or no offsetting benefit, unlike typical zoning where there is "an average reciprocity of advantage."
- It contended that the law imposed an affirmative duty on Penn Central to maintain the Terminal at its own expense and severely restricted its use, subjecting it to a nonconsensual servitude.
- The dissent asserted that the city was not prohibiting a "noxious use" of property, but rather preventing a beneficial use (construction of an office building) for aesthetic reasons.
- It believed that the government was forcing a few individuals to bear public burdens that should be borne by the public as a whole, which the Fifth Amendment prohibits.
- The dissent questioned whether TDRs constituted "just compensation," noting the New York Court of Appeals' acknowledgment of their defects and speculative market value, and would have remanded for a determination on this point.
- It warned that the Court's decision allowed the city to achieve its desire to improve the public condition by a "shorter cut than the constitutional way of paying for the change," echoing Justice Holmes in *Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon*.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a landmark (pun intended) in regulatory takings jurisprudence, establishing the **ad hoc factual inquiry** test. It is often contrasted with *Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon*, which found a taking, to illustrate the difficulty in drawing the line between a legitimate regulation and a compensable taking. It also introduces the concept of **transferable development rights (TDRs)** as a mitigating factor in takings analysis.
2. The dissent raises an important question about the "parcel as a whole" rule and how to define the relevant property interest when air rights are restricted. It also highlights the "average reciprocity of advantage" concept from *Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon* as a key distinction between general zoning and specific landmark designations.

**Takeaway:** A government regulation that restricts the use of private property for a legitimate public purpose, such as historic preservation, does not constitute a compensable taking if it allows the owner a reasonable beneficial use of the property as a whole and does not interfere with distinct investment-backed expectations.
