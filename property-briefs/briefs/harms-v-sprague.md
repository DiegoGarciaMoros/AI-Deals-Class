## Harms v. Sprague (Illinois Supreme Court, 1984)

**Facts**
- On June 26, 1973, William Harms and his brother John Harms took title to real estate as joint tenants.
- On June 12, 1981, John Harms co-signed a promissory note for $7,000 with Charles Sprague, payable to Carl and Mary Simmons.
- To secure the note, John Harms executed a mortgage on his undivided one-half interest in the joint tenancy property to the Simmonses. William Harms was unaware of this mortgage.
- On December 10, 1981, John Harms died.
- John Harms' will devised his entire estate to Charles Sprague.
- On December 29, 1981, the mortgage given by John Harms to the Simmonses was recorded.

**Procedural History**
- William Harms filed a complaint to quiet title and for declaratory judgment in the circuit court.
- Sprague counterclaimed, challenging William's claim of full ownership and asking the court to recognize his interest as a tenant in common, subject to the mortgage.
- The trial court held that the mortgage severed the joint tenancy and survived John's death as a lien against the one-half interest passing to Sprague.
- The appellate court reversed, finding the mortgage did not sever the joint tenancy and William, as the surviving joint tenant, owned the property unencumbered.
- Sprague petitioned for leave to appeal, and the Simmonses joined. The Illinois Supreme Court allowed the petition.

**Issue**
- Does a mortgage executed by one joint tenant on their interest in the property sever the joint tenancy, and does that mortgage survive the mortgagor's death as a lien on the property?

**Holding**
- William Harms (plaintiff) wins. A mortgage by one joint tenant does not sever the joint tenancy, and the mortgage does not survive the mortgagor's death as a lien on the property. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Joint Tenancy Unities.** A joint tenancy requires the unities of interest, title, time, and possession. The destruction of any unity by one joint tenant severs the joint tenancy.
- **Lien Theory of Mortgages.** In Illinois, a mortgage is considered a mere lien on the mortgagor's interest in property, not a conveyance of title.
- **Mortgage and Joint Tenancy Severance.** The execution of a mortgage by one joint tenant on their interest in the property does not sever the joint tenancy because it does not destroy the unity of title.
- **Judgment Lien and Joint Tenancy Severance.** A judgment lien on a joint tenant's interest does not sever the joint tenancy unless there is a conveyance by deed following the expiration of a redemption period.
- **Right of Survivorship.** An inherent feature of joint tenancy is the right of survivorship, where the last survivor takes the whole estate.
- **Extinguishment of Interest.** Upon the death of a joint tenant, their property right in the joint tenancy is extinguished.
- **Mortgage Survival.** A mortgage on a joint tenant's interest does not survive the death of the mortgagor because the mortgagor's interest in the property ceases to exist at death, and with it, the lien.
- **Rejected: Title Theory of Mortgages.** A mortgage is a conveyance of a legal estate vesting title in the mortgagee. (This theory was historically followed in Illinois but has been rejected.)

**Reasoning**
- The court adheres to the rule that a lien on a joint tenant's interest does not sever the joint tenancy unless there is a conveyance by deed.
- Illinois follows the **lien theory of mortgages**, meaning a mortgage is a lien, not a conveyance of title.
    - Early Illinois cases followed the title theory but recognized the mortgagee's title was limited.
    - *Kling v. Ghilarducci* expressly characterized a mortgage as a mere lien, not a separation of title.
    - Since a mortgage is a lien, it has the same effect as other liens (like judgment liens) which do not sever joint tenancies.
- Because the mortgage did not sever the joint tenancy, William Harms' right of survivorship became operative upon John's death, making William the sole owner.
- The mortgage executed by John Harms does not survive as a lien on William's property.
    - A surviving joint tenant takes by virtue of the original conveyance, not as a successor to the deceased.
    - John Harms' property right was extinguished at his death, and thus the mortgage lien on that interest also ceased to exist.
- The court declined to consider the applicability of Probate Act section 20-19 because the issue was waived at trial and, in any event, the lien no longer existed.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the **four unities** of joint tenancy and how they are affected by encumbrances. It directly contrasts with jurisdictions that follow the "title theory" of mortgages, where a mortgage *would* sever a joint tenancy. It connects to the general concept of **severance** in concurrent estates.
2. What if John Harms had defaulted on the mortgage and the Simmonses had foreclosed and received a master's deed *before* John's death? Would that have severed the joint tenancy?

**Takeaway:** In a lien theory state, a mortgage by one joint tenant does not sever the joint tenancy, and the mortgage lien is extinguished upon the mortgagor's death due to the right of survivorship.
