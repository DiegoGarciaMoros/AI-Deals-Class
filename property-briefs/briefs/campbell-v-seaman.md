## Campbell v. Seaman (New York Court of Appeals, 1876)

**Facts**
- Around 1849, the plaintiffs, Samuel B. Campbell et al., acquired 30-40 acres of land in Castleton, New York.
- From 1857 to 1859, the plaintiffs built an expensive dwelling-house on their land and improved the land with grading, terracing, roads, walks, and planting ornamental and useful trees and shrubs.
- The defendant, Nathan N. Seaman, owned adjoining land which he had used as a brick-yard for some years.
- The defendant's brick-yard was about 1,320 feet south of the plaintiffs' dwelling-house and 567 feet south of their woods.
- In 1869 and 1870, the defendant burned bricks using anthracite coal.
- During the burning process, sulphurous acid gas was generated, which escaped from the kiln during the last two days of burning.
- When the wind blew from the south, this gas was carried over the plaintiffs' land.
- The gas killed the foliage on the plaintiffs' white and yellow pines and Norway spruce, and after repeated attacks, killed 100 to 150 valuable pine and spruce trees, and injured their grape vines and plum trees.
- The defendant's premises had been known and used as a brick-yard for over twenty-five years, but the use of anthracite coal for brick burning was not continuous for twenty years. From 1840 to 1853, no bricks were burned. From 1853 to 1857, bricks were burned, then discontinued until 1867. From 1857 to 1867, the land was used for agriculture.
- The plaintiffs objected to the brick burning before bringing suit.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs brought an action to recover damages and to restrain the continuance of the alleged nuisance.
- A referee found in favor of the plaintiffs, awarding damages and an injunction.
- The General Term of the Supreme Court affirmed the judgment.
- Defendant appealed to the New York Court of Appeals.

**Issue**
Can a landowner be enjoined from operating a lawful business on their property if it causes substantial and irreparable injury to a neighbor's property, even if the business existed before the neighbor improved their land?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (landowner) wins. A landowner can be enjoined from operating a lawful business on their property if it causes substantial and irreparable injury to a neighbor's property, even if the business existed before the neighbor improved their land. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Nuisance.** Every person is bound to make a reasonable use of their property so as to occasion no unnecessary damage or annoyance to their neighbor. An unreasonable, unwarrantable, or unlawful use of property that produces material annoyance, inconvenience, discomfort, or hurt to a neighbor constitutes a nuisance.
- **Reasonable Use.** What constitutes a reasonable use of one's property depends on the circumstances of each case; a use lawful and reasonable in one locality may be unlawful and unreasonable in another.
- **Irreparable Injury.** An injury is irreparable if the damaged property cannot be replaced, or if money damages cannot adequately compensate for the loss of unique items.
- **Injunction for Nuisance.** An injunction can be rightfully demanded to prevent irreparable injury, interminable litigation, and a multiplicity of suits.
- **Injunction Discretion.** The discretion of the court in granting or refusing an injunction is not arbitrary and can be corrected on appeal if improperly exercised.
- **Prior Use (Rejected).** It does not matter that the alleged nuisance-causing activity existed before the plaintiff acquired or improved their land.
- **Prescriptive Right to Nuisance (Rejected).** A prescriptive right to continue a nuisance can only be acquired by continuous use for twenty consecutive years.
- **Acquiescence (Rejected).** Acquiescence short of twenty years will not bar a complaint of nuisance unless the plaintiff's conduct induced the defendant to incur large expenditures, forming an estoppel.
- **Damnum Absque Injuria (Rejected).** While a reasonable and lawful use of land may cause some inconvenience or damage that is *damnum absque injuria*, one cannot place anything on their land that the law would pronounce a nuisance and thereby compel a neighbor to leave their land vacant or restrict its use.

**Reasoning**
- The defendant's brick burning was a nuisance because the sulphurous acid gas caused tangible and appreciable injury to the plaintiffs' property, killing trees and damaging other vegetation.
- The injury was substantial and irreparable because the destroyed ornamental trees and vines could not be replaced, and their value was difficult to estimate in monetary terms.
- An injunction was appropriate to prevent interminable litigation and a multiplicity of suits, as the injury was recurring with each brick burning.
- The fact that the nuisance was not continual, but only occasional (when the wind blew from the south), did not preclude an injunction, as the damage was significant.
- The defendant's prior use of the land as a brick-yard did not give him a right to continue the nuisance, as one cannot establish a nuisance on vacant land and thereby control a neighbor's future use.
- The defendant had not acquired a prescriptive right because the use of anthracite coal, which caused the damage, had not been continuous for twenty years.
- The plaintiffs did not acquiesce in the nuisance; their conduct did not induce the defendant to incur large expenses that would create an estoppel.
- The damage to the plaintiffs was large and substantial, while it was not clear that restraining the defendant would cause him equally large damage, as he could potentially make bricks elsewhere or use his land for other profitable purposes.
- The court rejected the argument that the brick-making business was useful and necessary, stating that similar decisions in England had not caused embarrassment to the industry, and suitable locations for brick-making could be found without harming neighbors.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a foundational nuisance case, establishing that a lawful business can be enjoined if it causes substantial harm to a neighbor's property. It connects to *Jost v. Dairyland Power Cooperative* by illustrating the court's willingness to grant injunctive relief for nuisance, even against economically productive activities, when the harm is significant. It also contrasts with cases like *Adams v. Cleveland-Cliffs Iron Co.* which might focus more on the "reasonableness" of the use in an industrial context, whereas here the court emphasizes the tangible injury to the plaintiff's enjoyment of their property. The discussion of "irreparable injury" and "multiplicity of suits" is key for understanding when equitable remedies like injunctions are granted over mere damages.
2. What if the defendant could prove that their land was uniquely suited for brick-making and that an injunction would effectively destroy their business, leading to a much greater economic loss for them than the plaintiffs' damages? Would the court still grant an injunction, or would it lean towards damages?

**Takeaway:** A lawful business can be a private nuisance if it causes substantial and irreparable harm to a neighbor's property, warranting an injunction even if the business predates the neighbor's improvements.
