## Strain v. Green (Washington Supreme Court, 1946)

**Facts**
- On August 7, 1943, William Strain agreed to buy Jacob Green's waterfront home for $35,000, with Greens to have 60 days to move out.
- On August 20, 1943, Green and wife executed a warranty deed to Strain and wife.
- On August 27, 1943, the deed was delivered, and the purchase price was paid in full.
- At some time after August 27, 1943, the Greens moved out, taking a hot water tank and electric heater, Venetian blinds, lighting fixtures, and three mirrors. They also replaced the original hot water tank with a smaller one and the original light fixtures with inferior plastic imitations.
- On November 8, 1943, Strain's attorney demanded the return of the removed property.
- No property was returned.
- In March 1944, Strain brought an action against Green.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court held the hot water tank and Venetian blinds were fixtures and ordered their return, awarding damages for reinstallation.
- The trial court held the light fixtures and mirrors were personal property that the defendants had a right to remove.
- Strain appealed the trial court's holding regarding the light fixtures and mirrors.

**Issue**
Can a seller remove items like light fixtures and mirrors from a home after selling it, claiming they were personal property, when the items were installed by the seller and were physically attached to the house?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Strain) wins. The light fixtures and two of the three mirrors were fixtures and should not have been removed by the sellers. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Fixture Test.** The true criterion of a fixture is the united application of three requisites: (1) actual annexation to the realty, or something appurtenant thereto; (2) application to the use or purpose to which that part of the realty with which it is connected is appropriated; and (3) the intention of the party making the annexation to make a permanent accession to the freehold.
- **Intent for Fixtures.** The intent to make an article a fixture is not gathered from testimony of the actual state of mind of the party making the annexation, but is inferred from the nature of the article affixed, the relation and situation to the freehold of the party making the annexation, the manner of the annexation, and the purpose for which it is made.
- **Presumption of Intent (Owner vs. Tenant).** When an annexation is made by the owner of the property, the presumption is that they intended to enrich the freehold. When made by a tenant, the presumption is that they did not intend to enrich the freehold.
- **Rejected: Secret Intent.** It is not the law that the secret intention of the owner who affixed the disputed article, of itself, determines whether or not it was a fixture or a mere personal chattel.

**Reasoning**
- The court rejects the idea that a seller's secret, uncommunicated intention determines whether an item is a fixture, as this would encourage dishonesty and make it impossible for purchasers to know what they are buying.
- The court applies the three-part fixture test.
    - **Actual Annexation:** The chandelier and sidelights were attached to the walls and ceiling. Two mirrors were attached to plywood that was firmly nailed to the plastered wall. The powder room mirror was not adequately proven to be physically annexed.
    - **Application to Use/Purpose:** The light fixtures were for the use and purpose of making the house livable. The mirrors were part of the house walls.
    - **Intention of Permanent Accession:**
        - The Greens were the owners when they installed the articles, creating a presumption that they intended to enrich the freehold.
        - This presumption is not overcome by their secret intention.
        - The fact that light fixtures are universally called "light fixtures" is some evidence they are fixtures.
        - Replacing the removed light fixtures with inferior ones implies an admission that a house needs light fixtures to be complete.
        - The plywood backing for the two mirrors, when removed, would damage the plaster or leave nail holes and discolored wall, indicating they were intended as part of the wall.
- The court distinguishes older cases (Philadelphia Mtg. & Trust Co. v. Miller, Hall v. Law Guarantee & Trust Society) that might suggest items like bathtubs or hot water heaters were not fixtures, noting that the law of fixtures has evolved with changing times and household appliances.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a classic example of the **fixture** doctrine, which determines when personal property becomes part of the real estate. It highlights the importance of objective intent over subjective, secret intent, especially in transactions between a seller and a buyer. It connects to the broader theme of defining the boundaries of property and what is included in a transfer of land.
2. What if the Greens had explicitly stated in the earnest money receipt that they intended to remove the chandelier and mirrors? Would that have changed the outcome, even if the items were physically annexed? [CHECK: This is a good question for class discussion.]

**Takeaway:** The objective intent of the annexor, inferred from the circumstances, rather than their secret subjective intent, determines whether an item becomes a fixture.

## Nebraska v. Iowa (Supreme Court of the United States, 1892)

**Facts**
- Iowa was admitted into the Union in 1846, with its western boundary defined as the middle of the main channel of the Missouri River.
- Nebraska was admitted in 1867, with its eastern boundary likewise defined as the middle of the channel of the Missouri River.
- Between 1861 and 1877, the Missouri River channel near Omaha underwent significant changes.
- In 1877, the river above Omaha, which had a course like an ox-bow, suddenly cut through the neck of the bow and formed a new channel.
- Nebraska filed an original suit against Iowa to determine the boundary line between the two states, claiming jurisdiction over a tract of land.
- Iowa answered, alleging the disputed ground was part of its territory, and filed a cross-bill for affirmative relief.

**Procedural History**
- Original suit filed by Nebraska in the Supreme Court of the United States.
- Iowa filed an answer and a cross-bill.
- Nebraska answered the cross-bill.
- Replications were filed and proofs taken.

**Issue**
Does a sudden change in a river's course (avulsion) alter the boundary line between states, or does the boundary remain in the old channel?

**Holding**
Nebraska wins. The boundary between the states remains in the center of the old channel after an avulsion. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Accretion.** When land grants border on running water, and the banks change by gradual accretion, the riparian owner's boundary line remains the stream, even if the actual area of possession varies.
- **Avulsion.** When a boundary stream suddenly abandons its old bed and seeks a new course, such a change of channel works no change of boundary, and the boundary remains as it was, in the center of the old channel.
- **State Boundaries.** These rules for private property boundaries on streams also apply to boundaries between states or nations found in running water.
- **Missouri River.** The law of accretion controls on the Missouri River, despite its rapid and great changes.
- **Rejected: Rapid Accretion Exception.** The rapid and great changes of the Missouri River do not create an exception to the general law of accretion.

**Reasoning**
- The court relies on established common law principles regarding **accretion** and **avulsion**.
- **Accretion** involves gradual, imperceptible changes where the boundary shifts with the stream.
    - This rule is based on the idea that a proprietor subject to loss by gradual change should also benefit from gradual gain.
    - The test for "gradual and imperceptible" is that witnesses may see progress over time, but not while the process is ongoing.
- **Avulsion** involves sudden, violent, and visible changes where the boundary remains in the old channel.
    - This prevents significant loss of territory to one party due to a sudden natural event.
- These principles are universally recognized for both private property and international/state boundaries.
- The court specifically addresses the argument that the Missouri River's peculiar character (rapid current, soft soil, rapid changes) should exempt it from the accretion rule.
    - It cites *Jefferis v. Land Company* which held that the general law of accretion applies to the Mississippi River, and the Missouri River, while more rapid, does not differ in principle.
    - While parts of the bank may fall suddenly into the river, the material disintegrates and is carried downstream as particles, not as a solid mass transferred to the opposite shore. The increase on the forming side is still gradual and imperceptible.
- The 1877 change in the Missouri River near Omaha, where it cut through an ox-bow, was a sudden event, thus an **avulsion**.
    - Therefore, the boundary did not change and remains the center line of the old, abandoned channel.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes the critical distinction between **accretion** and **avulsion** for determining property boundaries along rivers, particularly for state boundaries. It connects to the concept of **original acquisition** and how natural processes can alter property rights. It reinforces the idea that **settled expectations** and **administrability_clear_rules** are important in property law, even when dealing with dynamic natural phenomena.
2. What if the river's new channel completely dries up and the old channel becomes the only navigable waterway? Would the boundary revert to the new navigable channel, or remain in the old, dry channel?

**Takeaway:** Gradual changes in a river's course shift property boundaries, but sudden changes leave the boundary in the old channel.

## Scott v. Anderson-Tully Co. (Mississippi Court of Appeals, 2015)

**Facts**
- In 1925, Stewart Scott Jr. and Willie Scott inherited an undivided one-half interest in a 584.6-acre tract of land in Jefferson County, Mississippi.
- In 1944, a plat prepared for a timber deed from the Scotts noted a wire fence on the east side of the property and stated that "20 1/10 [acres] outside of fence is included in total acreage" but also that the property "outside of fence & may be disputed."
- In 1954, Richard Scott, an heir, rebuilt the wire fence, moving it slightly west along the tree line to avoid digging new post holes.
- In 1969, Anderson-Tully Company came into possession of an adjacent tract of land through a quitclaim deed.
- In 1969, Anderson-Tully marked its perceived boundary line along the wire fence with blue paint.
- From 1969 to 2010, Anderson-Tully maintained the disputed twenty acres, repainting the blue line in 1986 and 1998.
- Anderson-Tully cut and harvested timber from the disputed land in 1990, 1999, and 2010.
- Anderson-Tully issued five hunting licenses for the disputed property.
- In 1993, Herman Scott began paying property taxes on the disputed tract.
- In 2003, Scott objected to Anderson-Tully's possession.
- In 2007, Scott cancelled a hunting lease with the Linwood Hunting Club, which had also leased from Anderson-Tully.
- In December 2008, Anderson-Tully's attorney sent Scott a letter asserting ownership of the disputed tract.
- In early 2010, Anderson-Tully harvested timber from the disputed land.
- On March 19, 2010, Herman Scott, as administrator of Stewart Scott Jr.'s estate, filed suit.

**Procedural History**
- Scott filed suit in Jefferson County Chancery Court seeking damages for timber removal, an injunction, and to quiet and confirm title.
- Anderson-Tully counterclaimed to quiet and confirm its own title, asserting ownership by deed or, alternatively, by adverse possession.
- The chancellor dismissed Scott's claim, finding Anderson-Tully had acquired title through adverse possession.
- Scott filed a Rule 60(b) motion for relief from judgment or a new trial, which the chancellor denied.
- Scott appealed to the Mississippi Court of Appeals.

**Issue**
Did the chancellor err in finding that Anderson-Tully Company acquired title to the disputed twenty acres through adverse possession?

**Holding**
Defendant (Anderson-Tully) wins. The chancellor did not err in finding that Anderson-Tully proved adverse possession by clear and convincing evidence. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Adverse Possession (Mississippi).** To establish adverse possession, the claimant must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the possession was (1) under claim of ownership; (2) actual or hostile; (3) open, notorious, and visible; (4) continuous and uninterrupted for a period of ten years; (5) exclusive; and (6) peaceful.
- **Claim of Ownership.** The possessor must "fly [his] flag over the property" to put the actual owner on notice of an adverse claim.
- **Actual or Hostile Possession.** Possession is hostile when the possessor intends to claim title even under a mistaken belief that the land is within their deed, and without the owner's permission.
- **Actual or Hostile Possession (Wild Lands).** Possessory acts necessary for adverse possession may vary with the characteristics of the land; for "wild" or unimproved lands, the quantum of proof is lower, and possession may be established by continued public acts of ownership.
- **Open, Notorious, and Visible Possession.** The possessor must "unfurl his flag on the land, and keep it flying, so that the actual owner may see... that an enemy has invaded his domains."
- **Continuous and Uninterrupted Possession.** Possession must be maintained for at least ten years, and a mistaken belief about deed calls does not defeat this element if the land was occupied for the statutory period under a claim of ownership.
- **Exclusive Possession.** The possessor must evince an intention to possess and hold the land to the exclusion of all others, and their conduct must unequivocally indicate dominion of a sole owner, though it does not mean no one else can use the property.
- **Peaceful Possession.** The adverse possessor's use of the claimed property must be peaceful, and expected disputes associated with use or ownership are not indicative of non-peaceful possession.

**Reasoning**
- **Claim of Ownership:** Anderson-Tully marked its borders with recognizable blue paint along the fence, which multiple witnesses observed and understood to mark Anderson-Tully's property. They also cut timber, performed timber-stand improvements, and issued hunting licenses, all indicating an ownership claim. Scott's payment of taxes alone was not dispositive.
- **Actual or Hostile:** Anderson-Tully marked, managed, harvested timber, and issued hunting licenses without permission. The acts were sufficient for wild or unimproved land, where a lower quantum of proof is required.
- **Open, Notorious, and Visible:** The blue paint lines were visible and recognized in the community as Anderson-Tully's boundary. Timber cutting and hunting licenses were also public acts.
- **Continuous and Uninterrupted:** Anderson-Tully's possession began in 1969 and continued until Scott's suit in 2010, exceeding the ten-year statutory period. Scott's objections in 2003 and 2007 came after Anderson-Tully's claim had already ripened.
- **Exclusive:** No evidence showed the Scotts or anyone else used the property east of the fence after 1969. Anderson-Tully leased the land for hunting and its employees testified no one crossed the blue line.
- **Peaceful:** Anderson-Tully's possession was peaceful because Scott's first dispute occurred in 2003, well after the ten-year statutory period for adverse possession had ripened.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the application of the **adverse possession** doctrine, specifically how the elements are applied to "wild" or unimproved land, where the standard for "actual or hostile" possession is lower. It connects to cases like *Howard v. Kunto* by showing that continuous possession does not necessarily mean constant physical presence, especially for certain types of land. The court's emphasis on community recognition of the boundary (the blue paint and fence) highlights the importance of **open, notorious, and visible** acts in establishing notice to the true owner.
2. The chancellor noted that neither party's deed "accurately describe[s] the disputed tract to any certainty." This raises the question of how much ambiguity in original title documents impacts the burden of proof for adverse possession, or if it makes adverse possession more likely to succeed as a means of clarifying boundaries.

**Takeaway:** Adverse possession can be established on wild or unimproved land through public acts of ownership that provide notice to the true owner, even if those acts are not constant physical occupation.

## Carpenter v. Ruperto (Iowa Supreme Court, 1982)

**Facts**
- In July 1951, Virginia Carpenter and her husband moved into a home they purchased in southeast Des Moines.
- Carpenter's lot was 40 feet by 125 feet.
- A larger undeveloped lot, owned by the defendants and their predecessors, bounded Carpenter's property to the north.
- Carpenter knew her lot's dimensions and that it did not include the cornfield to the north.
- In 1952, Carpenter and her husband cleared several feet of the property to the north, graded it, and planted grass.
- Carpenter used this land as an extension of her yard, planting peony bushes in the 1950s, installing a propane tank 30 feet north of her lot in 1964, constructing a dirt bank in 1965, and putting in a driveway infringing five feet onto the land in 1975.
- The Rosenfelds owned the adjacent lot from July 1960 to February 1978, storing junk and debris on it, and paying all taxes.
- Carpenter and her husband once obtained the Rosenfelds’ permission to keep a horse on the lot.
- In the 1960s, Carpenter examined the plat of the defendants’ lot in the courthouse.
- In 1978, defendant McCormick purchased his interest in the lot, aware of a potential boundary dispute due to Carpenter's propane tank and driveway.
- Defendants were unsuccessful in settling the dispute with Carpenter.
- Carpenter brought an action to quiet title to the south 60 feet of the defendants' lot, claiming adverse possession.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court found Carpenter did not establish her possession was under a claim of right and denied her adverse possession claim.
- The trial court ordered defendants to deed Carpenter the strip of land her driveway was on and pay to move the propane tank.
- Carpenter appealed the denial of her adverse possession claim.
- Defendants cross-appealed the equitable relief granted to Carpenter.
- The Iowa Supreme Court affirmed on the merits of the appeal and dismissed the cross-appeal for want of jurisdiction.

**Issue**
Can a party acquire title by adverse possession under a claim of right if they knew they had no title and no basis for claiming an interest in the property when they entered possession?

**Holding**
Defendant (record owner) wins. A party cannot establish a good faith claim of right for adverse possession if they knew they had no title and no basis for claiming an interest in the property. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Adverse Possession Elements.** One claiming title by adverse possession must establish hostile, actual, open, exclusive, and continuous possession, under a claim of right or color of title, for at least ten years, by clear and positive proof.
- **Strict Construction of Adverse Possession.** The doctrine of adverse possession is strictly construed because the law presumes possession under regular title.
- **Claim of Right.** A claim of right must be made in good faith.
- **Rejected: Squatter's Rights.** To permit a squatter to assert a claim of right would put a premium on dishonesty.
- **Knowledge of Defective Title.** Knowledge of a defect in title is not alone sufficient to preclude proof of good faith.
- **Knowledge of No Title and No Basis for Claim.** When knowledge of lack of title is accompanied by knowledge of no basis for claiming an interest in the property, a good faith claim of right cannot be established.

**Reasoning**
- The court found Carpenter failed to prove a good faith claim of right.
- Carpenter knew her lot did not include the cornfield to the north.
- Carpenter knew someone else had title to the land and that she had no interest in it or claim to it.
- This was not a case of confusion or mistake regarding the boundary.
- At the time she entered possession, Carpenter knew she had no legal right to do so.
- Allowing a claim of right based merely on entering possession would recognize "squatter's rights," which the court rejects.
- The court distinguished cases where knowledge of a *defective* title does not defeat good faith from cases where there is knowledge of *no* title and *no basis* for a claim.
- The purpose of the claim of right requirement is to bar mere squatters from the benefits of adverse possession.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the "claim of right" element of adverse possession, particularly the "good faith" requirement. It contrasts with cases where a mistaken belief about a boundary might still satisfy the claim of right, emphasizing that knowing you have no right to the land, as Carpenter did, defeats the claim. This case highlights the tension between rewarding productive use of land and protecting the rights of record owners, similar to the policy considerations in *Howard v. Kunto* regarding mistaken boundaries, but with a different outcome due to the possessor's knowledge.
2. What if Carpenter had *not* looked at the plat in the courthouse and genuinely believed the land was abandoned or part of her property due to the lack of clear boundaries? Would that change the outcome regarding "good faith"?

**Takeaway:** A possessor cannot establish a good faith claim of right for adverse possession if they knew they had no legal right to the property when they entered possession.

## Howard v. Kunto (Washington Court of Appeals, 1970)

**Facts**
- As early as 1932, McCall occupied a house on a 50-foot parcel of land on Hood Canal, but his deed described an adjacent 50-foot parcel to the west.
- Over the years since 1946, several conveyances occurred using the same incorrect legal description, with each new owner taking possession of the house and land McCall occupied.
- The Millers, predecessors to the Kuntos, had a survey done that mistakenly confirmed their deed description matched their physical occupation, and they built a dock and other improvements.
- In 1959, the Kuntos took possession of the disputed property from the Millers under a deed with the same incorrect description.
- In 1960, the Howards, who owned land east of the Kuntos, had a survey performed for their own property, which revealed that the deed descriptions and land occupancy of several parties, including the Kuntos, did not coincide with the government survey.
- The survey showed the Howards were record owners of land occupied by the Moyers, and the Moyers held record title to the land occupied by the Kuntos.
- Howard obtained a conveyance from Moyer for the land the Kuntos occupied in April 1960.
- Until this conveyance, neither Moyer nor his predecessors asserted any right to the property occupied by the Kunto chain of possession.
- The Howards then initiated this action to quiet title.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court quieted title in the Howards and Yearlys, denying the Kuntos' claim of adverse possession.
- The Kuntos appealed.

**Issue**
- Can periods of possession by previous owners be "tacked" together to meet the statutory period for adverse possession when the deed mistakenly describes an adjacent parcel, and can summer-only occupancy satisfy the continuity requirement?

**Holding**
- The Kuntos (appellants) win. Successive periods of possession can be tacked when there is a reasonable connection between occupants, even if the deed description is erroneous, and summer occupancy can be continuous for adverse possession. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Adverse Possession Elements.** To constitute adverse possession, there must be actual possession which is uninterrupted, open and notorious, hostile and exclusive, and under a claim of right made in good faith for the statutory period.
- **Continuity of Possession.** The requisite possession requires such possession and dominion "as ordinarily marks the conduct of owners in general, in holding, managing, and caring for property of like nature and condition."
- **Tacking.** Tacking of adverse possession is permitted if the successive occupants are in "privity."
- **Privity (for Tacking).** The deed running between the parties purporting to transfer the land possessed traditionally furnishes the privity of estate which connects the possession of the successive occupants.
- **Rejected: Privity (Strict Deed Match).** Where the deed does not describe any of the land which was occupied, the actual transfer of possession is insufficient to establish privity.

**Reasoning**
- **Continuity of Possession:**
    - Summer occupancy of a summer beach home does not destroy the continuity of possession.
    - The nature and condition of the property dictate what constitutes "uninterrupted" possession.
    - Occupancy during the period of the year the land is capable of use is sufficient continuity.
- **Tacking and Privity:**
    - The court rejects the trial court's strict interpretation of privity, which would prevent tacking when the deed description does not match the occupied land.
    - The historical reason for privity was to prevent a succession of trespasses from defeating record title, and to ensure the adverse possessor had a "claim of right."
    - There is a substantial difference between a squatter and a property purchaser who occupies the wrong land due to an inaccurate survey.
    - Public policy favors early certainty as to land ownership, especially where exact boundary determinations are difficult and expensive.
    - It is not reasonable or customary for every purchaser to hire a surveyor to verify deed boundaries for a beach home.
    - The technical requirement of "privity" should not upset long periods of occupancy by those who received erroneous deed descriptions in good faith.
    - "Privity" is merely a judicial recognition of the need for a reasonable connection between successive occupants to elevate their claim above that of a wrongdoer.
    - Such a reasonable connection exists when successive purchasers receive record title to tract A under the mistaken belief they are acquiring tract B, and possession of tract B is transferred and occupied continuously for the statutory period.
    - This is analogous to cases where a purchaser claims more land than described in their deed, where tacking is permitted.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the flexibility of adverse possession requirements, particularly regarding continuity and tacking. It contrasts with cases that might require more stringent proof of continuous possession or a direct link in the chain of title for tacking. It shows how courts can interpret the elements of adverse possession to achieve what they perceive as equitable outcomes and to reflect common practices (like summer home use).
2. What if the Kuntos had known their deed was incorrect but still occupied the land? Would the court's reasoning about "good faith" and "mistaken belief" still apply, or would they be considered mere trespassers?

**Takeaway:** Adverse possession requirements for continuity and privity can be interpreted flexibly to reflect the nature of the property and the good faith of the possessors, allowing tacking even when deeds contain errors.

## Songbyrd Inc. v. Estate of Grossman (United States Court of Appeals, Second Circuit, 2000)

**Facts**
- In 1971, Henry Roeland Byrd ("Professor Longhair") was discovered by Arthur "Quint" Davis.
- Early 1970s, Davis and Parker Dinkins arranged for Byrd to make master tapes in Baton Rouge.
- Albert Grossman, president of Bearsville Records, Inc. in Woodstock, New York, heard demo tapes from these master recordings.
- Grossman arranged for Byrd and another musician to travel to Woodstock for a recording session, which was unsatisfactory.
- Davis sent the master tapes to Grossman "as demonstration tapes only, without any intent for either Albert Grossman or Bearsville Records Inc. to possess these aforementioned tapes as owner."
- The tapes remained in Grossman's possession.
- In 1975, Dinkins, acting for Davis and Byrd, sent two letters to Bearsville Records, Inc. requesting return of the master recordings, but received no response.
- Grossman died in 1985. Bearsville Records, Inc. was dissolved, but Grossman's estate ("the Estate") continued doing business as Bearsville Records and possessed the tapes.
- In 1986, the Estate licensed some of the Byrd master recordings to Rounder Records Corporation.
- In 1987, Rounder released an album of Byrd's recordings, which won a Grammy Award.
- In 1991, the Estate licensed some master recordings to Rhino Records.
- In 1993, SongByrd, Inc. was incorporated as a successor-in-interest to Byrd's intellectual property rights.

**Procedural History**
- SongByrd filed a "Petition in Revendication" against the Estate in Louisiana state court in 1995, seeking declaration of ownership, return of tapes, and damages.
- The Estate removed the case to federal court in Louisiana.
- The Louisiana District Court dismissed the suit as time-barred, explicitly declining to consider personal jurisdiction.
- The Fifth Circuit reversed the time-bar ruling and remanded for consideration of personal jurisdiction.
- On remand, the Louisiana District Court found it lacked personal jurisdiction and transferred the case *sua sponte* to the Northern District of New York.
- The Northern District of New York Magistrate Judge ruled that New York law applied, the three-year statute of limitations for conversion applied, the cause of action accrued no later than 1986, and the suit was time-barred.
- SongByrd appealed to the Second Circuit.

**Issue**
Is SongByrd's claim for wrongful possession of master recording tapes time-barred under New York law?

**Holding**
Defendant (Estate of Grossman) wins. SongByrd's claim is time-barred under New York law because the cause of action for conversion accrued when the Estate licensed the tapes in 1986, more than three years before the suit was filed. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Personal Jurisdiction.** A court must have personal jurisdiction over a defendant to apply its forum state's substantive law in a transferred case.
- **New York Statute of Limitations for Conversion.** The limitations period for conversion and recovery of chattels is three years.
- **Accrual of Conversion Claim (New York).** A cause of action for conversion accrues when the possessor openly deals with the property as its own, such as by commercially exploiting it.
- **Demand and Refusal Rule (New York).** A cause of action for replevin against a good-faith purchaser of a stolen chattel accrues when the true owner demands return and the possessor refuses; this rule can also apply to a bailee who has not asserted ownership.
- **Rejected:** New York law requires a demand and refusal for the accrual of a conversion claim against a possessor who openly deals with the property as its own.

**Reasoning**
- The Louisiana District Court properly transferred the case to New York because it lacked personal jurisdiction over the Estate.
    - Grossman's contacts with Louisiana were "extremely scant," limited to hearing demo tapes made in Louisiana and inviting Louisiana residents to New York.
    - Grossman never acted in Louisiana to obtain the tapes.
    - The invitation to Byrd and his managers to come to New York was too tenuous a "but for" cause to establish specific personal jurisdiction for a claim of wrongful possession of tapes later sent to Grossman.
    - General jurisdiction was not satisfied as there were no continuous and systematic business contacts.
- New York's three-year statute of limitations for conversion applies because the case was properly transferred to New York due to lack of personal jurisdiction in Louisiana.
- SongByrd's claim is time-barred because the cause of action accrued no later than 1986.
    - The Estate's licensing of the master tapes to Rounder in 1986 constituted an open commercial exploitation of the property, which under New York law (Sporn v. MCA Records, Inc.) triggers the accrual of a conversion claim.
    - The demand-and-refusal rule (Guggenheim Foundation v. Lubell) does not apply here because the Estate openly treated the tapes as its own, changing the character of its possession.
    - Even if a demand were required, SongByrd's delay in making a demand after the 1987 Grammy Award, which made the licensing widely known, would be unreasonable.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the **accrual rule** for conversion claims in New York, distinguishing it from the **demand and refusal rule** for replevin against good-faith purchasers of stolen property (as seen in *Guggenheim Foundation v. Lubell*). It highlights how a possessor's actions can transform a non-wrongful possession into a conversion, triggering the statute of limitations.
2. [CHECK: The opinion mentions that the Fifth Circuit had previously ruled that SongByrd's claim was not time-barred in Louisiana, applying Louisiana's "acquisitive prescription" (adverse possession) rather than a "liberative" (standard) limitations period. This creates a tension between the two states' approaches to property claims and statutes of limitations, and how the choice of law can be outcome-determinative.]

**Takeaway:** A cause of action for conversion accrues in New York when a possessor openly treats property as its own, even without a demand and refusal from the true owner.

## Moore v. Regents of the University of California (Supreme Court of California, 1990)

**Facts**
- In October 1976, John Moore (Moore) sought treatment for hairy-cell leukemia at UCLA Medical Center.
- Dr. David Golde (Golde) confirmed the diagnosis and, along with other defendants, was aware that Moore's blood products were valuable for commercial and scientific efforts.
- On October 8, 1976, Golde recommended a splenectomy, telling Moore it was necessary to slow his disease. Moore signed a consent form for the splenectomy.
- Before the operation, Golde and researcher Shirley Quan (Quan) planned to obtain portions of Moore's spleen for research, unrelated to Moore's medical care, without informing Moore or seeking his permission.
- On October 20, 1976, surgeons at UCLA Medical Center removed Moore's spleen.
- Between November 1976 and September 1983, Moore returned to UCLA Medical Center multiple times at Golde's direction, believing these visits were necessary for his health. During these visits, Golde withdrew additional samples of blood, skin, bone marrow, and sperm.
- Defendants concealed their research on Moore's cells and their plans for financial and competitive benefit from exploiting them.
- Moore inquired about the commercial value of his bodily substances, but defendants repeatedly represented there was no commercial value and actively discouraged such inquiries.
- Sometime before August 1979, Golde established a cell line from Moore's T-lymphocytes.
- On January 30, 1981, the Regents of the University of California (Regents) applied for a patent on the cell line, listing Golde and Quan as inventors, with a policy for them to share in royalties.
- Golde negotiated commercial development agreements for the cell line with Genetics Institute, Inc. (Genetics Institute) and Sandoz Pharmaceuticals Corporation (Sandoz), becoming a paid consultant and acquiring stock rights.
- The patent for the cell line issued on March 20, 1984, naming Golde and Quan as inventors and the Regents as assignee.

**Procedural History**
- Moore filed a complaint alleging 13 causes of action, including conversion, breach of fiduciary duty, and lack of informed consent.
- The trial court sustained demurrers to the entire complaint, finding Moore had not stated a cause of action for conversion and that other allegations were conclusory.
- The Court of Appeal reversed, holding that the complaint did state a cause of action for conversion, and directed leave to amend for other defendants.
- Defendants appealed to the California Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Can a patient sue for conversion of their excised bodily tissues used in medical research without their consent, or for breach of fiduciary duty and lack of informed consent due to a physician's undisclosed research and economic interests?

**Holding**
Moore (plaintiff) wins in part. A patient can sue for breach of fiduciary duty and lack of informed consent when a physician fails to disclose personal research or economic interests that may affect medical judgment, but cannot sue for conversion of excised bodily tissues. Affirmed in part and Reversed in part.

**Rules**
- **Informed Consent.** A physician has a fiduciary duty to disclose all information material to a patient's decision regarding medical treatment, including personal interests unrelated to the patient's health, whether research or economic, that may affect the physician's professional judgment.
- **Conversion.** To establish conversion, a plaintiff must show actual interference with their ownership or right of possession of personal property.
- **Rejected: Property Interest in Excised Cells.** A patient does not retain a sufficient ownership interest in excised cells to support a cause of action for conversion.
- **Rejected: Patentability of Naturally Occurring Organisms.** Federal law permits the patenting of organisms that are the product of human ingenuity, but not naturally occurring organisms.

**Reasoning**
- **Breach of Fiduciary Duty and Lack of Informed Consent:**
    - Physicians have a fiduciary duty to disclose personal interests (research or economic) that might affect their professional judgment, as these are material to a patient's informed consent.
    - Moore's allegations that Golde had an undisclosed research interest in his cells before the splenectomy and concealed economic interests during postoperative procedures state a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty or lack of informed consent.
    - This cause of action protects a patient's right to make autonomous medical decisions.
    - The other defendants (Regents, Quan, Genetics Institute, Sandoz) are not physicians and thus did not have a direct fiduciary duty to Moore, but may be liable under secondary liability theories.
- **Conversion:**
    - Moore's claim for conversion fails because he does not retain an ownership interest in his excised cells.
    - No reported judicial decision supports a claim of conversion for the use of human cells in medical research.
    - California statutory law, specifically Health and Safety Code section 7054.4, drastically limits a patient's control over excised cells by requiring their disposal after scientific use, eliminating many rights ordinarily attached to property.
    - The patented cell line and products derived from it are legally and factually distinct from Moore's original cells, as patent law rewards inventive effort, not the discovery of naturally occurring raw materials.
    - Extending conversion liability to this context would raise significant policy concerns:
        - It would threaten socially useful medical research by creating uncertainty about legal title to biological materials, hindering access to cell lines and discouraging investment.
        - Conversion is a strict liability tort, which would impose liability on innocent researchers who had no reason to know of inadequate disclosures.
        - The Legislature is better suited to address complex policy decisions regarding the commercial use of human biological materials.
    - The existing causes of action for breach of fiduciary duty and lack of informed consent adequately protect patients' rights without hindering research.

**Concurrence (Arabian, J.)**
- Agrees with the majority's holding.
- Expresses concern about the moral implications of treating human tissue as a commercial commodity.
- Believes the question of whether cells should be treated as property is not for the courts to decide, but for the Legislature, due to the complex moral, philosophical, and religious values involved.
- Notes that Moore has a remedy through the breach of fiduciary duty claim.

**Concurrence and Dissent (Broussard, J.)**
- Concurs with the majority that Moore states a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty.
- Dissents from the majority's rejection of the conversion claim.
- Argues that the majority fails to focus on the specific allegations that defendants interfered with Moore's rights *before* his body part was removed.
- Believes a patient has a right to determine the use of a body part before its removal, supported by the Uniform Anatomical Gift Act.
- Contends that unauthorized use of property or interference with the right to control its use can constitute conversion.
- Disagrees with the majority's policy arguments, stating that conversion liability would not significantly hinder research, especially against parties who directly interfere with a patient's rights.
- Argues that the patient's economic interest in their body parts should be protected to prevent unjust enrichment.

**Dissent (Mosk, J.)**
- Disagrees with the majority's conclusion that Moore cannot state a cause of action for conversion.
- Argues that the common law is flexible and should adapt to new scientific and technological advancements.
- Contends that California statutory law (Health and Safety Code section 7054.4) does not eliminate Moore's property interest in his cells, as "scientific use" does not encompass commercial exploitation.
- Believes that the concept of property is broad and includes the right to exploit the commercial potential of one's own tissue.
- Asserts that the patented cell line is not factually or legally distinct enough from Moore's original cells to negate his ownership interest, especially since the patent was obtained years after the initial unauthorized use.
- Rejects the majority's policy concerns about hindering research, arguing they are overstated and outweighed by ethical and equitable considerations.
- Emphasizes the ethical imperative to respect the human body and prevent unjust enrichment, arguing that patients should share in the profits derived from their tissues.
- Believes the nondisclosure cause of action is inadequate because it is difficult to prove, only allows a patient to refuse consent (not share in profits), and fails to reach all parties involved in commercial exploitation.
- Criticizes the majority's reliance on scientific material not in the record and their competence to interpret it.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is crucial for understanding the limits of property rights in one's own body and the tension between individual autonomy and the public interest in scientific research. It connects to **Moore v. Regents of the University of California** itself, as it is the case. It also relates to **Moore v. Regents of the University of California** because it explores the concept of what constitutes "property" in novel contexts, similar to discussions around intellectual property or natural resources. The court's balancing of individual rights against societal benefits echoes themes in cases like **Ghen v. Rich** (custom vs. individual claim) and **Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois** (public trust doctrine).
2. The dissent raises an interesting point about the "paper tiger" nature of the informed consent claim, suggesting that it might be difficult for a plaintiff to prove that a reasonably prudent person would have refused life-saving treatment even with full disclosure of commercial interests. This highlights a potential gap in protection for patients if conversion is disallowed.

**Takeaway:** While individuals have a right to informed consent regarding medical procedures, they generally do not retain property rights in excised bodily tissues once removed, especially when those tissues are used to create new, patentable products through scientific ingenuity.

## Hecht v. Superior Court (Court of Appeal of the State of California, 1993)

**Facts**
- In October 1991, William E. Kane (decedent) deposited 15 vials of his sperm at California Cryobank, Inc.
- On September 24, 1991, decedent signed a "Specimen Storage Agreement" with the sperm bank, instructing the sperm bank to continue to store or release the specimens to the executor of his estate upon his death.
- The agreement also included an "Authorization to Release Specimens" stating, "I, William Everett Kane, . . . authorize the [sperm bank] to release my semen specimens (vials) to Deborah Ellen Hecht."
- On September 27, 1991, decedent executed a will, naming Deborah E. Hecht (petitioner) as executor and bequeathing to her "all right, title, and interest that I may have in any specimens of my sperm stored with any sperm bank or similar facility for storage."
- The will also included a "Statement of Wishes" expressing his intention for Hecht to use his sperm to have a child after his death.
- On October 21, 1991, decedent wrote a letter to his children, William E. Kane, Jr., and Katharine E. Kane (real parties in interest), stating his intention for Hecht to have a child by him after his death using his frozen sperm samples.
- On October 30, 1991, decedent committed suicide.
- On November 18, 1991, Robert L. Greene was appointed special administrator of decedent's estate.
- On December 3, 1991, decedent's children filed will contests.
- On December 3, 1991, a "tentative agreement" (First Settlement Agreement) was placed on the record to resolve estate matters, but it did not explicitly mention the sperm.
- In October 1992, the administrator filed a petition for approval of a "Second Settlement Agreement" which would assign the estate's interest in the sperm to Hecht, subject to court approval.
- On November 5, 1992, the administrator filed a "Petition for Alternative Relief" seeking instructions to destroy the sperm or for its distribution.
- Decedent's children requested the sperm be destroyed on public policy grounds.
- Hecht argued the sperm was a gift to her and should be distributed to her.
- On December 9, 1992, the trial court ordered the sperm destroyed.
- On January 4, 1993, the trial court entered an order directing the administrator to destroy the sperm.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court ordered the destruction of the decedent's sperm.
- Hecht filed a petition for peremptory writ of mandate/prohibition to vacate the trial court's order.
- The Court of Appeal issued an order to show cause and stayed the execution of the trial court's order.

**Issue**
Did the trial court abuse its discretion by ordering the destruction of a deceased man's sperm, which he had bequeathed to his girlfriend for posthumous artificial insemination, based on public policy arguments against artificial insemination of unmarried women or posthumous conception?

**Holding**
Petitioner (Hecht) wins. The trial court abused its discretion in ordering the destruction of the decedent's sperm. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Probate Court Jurisdiction.** The power of the probate court extends only to the property of the decedent.
- **Right of Inheritance.** The right of inheritance and testamentary disposition is entirely within the control of the state Legislature.
- **Probate Court Distribution.** Except as expressly provided by law, the probate court has no jurisdiction to distribute a decedent’s estate except to heirs, devisees, or legatees.
- **Fiduciary Duty of Executor.** An executor or administrator occupies a fiduciary relationship to all parties having an interest in the estate and has a duty to protect their legal rights.
- **Probate Code Section 62.** "Property" includes "anything that may be the subject of ownership and includes both real and personal property and any interest therein."
- **Nature of Rights in Human Biological Materials.** Human biological materials are objects *sui generis*, regulated by specialized statutes to achieve policy goals rather than abandoned to the general law of personal property.
- **Decision-making Authority over Gametes.** Gamete providers have primary decision-making authority regarding preembryos in the absence of specific legislation.
- **California Civil Code Section 7005(b).** This section, part of the Uniform Parentage Act, applies to all women, married or not, allowing them to obtain semen for artificial insemination without fear of the donor claiming paternity, provided the semen is provided to a licensed physician.
- **Public Policy on Single-Parent Families.** The state does not have a policy of preventing the formation of single-parent families.
- **Public Policy on Posthumous Conception.** There is no state interest sufficient to justify infringing on gamete-providers’ decisional authority regarding posthumous conception.
- **Uniform Status of Children of Assisted Conception Act (Section 4(b)).** An individual who dies before implantation of an embryo, or before a child is conceived other than through sexual intercourse, using the individual’s egg or sperm, is not a parent of the resulting child, primarily to avoid intestate succession problems.
- **Probate Code Section 6407.** Relatives of the decedent conceived before the decedent’s death, but born thereafter, inherit as if they had been born in the lifetime of the decedent.
- **Probate Code Section 6408.** For purposes of intestate succession, this section provides rules for determining parent-child relationships and inheritance rights.
- **Judicial Role in Reproductive Technology.** It is not the role of the judiciary to inhibit the use of reproductive technology when the Legislature has not seen fit to do so, as such efforts raise serious questions regarding fundamental rights of procreation and privacy.

**Reasoning**
- The decedent's interest in his frozen sperm vials, while not strictly personal property, occupies an "interim category" entitling them to special respect due to their potential for human life.
- The decedent had an interest in his sperm, in the nature of ownership, to the extent that he had decision-making authority over its use for reproduction, which constitutes "property" under Probate Code section 62.
- The trial court's order to destroy the sperm could not be upheld by applying the will, as the will evidenced the decedent's intent for Hecht to receive the sperm for posthumous artificial insemination.
- The trial court's order could not be supported by the settlement agreements, as neither agreement mandated destruction of the sperm, and the enforceability of the Second Settlement Agreement was still at issue.
- Public policy does not prohibit the artificial insemination of an unmarried woman in California, as indicated by the Legislature's exclusion of the word "married" from Civil Code section 7005(b).
- There is no established state policy against posthumous conception or against the formation of single-parent families.
- The court found no authority for the trial court to make a value judgment on whether it is better for a potential child not to be born, assuming both gamete providers wish to conceive.
- The court rejected arguments that posthumous conception would create psychological or financial burdens on the decedent's existing children or society, noting that existing statutes (Probate Code sections 6407 and 6408) and potential settlement agreements address inheritance and support issues.
- The court emphasized that the judiciary should not inhibit reproductive technology when the Legislature has not done so, given the fundamental nature of procreation and privacy rights.
- The court did not adjudicate the validity of the will, sperm bank contract, or settlement agreements, or issues of decedent's competence or undue influence, as these were not before it.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is significant for establishing that cryogenically preserved sperm can be considered "property" within the meaning of probate law, even if it is *sui generis* and not subject to all general personal property rules. It connects to **Moore v. Regents of the University of California** by distinguishing the property interest in excised cells (where Moore had no ownership claim) from the decision-making authority over gametes intended for reproduction. The court here emphasizes the "potential for human life" as a key differentiator. It also touches on the tension between individual procreative rights and societal concerns, similar to discussions in cases like **Hecht v. Superior Court** itself regarding the disposition of genetic material.
2. The court explicitly states it is not adjudicating the validity of the will or the enforceability of the settlement agreements. This leaves open the question of whether Hecht will ultimately receive the sperm, even if the destruction order is vacated. What if the will is found invalid, or the settlement agreement that gives her the sperm is not enforced?

**Takeaway:** Human gametes intended for reproduction, even after the donor's death, constitute a unique form of "property" subject to the donor's decision-making authority, and public policy does not prohibit posthumous artificial insemination.

## Flynn v. Holder (United States Court of Appeals, Ninth Circuit, 2012)

**Facts**
- The National Organ Transplant Act (NOTA) was enacted in 1984, prohibiting compensation for human organs, including "bone marrow."
- Around 20 years prior to this case, a new method for obtaining hematopoietic stem cells, called "peripheral blood stem cell apheresis," was developed. This method involves stimulating stem cell production, drawing blood from a vein, filtering out stem cells, and returning the rest of the blood to the donor. This method does not involve extracting the soft, fatty substance from bone cavities.
- Plaintiffs include parents of sick children needing bone marrow transplants, a physician, and a nonprofit organization, MoreMarrowDonors.org.
- MoreMarrowDonors.org proposed a pilot program to offer $3,000 awards (scholarships, housing allowances, or charitable gifts) to minority and mixed-race donors of bone marrow cells, specifically using the apheresis method.
- The plaintiffs alleged that they could not launch this program because NOTA criminalizes payment for organs and classifies bone marrow as an organ.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs filed a complaint challenging the constitutionality of NOTA as applied to bone marrow transplants.
- The district court dismissed the complaint for failure to state a claim.
- Plaintiffs appealed.

**Issue**
Does the National Organ Transplant Act's prohibition on compensating "bone marrow" donors apply to donations of hematopoietic stem cells extracted from peripheral blood via apheresis?

**Holding**
Plaintiffs (appellants) win. The National Organ Transplant Act does not prohibit compensation for donations of hematopoietic stem cells extracted from peripheral blood via apheresis because these cells are considered part of blood, not "bone marrow" or a "subpart thereof" as defined by the statute. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Statutory Interpretation.** Courts interpret statutory terms according to their ordinary usage unless Congress clearly intended a different meaning.
- **National Organ Transplant Act (NOTA).** NOTA makes it a felony to knowingly acquire, receive, or transfer any human organ for valuable consideration for use in human transplantation.
- **Definition of "Human Organ" under NOTA.** The term "human organ" includes "kidney, liver, heart, lung, pancreas, bone marrow, cornea, eye, bone, and skin or any subpart thereof." It does not include "blood."
- **Rational Basis Review.** A legislative classification must have a rational basis to satisfy the Equal Protection Clause; it does not need to be perfect or persuasive to all.

**Reasoning**
- The court first addressed the challenge to the aspiration method of bone marrow donation.
    - The statute explicitly includes "bone marrow" as a human organ. The soft, fatty substance extracted by aspiration is bone marrow.
    - Congress had rational policy and philosophical reasons for prohibiting compensation for organs, including concerns about exploitation of the poor, commodification of human body parts, and degradation of the organ supply.
    - These reasons provide a rational basis for the prohibition on compensating donors for bone marrow extracted by aspiration, satisfying the Equal Protection Clause.
- The court then focused on the apheresis method.
    - The apheresis method did not exist when NOTA was passed, so Congress could not have specifically intended to address it.
    - NOTA does not include "blood" in its definition of "human organ," and the government concedes that compensating blood donors is not prohibited.
    - The government's argument that hematopoietic stem cells from peripheral blood are a "subpart" of bone marrow is rejected.
        - This argument would logically extend to all blood cells (red, white), which also originate in bone marrow, thus prohibiting compensation for all blood donations, which the government concedes is not the case.
        - In ordinary usage, the liquid flowing through veins is called "blood," not "bone marrow," even though its components originate in the marrow.
        - Once stem cells are in the bloodstream, they are a subpart of the blood, not the bone marrow. The term "subpart" refers to the organ from which material is taken, not where it was created.
    - The government's argument that a later statute (Stem Cell Therapeutic and Research Act of 2005) defined "bone marrow" to include cells in peripheral blood is also rejected.
        - That definition was explicitly limited to a specific "part" of the statute, not the "part" containing the organ purchase prohibition.
        - The purposes of the two statutory parts are different.
- Therefore, compensating donors for hematopoietic stem cells obtained via apheresis is not prohibited by NOTA because these cells are considered part of blood, not "bone marrow."

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case highlights the importance of statutory interpretation, particularly when new technologies emerge that were not contemplated by the original drafters. It connects to the concept of **commodification** of the human body, as seen in *Moore v. Regents of the University of California*, but here the court avoids the constitutional question by narrowly interpreting the statute. The court distinguishes between the physical "bone marrow" and cells derived from it that circulate in the blood, effectively creating a distinction based on the method of extraction and the nature of the substance being donated.
2. What if medical science develops a way to extract a "subpart" of the liver without any incision, perhaps through a blood filtration process similar to apheresis? Would that still be considered a "subpart" of the liver under NOTA, or would it be treated like blood?

**Takeaway:** The National Organ Transplant Act's prohibition on compensating "bone marrow" donors does not apply to hematopoietic stem cells extracted from peripheral blood via apheresis because these cells are considered part of blood, not "bone marrow" in its ordinary meaning.

## Ghen v. Rich (United States District Court for the District of Massachusetts, 1881)

**Facts**
- In the early spring months, fin-back whales frequent the easterly part of Massachusetts Bay.
- Provincetown fishermen pursue these whales in open boats and shoot them with bomb-lances.
- When killed, the whales sink and rise to the surface one to three days later.
- The usage on Cape Cod for many years has been that the person who kills a whale in this manner owns it.
- On April 9, 1880, the libellant (Ghen) shot and instantly killed a fin-back whale with a bomb-lance near Cape Cod.
- The whale sank immediately.
- On April 12, the whale was found stranded on a beach in Brewster, 17 miles from where it was killed, by one Ellis.
- Ellis advertised the whale for sale at auction and sold it to the respondent (Rich).
- Rich shipped off the blubber and tried out the oil.
- On April 13, Ghen heard the whale had been found and sent his men to claim it.
- Neither Rich nor Ellis knew Ghen had killed the whale, but they knew it had been killed by a bomb-lance by someone in the whaling business.

**Procedural History**
- Libellant (Ghen) brought a libel to recover the value of the whale.
- The District Court ruled in favor of the libellant.

**Issue**
Can a local custom among whalers establish ownership of a whale killed by a bomb-lance, even if another party finds the whale after it floats to the surface?

**Holding**
Libellant (Ghen) wins. The usage among whalers is valid, and the property in the whale was in the libellant. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **First Possession (General Rule).** An animal *ferae naturae* does not become property until a firm possession has been established by the taker.
- **Customary Law.** A long-standing, reasonable, and widely accepted custom within a particular industry can establish property rights, especially when the general common law rule is difficult to apply.
- Rejected: **First Possession (Finder's Rule).** A whale found adrift in the ocean is the property of the finder. *(The court implicitly rejects this as a primary rule in this specific context.)*

**Reasoning**
- The court found the usage among Cape Cod whalers to be reasonable and valid.
- The application of this usage is limited and affects few people.
- The usage has been recognized and acquiesced in for many years.
- It requires the first taker to perform the only act of appropriation possible given the nature of the whale.
- Without this usage, the industry would cease because no one would engage in it if their labor's fruits could be taken by a chance finder.
- The usage provides reasonable salvage for securing or reporting the property.
- The success of the industry under this rule demonstrates its practical effectiveness and general acceptance.
- The court noted that it is not clear the common law, without regard to usage, would not reach the same result, citing cases where actual possession and appropriation by the first taker were deemed sufficient.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a classic example of how **custom** can define property rights, especially when traditional rules of **first possession** (like actual physical control) are difficult to apply due to the nature of the resource. It connects to *Ghen v. Rich* by showing how the specific characteristics of a resource (a whale that sinks) necessitate a different approach to establishing ownership than, for example, a fox caught in a trap. It contrasts with cases like *Pierson v. Post* where the court rejected custom in favor of a more formal rule of capture.
2. What if the custom was that the finder owned the whale, and the killer received a small fee? Would the court have found that custom equally valid?

**Takeaway:** Well-established and reasonable customs within a specific industry can define property rights where traditional rules of first possession are impractical.

## United States v. Corrow (United States Court of Appeals, Tenth Circuit, 1997)

**Facts**
- Ray Winnie, a Navajo religious singer, used Yei B’Chei (ceremonial masks) for over 25 years.
- When Mr. Winnie died in 1991, he made no provision for the Yei B’Chei, and no family or clan member requested them.
- Richard Corrow, an owner of Artifacts Display Stands, visited Mrs. Fannie Winnie, Mr. Winnie's widow, multiple times.
- During his third visit in August 1993, Mr. Corrow saw the Yei B’Chei and offered to buy them, stating he planned to deliver them to a young Navajo chanter.
- Mr. Corrow bought 22 Yei B’Chei, five headdresses, and other artifacts from Mrs. Winnie for $10,000. Mrs. Winnie, who spoke no English, placed her thumbprint on a receipt after her granddaughter read it to her in Navajo.
- In November 1994, Mr. Corrow was contacted by the East-West Trading Company about a potential buyer for the Yei B’Chei.
- The purported buyer was an undercover National Park Service ranger, James Tanner.
- Agent Tanner agreed to purchase the Yei B’Chei for $70,000, with $50,000 going to Mr. Corrow.
- On December 9, 1994, Mr. Corrow arrived in Santa Fe with the Yei B’Chei and other artifacts, some adorned with eagle and owl feathers.
- FBI agents executed a search warrant and found the items.

**Procedural History**
- The government charged Mr. Corrow with trafficking in Native American cultural items (Count 1) and selling protected bird feathers (Count 2).
- Mr. Corrow filed a pretrial motion to dismiss Count 1, arguing the definition of "cultural patrimony" in NAGPRA was unconstitutionally vague. The district court denied the motion.
- A jury convicted Mr. Corrow on Count 1 and on the lesser included offense of possession of protected feathers for Count 2.
- Mr. Corrow renewed his challenge to the constitutionality of NAGPRA and the sufficiency of the evidence. The district court denied the motion.
- Mr. Corrow appealed to the Tenth Circuit.

**Issue**
Is the definition of "cultural patrimony" in the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA) unconstitutionally vague as applied to Mr. Corrow?

**Holding**
The defendant (Corrow) loses. The definition of "cultural patrimony" in NAGPRA is not unconstitutionally vague as applied to Mr. Corrow. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Void-for-Vagueness Doctrine.** A penal statute must define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited and in a manner that does not encourage arbitrary and discriminatory law enforcement.
- **Vagueness Challenge (Non-First Amendment).** Vagueness challenges to statutes not involving First Amendment freedoms must be examined in light of the facts of the case at hand.
- **Vagueness Challenge (Non-First Amendment, Facial).** To succeed in a facial challenge raising no First Amendment or other claim that the act reaches constitutionally protected conduct, the complainant must demonstrate that the law is impermissibly vague in all of its applications.
- **Scienter Requirement.** A statutory requirement that an act must be willful or purposeful may mitigate a criminal law's vagueness by ensuring that it punishes only those who are aware their conduct is unlawful.
- **Cultural Patrimony (NAGPRA).** An object having ongoing historical, traditional, or cultural importance central to the Native American group or culture itself, rather than property owned by an individual Native American, and which, therefore, cannot be alienated, appropriated, or conveyed by any individual regardless of whether or not the individual is a member of the Indian tribe or Native Hawaiian organization and such object shall have been considered inalienable by such Native American group at the time the object was separated from such group.
- **Migratory Bird Treaty Act (MBTA) Misdemeanor Violations.** Misdemeanor violations under 16 U.S.C. § 703 are strict liability crimes, eliminating proof of scienter from the government's case.

**Reasoning**
- The court applied the **void-for-vagueness doctrine** to Mr. Corrow's challenge.
    - The court found that Mr. Corrow had fair notice that the Yei B’Chei were cultural items that could not be bought and sold for profit.
        - Mr. Corrow was knowledgeable about Navajo traditions and culture.
        - He was aware that tribal members viewed ownership of such property differently.
        - He represented that he wanted to pass the Yei B’Chei to another chanter, suggesting he understood their inherent inalienability in Navajo culture.
        - No expert testified it was acceptable to sell Yei B’Chei to non-Navajos for profit.
        - The Yei B’Chei were powerfully connected to Navajo religion and culture, residing within the Four Corners of the Navajo people.
    - The court found that the statute provided sufficient guidance to law enforcement to prevent arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement.
        - Law enforcement officers are required to consult with tribal officials to determine if an item constitutes cultural patrimony.
        - In this case, a Park Service officer consulted with other knowledgeable officers and Navajo Nation representatives before determining the items were cultural patrimony.
    - The presence of a **scienter requirement** in 18 U.S.C. § 1170(b) (knowingly sells, purchases, uses for profit) further mitigated any potential vagueness.
        - The government had to prove Mr. Corrow knowingly used the Yei B’Chei for profit, ensuring his understanding of the prohibited conduct.
- The court affirmed the sufficiency of the evidence for Count 1, finding that a rational jury could conclude the Yei B’Chei were cultural patrimony that Mr. Corrow could not resell for profit.
- For the MBTA violation (Count 2), the court held that misdemeanor violations under 16 U.S.C. § 703 are **strict liability crimes**, meaning scienter is not required.
    - The plain language of the statute ("it shall be unlawful") supports this interpretation.
    - The court noted that the district court had, in fact, instructed the jury that it must find Mr. Corrow knowingly possessed the feathers, and the evidence supported this finding anyway.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the concept of **cultural patrimony** as defined by NAGPRA, which distinguishes certain cultural items from individually owned property. It connects to broader themes of property rights in cultural artifacts and the tension between individual ownership and communal/tribal claims, similar to discussions around **Moore v. Regents of the University of California** regarding the commodification of human biological materials, or **Ghen v. Rich** concerning custom in property acquisition. The case highlights how statutory definitions can redefine traditional notions of alienability and ownership, particularly when dealing with items of profound cultural significance.
2. The case raises the question of how an individual, even one knowledgeable about a culture, can ascertain the alienability of an item when experts within that culture disagree. This tension between individual understanding and complex cultural norms could lead to difficult hypotheticals regarding due diligence in purchasing cultural items.

**Takeaway:** NAGPRA's definition of "cultural patrimony" is not unconstitutionally vague, especially when a defendant has knowledge of the cultural significance and inalienability of the items, and the statute includes a scienter requirement for trafficking.

## Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois (Supreme Court of the United States, 1892)

**Facts**
- In 1818, Illinois was admitted to the Union on equal footing with original states, with boundaries including a portion of Lake Michigan.
- In 1850, Congress granted Illinois a right-of-way and land for a railroad, including a branch to Chicago.
- In 1851, Illinois incorporated the Illinois Central Railroad Company (ICRR) to build this railroad. The ICRR charter granted it a 200-foot wide right-of-way and the right to use state lands and waters for construction, but prohibited it from interrupting navigation of streams and required city consent for construction within city limits.
- In 1852, the City of Chicago (City) granted ICRR permission to build its tracks along the lakefront, requiring it to build a breakwater and other structures to protect the shore and not obstruct the view of the lake. The ICRR accepted this ordinance and built its tracks and breakwater, reclaiming land from the lake.
- The ICRR also acquired title to certain lots fronting the lake in sections 10 and 15 and, as riparian owner, constructed piers and docks.
- In 1869, the Illinois legislature passed the "Lake Front Act," which confirmed ICRR's existing rights to occupied submerged lands and granted it in fee all the state's right and title to submerged lands for one mile east of its tracks and breakwater, between the south pier and lot 21. This grant was perpetual, but ICRR could not sell the fee, and had to pay 7% of gross receipts from these lands to the state. The act also stated it would not authorize obstructions to Chicago harbor or impair public navigation.
- In 1870, ICRR accepted the 1869 act.
- In 1873, the Illinois legislature repealed the 1869 act.
- The ICRR continued to construct piers and docks, some with War Department approval.
- In 1883, the State of Illinois filed suit against ICRR and the City.

**Procedural History**
- The State of Illinois filed a bill in equity in Illinois Circuit Court against ICRR and the City of Chicago.
- ICRR removed the case to the U.S. Circuit Court for the Northern District of Illinois.
- The City filed a cross-bill against the State and ICRR.
- The Circuit Court issued a decree.
- ICRR, the State, and the City each appealed to the Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Can a state legislature grant a private corporation title to a substantial portion of the submerged lands under a public harbor, thereby relinquishing the state's control over those lands?

**Holding**
The State of Illinois wins. A state cannot abdicate its trust over navigable waters and the lands beneath them to a private corporation. Reversed in part, affirmed in part, and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Equal Footing Doctrine.** States admitted to the Union possess the same jurisdiction, sovereignty, and dominion over persons and subjects within their limits as the original states.
- **Public Trust Doctrine.** The ownership of and dominion and sovereignty over lands covered by navigable waters within state limits belong to the respective states, held in trust for the public.
- **Navigable Waters.** The doctrine of state ownership and sovereignty over lands under navigable waters applies to the Great Lakes, just as it applies to tide waters.
- **Limitations on State Grants.** A state may grant parcels of submerged lands for the erection of wharves, docks, and piers in aid of commerce, or dispose of parcels that do not substantially impair the public interest in the remaining lands and waters.
- **Abdication of Trust (Rejected).** A state cannot abdicate its general control over lands under the navigable waters of an entire harbor or bay, or of a sea or lake, to a private party. Such an attempted grant is necessarily revocable.
- **Police Power.** The soil under navigable waters is held by the people of the State in trust for common use and as a portion of their inherent sovereignty; legislation concerning their use is an exercise of the state's police power.
- **Irrepealable Contract (Rejected).** There can be no irrepealable contract in a conveyance of property by a grantor in disregard of a public trust under which it was bound to hold and manage it.
- **Riparian Rights.** Riparian rights are incident to riparian ownership and attach to land contiguous to navigable water. The construction of a pier or extension into navigable waters by one not the owner of lands on the shore does not create riparian rights.
- **Riparian Owner's Access.** A riparian proprietor is entitled to access to the navigable part of the water and may build a landing, wharf, or pier for their own or public use, subject to legislative regulation, as long as it does not extend beyond the point of navigability.

**Reasoning**
- The State of Illinois holds title to the lands under Lake Michigan in trust for the people.
    - This trust ensures public enjoyment of navigation, commerce, and fishing, free from private obstruction.
    - This title is different from lands intended for sale; it is a governmental trust that cannot be alienated.
- The 1869 grant to ICRR of over 1,000 acres of submerged lands in Chicago's harbor was an attempted abdication of this trust.
    - This area is immense, encompassing nearly the entire harbor and future harbor expansion.
    - Such a large-scale transfer to a private corporation, whose primary purpose is railroad transportation, is inconsistent with the state's duty to preserve public waters for public use.
    - The limitations in the act (no obstructions, no impairment of navigation) were already existing obligations and did not mitigate the fundamental transfer of control.
    - The prohibition against selling the fee was inconsequential, as ICRR could lease the land indefinitely for profit.
- The legislature lacked the competence to make such a grant.
    - The power to manage public trusts, like navigable waters, cannot be placed entirely beyond the direction and control of the State.
    - While small parcels can be granted for public improvements (wharves, docks) or without substantially impairing public interest, a grant of the *whole* property in which the public is interested is not permissible.
    - General language in prior cases about absolute state ownership must be read in context of specific facts, not as sanctioning abdication of an entire harbor.
    - The state's control over such property is a matter of public concern and cannot be given away.
- The 1869 grant was necessarily revocable, and the 1873 repealing act was valid and effective.
    - The state's power to resume the trust is incontrovertible.
    - There can be no irrepealable contract when a grantor disregards a public trust.
    - The 1869 act was essentially a license to ICRR to act as a state agency for harbor improvement; the state could revoke this agency.
- ICRR's existing structures and riparian claims require further judicial review.
    - The ICRR's right-of-way and structures built under the 1852 ordinance are valid as they do not interfere with navigation.
    - ICRR's claim to piers and docks based on riparian ownership of lots is valid only if they do not extend beyond the point of practical navigability. This needs to be determined by the lower court.
- The City of Chicago owns the fee to streets, alleys, and public grounds on the lakefront, including reclaimed land, subject to public uses and ICRR's right-of-way. The City retains its riparian rights.

**Dissent (Shiras, Gray, Brown)**
- The ownership of a state in lands underlying its navigable waters is as complete, and its power to make them the subject of conveyance is as full, as its power over other public lands.
- The majority concedes the state's power to grant private rights in parcels of such lands, but attempts to restrict it based on the "size of the parcel" or "detriment to public interests," which is an arbitrary limitation on legislative discretion.
- The 1869 act, when accepted by ICRR, created a valid contract, not a mere revocable license. The state received consideration (7% of gross receipts).
- The state cannot, by arbitrary revocation (the 1873 act), impair the obligation of this contract, as protected by the U.S. Constitution.
- While the state retains sovereign powers and the grant is subject to public rights of navigation, these are incidents of the grant and do not defeat it.
- If the state now regrets the contract due to Chicago's growth, it can condemn ICRR's property rights through eminent domain, rather than unilaterally revoking the grant.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a foundational case for the **Public Trust Doctrine**, establishing that states hold navigable waters and the lands beneath them in trust for the public, and cannot alienate them to private parties in a way that substantially impairs public use. It connects to **Ghen v. Rich** and **Pierson v. Post** in discussing the balance between private property rights and public interests, though in a very different context (state sovereignty vs. private corporations, rather than individual capture). It also contrasts with cases like **Pocono Springs Civic Association v. MacKenzie** where the court upheld a private owner's right to abandon property, as here the state cannot abandon its public trust.
2. The dissent raises an interesting point about the "size of the parcel" as a limiting factor. How much is "too much" for a state to grant? What if the grant was for 10 acres instead of 1000? This could lead to a flexible standard rather than a bright-line rule.

**Takeaway:** States hold navigable waters and their beds in an inalienable public trust, meaning they cannot grant away substantial portions of these resources to private entities, and any such attempted grant is revocable.

## State ex rel. Thornton v. Hay (Oregon Supreme Court, 1969)

**Facts**
- William and Georgianna Hay owned a tourist facility at Cannon Beach, Oregon, which included dry-sand area between the sixteen-foot elevation contour line and the ordinary high-tide line of the Pacific Ocean.
- The public had used the dry-sand area for recreational purposes, such as picnics, gathering wood, and building fires, since the beginning of Oregon's political history.
- State and local officers policed the dry sand, and municipal sanitary crews maintained the area.
- The dry-sand area is unstable, unsafe during winter storms, and generally unfit for permanent structures.
- In 1967, public debate and legislative activity occurred regarding public and private interests in the dry-sand area.
- The Hays sought to construct fences or other improvements in the dry-sand area of their property.

**Procedural History**
- The State of Oregon, ex rel. Thornton, brought an action against the Hays to enjoin them from constructing fences or improvements.
- The trial court found in favor of the state, ruling that the public had acquired an easement for recreational purposes in the dry-sand area.
- The Hays appealed.
- The Oregon Supreme Court affirmed the trial court's decree.

**Issue**
Can the state prevent landowners from enclosing the dry-sand area of their ocean-front property when the public has historically used it for recreation?

**Holding**
The State wins. The public has a right to use the dry-sand area of ocean-front property for recreational purposes based on the doctrine of custom. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Public Easement by Custom.** A public right to use private land can be established by long-standing custom if the custom is ancient, exercised without interruption, peaceable and free from dispute, reasonable, certain, obligatory, and not repugnant to other customs or law.
- **Rejected: Implied Dedication.** Dedication, whether express or implied, rests upon an intent to dedicate.
- **Rejected: Prescriptive Easement (for this case).** While the public can acquire prescriptive easements in private land in Oregon, the doctrine of custom provides a better legal basis for affirming the decree in this case because it applies to a larger region and the unique nature of the land.

**Reasoning**
- The dry-sand area has been enjoyed by the general public as a recreational adjunct to the wet-sand area since the beginning of Oregon's political history.
- The public's use of the dry-sand area meets all seven requisites of a custom as outlined by Blackstone:
    - **Ancient:** The public has freely exercised the right to use the dry-sand area up and down the Oregon coast for recreational purposes for as long as there has been an institutionalized system of land tenure in Oregon.
    - **Without interruption:** The public's use and enjoyment of the dry-sand area has never been interrupted by private landowners.
    - **Peaceable and free from dispute:** This is satisfied by the evidence relating to the lack of interruption.
    - **Reasonable:** The public has always used the land in a manner appropriate to the land and community usages.
    - **Certain:** The visible boundaries of the dry-sand area and the character of the land limit its use to recreational purposes connected with the foreshore.
    - **Obligatory:** The dry-sand area has been used as of right, uniformly with similarly situated lands, and the public's use has never been questioned by upland owners as long as they remained on the dry sand.
    - **Not repugnant:** The custom violates no law and is not inconsistent with other customs.
- The custom is so notorious that notice of it must be presumed for persons buying land along the shore.
- The rule based on custom confirms a public right and takes nothing from landowners that they had a legitimate reason to regard as exclusively theirs.
- *(dicta)* The court declined to rule on the constitutionality of ORS 390.640 as a zoning regulation if the public had not acquired an easement.
- *(dicta)* The court left open the effect of consent on the doctrine of prescription.

**Concurrence (Denecke, J.)**
- Disagrees with basing the decision on the English doctrine of "customary rights" because the facts cannot be fitted into that ancient doctrine.
- Believes "custom" is an important ingredient, but only as an analogy.
- Bases the public's right on: (1) long usage by the public of the dry sands area; (2) a universal and long-held belief by the public in their right to such use; (3) long and universal acquiescence by upland owners; and (4) the extreme desirability to the public of the right to use the dry sands.
- Argues this is a new concept for dry sands but not for other public usages, citing cases about public rights to boat on lakes and fish in navigable rivers, which are based on the "jus publicum" doctrine.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes that public rights can arise from long-standing custom, even over privately owned land, which is a significant limitation on the traditional bundle of sticks associated with fee simple ownership. It connects to the idea of **public rights** and **custom** as sources of property law, similar to how **Ghen v. Rich** considered custom in establishing property rights in whales. It also touches on the tension between private property rights and public access, a theme seen in cases like **Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois** regarding the public trust doctrine.
2. The concurrence's argument for a broader "public interest" doctrine, rather than strict adherence to the ancient doctrine of custom, raises the question of how far courts should go in recognizing public rights based on evolving societal needs and expectations, especially when it impacts established private property interests.

**Takeaway:** Long-standing public custom can create an easement for public recreational use over privately owned dry-sand beach areas, limiting the landowner's right to exclude.

## Evans v. Merriweather (Illinois Supreme Court, 1842)

**Facts**
- In 1834, Smith & Baker bought six acres of land with a branch running through it and built a steam mill, relying on a well and the branch for water.
- One or two years later, John Evans bought six acres of land on the same branch, immediately upstream from Smith & Baker, and built a steam mill, also relying on a well and the branch for water.
- In 1836 or 1837, Smith & Baker sold their mill to Henry Merriweather.
- In the fall of 1837, during a drought, the branch did not provide enough water for both mills.
- Evans instructed his workers not to stop or divert the water in the branch.
- One of Evans's workers built a dam across the branch below Evans's mill, diverting all the water into Evans's well.
- Evans lived half a mile from the mill and was frequently there.
- After the diversion, the branch below Evans's mill went dry, and Merriweather's mill could only run about one day a week, using water from his well.
- Merriweather brought suit three or four weeks after the dam was built.
- The branch usually provided enough water for both mills without significantly affecting the current.
- At the time of the grievance, Evans was also hauling water for his boilers.
- The dam diverted both the natural branch water and any water Evans hauled and poured into the branch above his well.

**Procedural History**
- Merriweather (plaintiff) brought an action on the case in the Greene Circuit Court for obstructing and diverting a watercourse.
- The trial court rendered a verdict and judgment for Merriweather for $150 damages.
- Evans (defendant) appealed to the Illinois Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Can an upstream riparian proprietor divert an entire stream for manufacturing purposes if it deprives a downstream proprietor of water necessary for their manufacturing, especially when the stream is insufficient for both?

**Holding**
Merriweather (appellee) wins. An upstream riparian proprietor cannot divert an entire stream for artificial uses like manufacturing if it deprives a downstream proprietor of water for similar uses, especially when the stream is insufficient for both. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Riparian Rights (Usufructuary).** The property in water, by virtue of riparian ownership, is usufructuary, consisting of the advantage of its impetus rather than the fluid itself.
- **Reasonable Use Doctrine.** A riparian proprietor has a right to use the water of a stream but must do so in a way that causes no injury to other riparian proprietors, making a reasonable use of the water.
- **Natural vs. Artificial Wants.** Water uses are categorized as natural (absolutely necessary for existence, e.g., drinking, household, livestock) or artificial (increasing comfort and prosperity but not essential for existence, e.g., irrigation, manufacturing).
- **Priority of Natural Wants.** If a stream is small and only provides enough water for the natural wants of proprietors, none can use it for artificial purposes like irrigation or manufacturing.
- **Sharing for Artificial Wants.** Where water is not needed for natural wants, and there isn't enough for all proprietors to carry on manufacturing, no single proprietor has a right to use all the water; all have a right to participate in its benefit.
- **Jury Determination of Reasonable Use.** When all have a right to participate in a common benefit and no one has exclusive enjoyment, it is left to the jury to determine if a party has used more than their just proportion.
- **Employer Liability for Servant's Actions.** If a party avails themselves of the illegal act of their servant, the law presumes they authorized it.
- **Rejected: Absolute Non-Diminution Rule.** Decisions that restrict water use such that there shall be no diminution in quantity or obstruction to its course are not the true doctrine, as they would deny any valuable use of the water.
- **Rejected: Priority of Appropriation.** Mere priority of appropriation of running water, without general consent or grant, confers no exclusive right.

**Reasoning**
- The court adopts a **reasonable use doctrine** for riparian rights, balancing the needs of all proprietors.
    - This doctrine rejects the idea that any diminution or obstruction is illegal, as that would prevent any valuable use of water.
    - It also rejects the idea that mere priority of use grants an exclusive right.
- The court distinguishes between **natural wants** (essential for life) and **artificial wants** (for comfort/prosperity).
    - Natural wants (drinking, household, livestock) take precedence; an upstream owner can consume all water for these if necessary.
    - Artificial wants (manufacturing, irrigation in non-arid climates) are secondary.
- For artificial wants, when water is insufficient for all, no single proprietor can consume the entire stream.
    - All proprietors have a right to participate in the benefit of the water.
    - The question of what constitutes a "just proportion" is a factual one for the jury.
- Evans's complete diversion of the water for his steam mill was an artificial use and clearly illegal, as it deprived Merriweather of any water for his mill.
- Even though Evans claimed he forbade the diversion, his proximity to the mill and frequent presence allowed the jury to infer his knowledge and either countermanding of instructions or acquiescence in the dam's construction.
    - By benefiting from the diversion, Evans is presumed to have authorized it.
- The court affirmed the judgment despite potential issues with jury instructions because the damages were small, and justice was done on the facts. *(dicta)*

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes the **reasonable use doctrine** for riparian rights in Illinois, contrasting with the earlier "natural flow" theory (which suggested no diminution was allowed) and rejecting the "prior appropriation" doctrine (which grants rights based on first use). It is similar to **Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.** in that both deal with water rights, but Coffin adopts prior appropriation, while Evans adopts reasonable use. It also contrasts with **Higday v. Nickolaus**, which deals with groundwater and the absolute ownership rule.
2. The distinction between "natural" and "artificial" wants is a key element of the reasonable use doctrine. How would this distinction apply to modern water uses, such as large-scale agriculture for food production (arguably essential for human existence) or data centers requiring significant cooling water? Is the "natural wants" category too narrow in a modern context?

**Takeaway:** Riparian landowners have a right to the reasonable use of water flowing through their land, prioritizing natural wants over artificial ones, and requiring sharing for artificial uses when water is scarce.

## Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co. (Colorado Supreme Court, 1882)

**Facts**
- Appellee (Left Hand Ditch Co.) claimed ownership of water appropriated from the south fork of the St. Vrain creek.
- Appellee diverted this water through a ditch to James creek, then to Left Hand creek, where it was used to irrigate lands.
- Appellants (Coffin et al.) owned lands along the St. Vrain creek below the south fork, which were naturally irrigated by the St. Vrain.
- In 1879, there was not enough water in the St. Vrain to supply both appellee's ditch and irrigate appellants' lands.
- Appellants tore out a portion of appellee's dam, interfering with its water diversion.
- Appellee brought an action for damages and injunctive relief.

**Procedural History**
- Appellee sued appellants in District Court of Boulder County.
- Appellants' defenses included a specific denial, an alleged agreement about water diversion, and claims of water rights based on land ownership and appropriations.
- Demurrers were sustained to all defenses except the specific denial.
- Trial was held before a jury, which returned a verdict and judgment for appellee.
- Appellants appealed.
- Affirmed.

**Issue**
Can a party who diverts water from a natural stream for a beneficial use claim a prior right to that water, even if their land is not adjacent to the stream and other landowners claim riparian rights?

**Holding**
Appellee (Left Hand Ditch Co.) wins. The first appropriator of water from a natural stream for a beneficial purpose has a prior right to that water, regardless of the location of its application. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Prior Appropriation Doctrine.** The first appropriator of water from a natural stream for a beneficial purpose has a prior right to that water, to the extent of such appropriation.
- **Riparian Rights (Rejected).** The common law doctrine giving riparian owners a right to the flow of water in its natural channel, even without beneficial use, is inapplicable in Colorado due to imperative necessity.
- **Locus of Application.** The right to water acquired by prior appropriation is not dependent upon the locus of its application to the beneficial use designed.
- **Pre-existing Right.** The right to water by priority of appropriation existed prior to legislation on the subject of irrigation and is entitled to protection even after a patent to a third party for the land over which the stream flows.

**Reasoning**
- The climate in Colorado is dry, and artificial irrigation is an absolute necessity for agriculture.
- Water, when appropriated, rises to the dignity of a distinct usufructuary estate, not merely an incident to the soil.
- Both national and territorial/state governments have consistently encouraged the diversion and use of water for agriculture.
- Denying the doctrine of priority of right by prior appropriation would destroy the value of vast property and improvements made with the understanding that such appropriations would be protected.
- The common law doctrine of riparian proprietorship is unsuitable for Colorado's arid climate and is rejected due to "imperative necessity."
- The right of prior appropriation is not dependent on the location where the water is used.
    - It would be inequitable to deprive someone of water simply because they incurred significant expense to carry water over an intervening watershed to cultivate land in another valley.
    - Topography might make it impossible or impractical to get water from an adjacent stream.
    - Applying a riparian rule would prevent productive cultivation and sanction waste of water on sterile lands.
- Early statutes, while seemingly ambiguous, are interpreted to support the doctrine of prior appropriation, especially considering the "due regard to the legal rights of all" clause and the universal custom of respecting prior appropriations.
- The act of Congress protecting water rights in patents was a recognition of a pre-existing right, not the creation of a new one.
- The court found no error in sustaining demurrers to appellants' defenses, including the alleged agreement, as forcibly destroying the dam without notice was not excused.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is foundational for understanding the **Prior Appropriation Doctrine** in water law, which contrasts sharply with the **Riparian Rights** doctrine. It illustrates how environmental conditions (arid climate) can lead to the rejection of common law property principles in favor of new ones based on **productive use** and **labor**. This case connects to **Evans v. Merriweather** and **Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.** by showing the divergence of water rights systems based on regional needs. It also highlights the tension between established common law and the need for new legal frameworks to support economic development, similar to how **Ghen v. Rich** addressed custom in whaling.
2. The court emphasizes "imperative necessity" as the driving force behind adopting prior appropriation. What if the climate were to change significantly, making irrigation less necessary? Would the legal doctrine shift back towards riparian rights, or is the doctrine now too entrenched?

**Takeaway:** In arid regions, the first to appropriate water for a beneficial use gains a superior right to that water, regardless of land ownership along the stream.

## Higday v. Nickolaus (Kansas City Court of Appeals, 1971)

**Facts**
- Plaintiffs own approximately 6,000 acres of farmland in the McBaine Bottom, overlying an alluvial water basin.
- The land is fertile due to a high subterranean water level, which supplies moisture to crops and is used for personal consumption and livestock.
- The City of Columbia, a municipality of 50,000 inhabitants, sought a new water source due to dwindling supplies.
- Consulting engineers advised the City to withdraw water from the McBaine Bottom via shallow wells and transport it 12 miles away for sale.
- In December 1966, the electorate approved a revenue bond issue for this municipal water supply project.
- Scientific analysis determined the underground percolating water table, when undisturbed, rises to an average of ten feet below the soil surface and moves laterally at two feet per day, displacing 10.5 million gallons daily.
- The City acquired five well sites totaling 17.25 acres from some plaintiffs through threat of condemnation.
- The City intends to extract groundwater at a rate of 11.5 million gallons daily for sale, purposes unrelated to the beneficial use of the overlying land.
- This extraction will reduce the water table from an average of ten feet to twenty feet below the surface.
- Plaintiffs allege this reduction will divert percolating waters normally available, turning their land into an arid and sterile surface.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs filed a Petition for Declaratory Judgment and Injunction.
- The trial court dismissed the petition based on the City's Motion to Dismiss, which alleged failure to plead a justiciable controversy or a claim for relief.
- Plaintiffs appealed the dismissal.

**Issue**
Can a municipality withdraw percolating groundwater from beneath landowners' property for sale to its inhabitants, if doing so significantly lowers the water table and injures the landowners' beneficial use of their land?

**Holding**
Plaintiffs (landowners) win. A municipality may not withdraw percolating water and transport it for sale away from the land from which it was taken if the result is to impair the supply of an adjoining landowner to his injury. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Justiciable Controversy.** A petition for declaratory relief must allege facts showing a subsisting justiciable controversy between the parties as to their respective rights and duties, admitting of specific relief that is conclusive and determinative.
- **Declaratory Judgment.** A dispute as to legal rights does not require an accomplished injury to be ripe for declaratory adjudication.
- **Injunction.** An injunction requires a substantial right to be protected and a wrongful and injurious invasion of that legal right, or a threatened invasion by one with the power to do wrong.
- **Percolating Waters.** All underground waters are presumed to be percolating, meaning they pass through the ground without a definite channel.
- **English Common Law Rule (Percolating Waters).** A landowner has absolute ownership of percolating waters under their land and may withdraw any quantity for any purpose without liability, even if it drains water from neighbors' lands. Rejected: This rule is based on unsound scientific postulates and does not consider the rights of adjoining landowners.
- **American Rule / Rule of Reasonable Use (Percolating Waters).** An overlying owner has a proprietary interest in the water under their land, but incidents of ownership are restricted to reasonable exercise of rights and reasonable use of property, in view of similar rights of others.
- **Reasonable Use (Percolating Waters - Specific Application).** An overlying owner, including a municipality, may not withdraw percolating water and transport it for sale or other use away from the land from which it was taken if the result is to impair the supply of an adjoining landowner to his injury. Such a use is unreasonable because it is non-beneficial to the overlying land.
- **Eminent Domain (Injunction).** If injunctive relief is appropriate for an appropriation of private property for a public purpose, a court may condition the injunction on the acquiring party exercising its power of eminent domain to acquire the violated rights.
- **Comparative Injury (Injunction).** Injunctions are matters of grace, not right, and courts must consider their effect on all parties and the public, weighing relative convenience, inconvenience, and comparative injuries.

**Reasoning**
- The petition sufficiently pleads a justiciable controversy because it describes an actual, existing, and real controversy where the City's actions will inevitably culminate in damage to plaintiffs' lands by lowering the water table.
- The City's argument that the English common law rule of absolute ownership applies in Missouri is rejected.
    - The English rule was established after Missouri adopted the common law, so it is not binding.
    - The single Missouri case on groundwater, *Springfield Waterworks Co. v. Jenkins*, contained dictum suggesting the English rule but its holding could be reconciled with a reasonable use limitation.
    - The scientific premises underlying the English rule (that groundwater movement is "secret, changeable, and uncontrollable") have been discredited by modern hydrology.
    - The Supreme Court of Missouri has already applied the rule of reasonable use to riparian owners and subterranean streams, suggesting a uniform standard for all waters is appropriate.
- The **rule of reasonable use** is adopted for percolating waters.
    - This rule recognizes a proprietary interest but restricts it to beneficial enjoyment of the overlying land.
    - Transporting water for sale away from the land, to the injury of an adjoining landowner, is an unreasonable use.
    - A municipality acts as a private owner when acquiring land for water, and its need for water does not justify injuring adjoining landowners.
- Plaintiffs' petition sufficiently pleads a property right to the reasonable use of percolating waters threatened by the City's actions, thus invoking the court's equitable jurisdiction for injunctive relief.
- While an injunction is an extraordinary remedy and the court must consider public interest (e.g., the City's water supply), the trial court erred in dismissing the petition without an evidentiary hearing.
- If the City's withdrawals do not interfere with plaintiffs' beneficial use, there would be no basis for complaint.
- If the City's actions cause injury, it will be accountable for damages.
- The trial court could condition an injunction on the City exercising its power of eminent domain to acquire the water rights it is violating, or plaintiffs could pursue inverse condemnation.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is significant for the shift from the **English Rule of Absolute Ownership** to the **American Rule of Reasonable Use** for percolating groundwater. It connects to **Evans v. Merriweather** and **Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.** by illustrating different approaches to water rights (riparian vs. prior appropriation) and the evolution of common law to address changing societal needs and scientific understanding. The court explicitly rejects the "dogma of absolute ownership" in favor of a more equitable allocation, similar to how courts might balance competing interests in other property disputes.
2. The court mentions that the City could limit its withdrawals to the daily recharge rate (10.5 million gallons) to avoid damage. What if the City's need for water exceeds this rate, and there are no other viable sources? Does the "reasonable use" rule effectively cap a municipality's growth if it relies on groundwater?

**Takeaway:** Courts may evolve common law property rules, such as those governing percolating groundwater, to reflect modern scientific understanding and balance competing beneficial uses, particularly when public and private interests clash.

## Intel Corp. v. Hamidi (Supreme Court of California, 2003)

**Facts**
- Intel Corporation (Intel) maintained an electronic mail system for its employees, allowing reasonable nonbusiness use.
- Kourosh Kenneth Hamidi, a former Intel employee, formed an organization called Former and Current Employees of Intel (FACE-Intel) to criticize Intel's employment practices.
- Over almost two years, Hamidi sent six mass e-mails to thousands of current Intel employees using Intel's e-mail system.
- Hamidi obtained employee addresses from an anonymously sent floppy disk and did not breach Intel's computer security.
- The messages criticized Intel's employment practices, warned employees, solicited participation in FACE-Intel, and urged employees to visit FACE-Intel's website.
- Hamidi offered to remove recipients from his mailing list upon request, and did so for those who asked.
- Intel demanded Hamidi stop sending e-mails in March 1998, but he sent another mass mailing in September 1998.
- Intel's attempts to block the messages were only partially successful, as Hamidi evaded blocking efforts.
- Hamidi's communications caused discussion among employees and managers, and some employees asked management to stop the messages.
- Intel staff spent time attempting to block further messages.
- Hamidi's messages caused no physical damage or functional disruption to Intel's computers.

**Procedural History**
- Intel sued Hamidi and FACE-Intel, alleging trespass to chattels and nuisance, and sought damages and an injunction.
- Intel later dismissed its nuisance claim and waived its demand for damages.
- The trial court granted Intel's motion for summary judgment and issued a permanent injunction against Hamidi and FACE-Intel from sending unsolicited e-mail to Intel's systems.
- Hamidi appealed.
- The Court of Appeal affirmed the injunction, with one justice dissenting.
- The Supreme Court of California granted Hamidi's petition for review.

**Issue**
Can sending unsolicited e-mails that do not physically damage a computer system or impair its functioning constitute trespass to chattels?

**Holding**
Hamidi (defendant) wins. Sending unsolicited e-mails that do not physically damage a computer system or impair its functioning does not constitute trespass to chattels under California law. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Trespass to Chattels (California Law).** Trespass to chattels requires an intentional interference with possession of personal property that proximately causes injury to the chattel or to the plaintiff's rights in it.
- **Injury Requirement.** For interference with personal property not amounting to conversion, the owner may recover only actual damages suffered due to impairment of the property or loss of its use.
- **Restatement (Second) of Torts § 218.** An actor who intermeddles with another's chattel is subject to liability only if the intermeddling is harmful to the possessor's materially valuable interest in the physical condition, quality, or value of the chattel, or if the possessor is deprived of the use of the chattel for a substantial time, or some other legally protected interest of the possessor is affected.
- **Rejected: Trespass to Chattels without Actual Harm.** An interference with personal property (not amounting to dispossession) is not actionable without a showing of harm.
- **Injunctive Relief.** To obtain injunctive relief, the plaintiff must ordinarily show that the defendant's wrongful acts threaten to cause irreparable injuries.
- **Rejected: Computer Servers as Real Property.** Computer servers are personal property, not realty, for purposes of trespass law.

**Reasoning**
- The tort of trespass to chattels requires actual injury to the chattel or the possessor's rights in it.
    - This is consistent with California law (Zaslow v. Kroenert) and the Restatement (Second) of Torts § 218.
    - Unlike trespass to land, trespass to chattels generally does not allow for nominal damages for harmless intermeddling.
- Intel failed to demonstrate actual or threatened injury to its computer system or its rights in the property.
    - There was no evidence of physical damage, functional impairment, or dispossession of Intel's computers.
    - Hamidi's messages did not slow or impair the system's functioning, nor did they impose marginal costs on its operation.
    - The economic damage Intel claimed (lost employee productivity, staff time blocking messages) is not an injury to the computers themselves, which worked as intended.
    - This is distinct from "spamming" cases (e.g., CompuServe Inc. v. Cyber Promotions, Inc.) where the sheer volume of messages overburdened systems and impaired functioning.
    - The court rejected the idea that "impairment by content" (distraction from message content) constitutes injury to the computer system itself.
    - Intel cannot assert a property interest in its employees' time through trespass to chattels.
    - The cost of Intel's self-help efforts to block messages cannot be bootstrapped into an injury to its possessory interest in its computers.
- The court declined to extend California common law to cover harmless electronic communications whose contents are objectionable.
    - Extending trespass law to treat computer servers as real property, as suggested by some amici, would create a rigid property rule with potentially high costs to communication and network benefits.
    - The Internet is not a physical space, and applying real property rules to intangible intrusions (like electromagnetic transmissions) would require physical damage, which was not present here.
    - The Legislature is better suited to regulate unwanted electronic communications.
- Constitutional considerations (First Amendment) were not fully addressed because the common law grounds were dispositive, but the court noted that state action (like an injunction) must comply with First Amendment limits.
    - The injunction was sweeping, covering all communication to Intel addresses, which would not be justified by a "right not to listen" that applies to individual recipients.

**Dissent (Brown, J.)**
- Intel suffered actual injury through lost employee productivity and the expense of blocking messages, which impaired the value and utility of its computer system.
- The tort of trespass to chattels does not require physical damage; impairment of the chattel's value to the owner (subjective utility) is sufficient, as illustrated by the "toothbrush" example in the Restatement.
- Unauthorized use of another's property, even without damage, can be a trespass, especially when one party appropriates resources paid for by another (e.g., Buchanan Marine Inc. v. McCormack Sand Co.).
- Injunctive relief is appropriate for continuing trespasses, even without a showing of actual damage, to protect property rights and avoid multiplicity of suits (Mendelson v. McCabe).
- The right to exclude unwanted speech, recognized in cases like Rowan v. U.S. Post Office Dept., applies to private property owners like Intel, regardless of the content of the messages.
- The majority's ruling creates a perverse incentive for companies to invest less in computer capacity.

**Dissent (Mosk, J.)**
- The repeated transmission of bulk e-mails by Hamidi to Intel employees, despite demands to cease, constituted an actionable trespass to chattels.
- The majority fails to distinguish between open communication in the public internet and unauthorized intermeddling on a private, proprietary intranet.
- Hamidi's actions are akin to intruding into a private office mailroom and distributing unwanted broadsides.
- Intel's security measures were circumvented, leaving Intel with no recourse unless a malfunction or system crash occurs.
- The law of trespass to chattels is not limited to physical damage and can apply when a proprietary system is used contrary to its owner's purposes and desires, and self-help is ineffective.
- The costs incurred by Intel to block messages and the diminished employee productivity constitute sufficient damages to establish trespass to chattels.
- Other courts have applied trespass to chattels to similar situations involving unauthorized electronic contact with computer systems (e.g., CompuServe, eBay).
- Intel's connection to the Internet for business purposes does not forfeit its right to control access to its private system.
- Trespass to chattels is the most efficient and appropriate remedy, as it focuses on unauthorized use rather than content.
- There is no federal or state constitutional right to trespass.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the modern application of the common law tort of **trespass to chattels** in the context of digital property and the internet. It distinguishes between physical harm/functional impairment and mere annoyance or economic disruption caused by content. It connects to **Intel Corp. v. Hamidi** itself, as it is the case. It also relates to cases like **eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C.** and **ProCD Inc. v. Zeidenberg** by exploring the boundaries of property rights in digital and intangible assets, and the tension between property protection and open access/communication. The discussion of the "right to exclude" and its limits is central, echoing themes in **Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois** (public trust doctrine limiting exclusion) and **State ex rel. Thornton v. Hay** (public access to beaches).
2. The case raises the question of how to balance a property owner's right to exclude with the public interest in free communication, especially in digital spaces. What if Hamidi's messages had caused a measurable, but small, slowdown in Intel's system? Would that be enough for trespass? What if the messages were not just critical, but contained false information that directly harmed Intel's business? Would other torts be more appropriate, and if so, does that mean trespass to chattels is simply not the right tool for these types of harms?

**Takeaway:** For trespass to chattels in California, unauthorized electronic contact with a computer system requires actual or threatened physical damage or functional impairment to the system itself, not merely economic harm or annoyance caused by the content of the communication.

## Berg v. Wiley (Minnesota Supreme Court, 1978)

**Facts**
- On November 11, 1970, Wiley (landlord) and Berg's predecessor in interest (lessee) executed a lease for a restaurant for a 5-year term.
- The lease required the tenant to bear repair costs, make no structural changes without written authorization, and operate the restaurant lawfully.
- The lease also allowed Wiley to retake possession if the lessee failed to meet conditions.
- In early 1971, Kathleen Berg took assignment of the lease and opened "A Family Affair Restaurant."
- In January 1973, Berg incorporated the restaurant and assigned her interest in the lease to the corporation, continuing to act as sole shareholder.
- In June and July 1973, Wiley objected to Berg's remodeling without permission and alleged health code violations.
- On June 29, 1973, Wiley's attorney sent a letter to Berg, alleging lease breaches and demanding completion of remodeling items by July 13, 1973, or Wiley would retake possession.
- A June 13, 1973, health department inspection also required changes by July 15, 1973.
- Berg continued to operate the restaurant during this 2-week period.
- On Friday, July 13, 1973, Berg dismissed employees, closed the restaurant, and placed a "Closed for Remodeling" sign in the window.
- Earlier that day, Berg testified Wiley attempted to change the locks in her absence but complied with her request to leave them unchanged when she returned.
- Later that evening, Berg observed Wiley peering into the window and pounding on the back door.
- Berg called the sheriff, and Wiley called the police, leading to an agreement to preserve the status quo until Monday, July 16, 1973, for legal consultation.
- Wiley's attorney advised him to take possession and lock the tenant out.
- On Monday, July 16, 1973, Wiley, accompanied by a police officer and locksmith, entered the premises in Berg's absence and changed the locks.
- Berg found herself locked out later that day.
- The lease term was not due to expire until December 1, 1975.
- The premises were re-let to another tenant around August 1, 1973.
- Berg filed a damage action against Wiley on July 27, 1973.

**Procedural History**
- Berg sued Wiley for wrongful eviction and intentional infliction of emotional distress.
- Wiley asserted an affirmative defense of abandonment and surrender and counterclaimed for damages to the premises.
- The trial court found as a matter of law that Wiley locked Berg out and that the lockout was wrongful.
- The jury found no liability on Berg's emotional distress claim and no liability on Wiley's counterclaim.
- The jury awarded Berg $31,000 for lost profits and $3,540 for loss of chattels due to the wrongful lockout.
- The jury specifically found that Berg neither abandoned nor surrendered the premises.
- The trial court granted Wiley's post-trial motion for indemnification for mechanics lien liability by way of set-off from Berg's judgment.
- Wiley appealed the damages award for wrongful eviction.

**Issue**
Can a landlord use self-help to retake leased premises from a tenant who has not abandoned or surrendered the property, without resorting to judicial process?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (tenant) wins. A landlord may not use self-help to dispossess a tenant who has not abandoned or voluntarily surrendered the premises, but must instead resort to judicial process. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Common-law rule for self-help eviction.** A landlord may use self-help to retake leased premises if (1) the landlord is legally entitled to possession and (2) the means of reentry are peaceable.
- **Rejected: Peaceable reentry under common law.** Reentry by changing locks in the tenant's absence, even without actual violence, is not peaceable under the common-law rule if the tenant is claiming a right to possession.
- **Modern rule for landlord repossession.** The only lawful means to dispossess a tenant who has not abandoned or voluntarily surrendered but who claims possession adversely to a landlord's claim of breach of a written lease is by resort to judicial process.

**Reasoning**
- The jury's finding that Berg did not abandon or surrender the premises was supported by sufficient evidence, as she intended to retain possession and close temporarily for remodeling.
- The court's long-standing policy is to discourage landlords from taking the law into their own hands, especially when it could lead to breaches of the peace.
- Wiley's reentry by changing locks in Berg's absence, while she was asserting a right to possession, was not peaceable because it created a potential for public disturbance.
    - To approve such a lockout would encourage tenants to be vigilant, setting the stage for violence.
- The court adopts the modern rule that self-help is never available to dispossess a tenant in possession who has not abandoned or voluntarily surrendered the premises.
    - This rule recognizes the inherent potential for violent breach of peace in such self-help attempts.
    - Adequate and speedy judicial remedies are available to landlords through statutory summary procedures (Minn.St. 566.02 to 566.17) and temporary restraining orders.
- Applying the modern rule, Wiley's lockout was wrongful as a matter of law because he failed to resort to judicial remedies.
- Even under the common-law rule, the lockout would be considered nonpeaceable given the history of dispute and animosity, and Berg's absence being the only reason actual violence did not erupt.
- Equity dictates that Wiley, who caused the damage, should bear the loss.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is significant because it explicitly rejects the common-law rule allowing "peaceable" self-help eviction and adopts the modern rule requiring judicial process. It connects to *Berg v. Wiley* (the prior unlawful detainer action) by showing the tenant's attempt to use judicial process. It also relates to *Williams v. Ford Motor Credit Co.* by illustrating the limits on self-help repossession to prevent breaches of the peace.
2. What if the tenant had explicitly stated they were abandoning the property, but then returned to find the locks changed? Would that still be considered a wrongful eviction under this new rule?

**Takeaway:** Landlords must always use judicial process to evict a tenant who has not abandoned or voluntarily surrendered the premises, even if the lease allows reentry and the reentry is accomplished without actual violence.

## Williams v. Ford Motor Credit Co. (United States Court of Appeals, Eighth Circuit, 1982)

**Facts**
- In July 1975, David Williams purchased a Ford Mustang, and the title was in the names of both David and Cathy Williams.
- The car was financed through a Ford dealer, who assigned the paper to Ford Motor Credit Company (FMCC).
- In 1977, Cathy and David Williams divorced, and the divorce court granted Cathy title to the car and required David to continue payments to FMCC for 18 months.
- David defaulted on payments and signed a voluntary repossession authorization for FMCC.
- Cathy Williams was informed of the delinquency.
- FMCC directed S & S Recovery, Inc. (S & S) to repossess the automobile.
- On December 1, 1977, at approximately 4:30 a.m., Cathy Williams was awakened by a noise and saw a wrecker truck hooking up to her car.
- She went outside, told the men the car was hers, and asked what they were doing.
- One man, Don Sappington of S & S, informed her he was repossessing the vehicle for FMCC.
- Williams explained she was trying to bring payments up to date and that the car contained personal items.
- Sappington retrieved the personal items from the car and handed them to her.
- Williams made no further complaint, and Sappington drove off with the car.
- Williams testified that Sappington was polite and did not threaten her or cause her to fear physical harm.
- The car was parked in an unenclosed driveway.
- Williams called the police and reported her car stolen.

**Procedural History**
- Cathy Williams sued FMCC for conversion.
- A jury awarded Williams $5,000 in damages.
- FMCC moved for judgment notwithstanding the verdict.
- The district court initially ordered a nonsuit without prejudice.
- The Eighth Circuit reversed and remanded, directing the district court to rule on FMCC's motion.
- The district court entered judgment notwithstanding the verdict for FMCC.
- Williams appealed this judgment.

**Issue**
Did the repossession of Cathy Williams' car constitute a breach of the peace under Arkansas law, thereby making it an unlawful conversion?

**Holding**
Defendant (FMCC) wins. The repossession was a legal repossession under Arkansas law because there was no breach of the peace. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Self-help repossession.** Unless otherwise agreed, a secured party has on default the right to take possession of the collateral.
- **Breach of the peace.** In taking possession, a secured party may proceed without judicial process if this can be done without breach of the peace.
- **Breach of the peace (Arkansas pre-Code cases).** A finding of conversion only occurred where force, or threats of force, or risk of invoking violence, accompanied the repossession.
- **Rejected:** A breach of the peace occurs if the debtor does not give permission but also does not object. *(The court implies this rule was rejected by earlier cases where no objection meant no breach.)*

**Reasoning**
- The Uniform Commercial Code (UCC) allows self-help repossession without judicial process if it can be done "without breach of the peace."
- Arkansas pre-Code cases, which inform the interpretation of "breach of the peace," required force, threats of force, or a risk of invoking violence for a conversion finding.
- Williams admitted that the repossessors were polite, complied with her requests, and did not perform any oppressive or threatening acts.
- There was no evidence that Williams would have been forced to resort to physical violence to stop the repossession.
- Williams did not object to the taking after Sappington retrieved her personal items.
- The repossession was accomplished without any incident that might tend to provoke violence.
- While the night-time repossession was not commendable, it was legal under Arkansas law given the lack of objection or confrontation that risked violence.

**Dissent (Heaney, Circuit Judge)**
- The jury's finding of a breach of the peace by creating a "risk of invoking violence" was reasonable and should not have been overturned.
- Williams did everything she could to stop the repossession short of physical force, and the confrontation did not result in violence only because she was powerless.
- The commotion awakened neighbors, indicating a disturbance.
- The policy of the UCC restriction on self-help is to discourage extrajudicial acts fraught with the likelihood of violence.
- When an invasion is detected and a confrontation ensues, the repossessor should be under a duty to retreat and turn to judicial process.
- The majority's rule invites tragic consequences by allowing repossession to proceed after confrontation unless violence actually results.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the limits of self-help remedies and the interpretation of "breach of the peace" in the context of secured transactions, connecting to the broader theme of property rights and their enforcement. It contrasts with cases like *Berg v. Wiley*, where a landlord's self-help repossession was deemed a breach of the peace due to the tenant's clear objection and the landlord's use of force (changing locks). Here, the court finds no breach because the debtor did not actively object or resist, distinguishing it from situations where confrontation escalates.
2. The dissent raises an important tension: should the law require a repossessor to retreat upon any confrontation, or only when violence is imminent? The majority's ruling seems to require a higher threshold of actual or threatened violence.

**Takeaway:** A self-help repossession does not constitute a breach of the peace if the debtor does not actively object or resist, and no force or threats of force are used by the repossessor.

## Ploof v. Putnam (Vermont Supreme Court, 1908)

**Facts**
- On November 13, 1904, the plaintiff, Sylvester A. Ploof, was sailing his loaded sloop on Lake Champlain with his wife and two minor children.
- A sudden and violent tempest arose, placing the sloop and its occupants in great danger.
- To save his sloop, his family, and its contents, Ploof moored his sloop to a dock owned by the defendant, Henry W. Putnam, which was in charge of Putnam's servant.
- Putnam, through his servant, unmoored Ploof's sloop.
- The tempest then drove the unmoored sloop onto the shore, destroying it and its contents, and casting Ploof and his family into the lake and onto the shore, causing them injuries.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiff sued for damages in trespass and case.
- Defendant demurred to each count of the declaration.
- The trial court (Chittenden County, Haselton, J.) overruled the demurrers and adjudged each count sufficient.
- Defendant excepted.

**Issue**
Can a person be held liable for unmooring a vessel that was lawfully moored to their dock out of necessity to save lives and property during a tempest?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Ploof) wins. A person is liable for unmooring a vessel lawfully moored to their dock out of necessity, as such mooring is not a trespass. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Necessity Doctrine.** Necessity, due to an inability to control movements started without fault and in the proper exercise of a strict right, will often justify interferences with another’s real or personal property that would otherwise have been trespasses, especially to preserve human life or to avoid bodily harm.
- **Scope of Employment.** An allegation that a defendant acted "by his servant" implies that the servant was acting within the scope of his employment.

**Reasoning**
- The declaration sufficiently alleges a necessity for mooring the sloop to the defendant's dock to save lives and property.
    - The averment covers both the necessity of mooring and the necessity of mooring to this specific dock.
    - Details of the situation creating the necessity are matters of proof, not required in the pleading.
- The **necessity doctrine** applies with special force to the preservation of human life.
    - Examples include: chasing trespassing sheep off land, cattle escaping while being driven to pound, travelers passing onto adjoining land when a highway is obstructed, entering land to save goods from water or fire, and running through another's close to escape an assailant.
    - *Mouse's Case* established that one may sacrifice another's personal property to save lives in a tempest.
- The declaration sufficiently alleges that the servant was acting within the scope of his employment.
    - The phrases "wilfully and designedly" and "negligently, carelessly, and wrongfully" are applied to the defendant acting through the servant, not to the servant acting independently.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes the **necessity doctrine** as a defense to trespass, allowing temporary, non-damaging entry onto another's property when required to prevent greater harm, especially to life. It connects to *Berg v. Wiley* by showing limits on property owners' rights to exclude, though *Berg* focused on landlord-tenant self-help and this case on an emergency. It also contrasts with cases like *Pile v. Pedrick* and *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands* which emphasize strict property rights and the right to exclude, by introducing a significant exception.
2. The opinion doesn't explicitly state whether the plaintiff would have been liable for any damage caused to the dock by the mooring, only that the mooring itself was not a trespass. This leaves open the question of whether the property owner could seek compensation for any actual damage incurred.

**Takeaway:** The right to exclude is not absolute and can be limited by the doctrine of necessity, particularly when human life is at stake.

## Olwell v. Nye & Nissen Co. (Washington Supreme Court, 1946)

**Facts**
- On May 6, 1940, E. L. Olwell sold his interest in Puget Sound Egg Packers to Nye & Nissen Company.
- The agreement stipulated that Olwell would retain full ownership of an "Eggsact" egg-washing machine, and Nye & Nissen would make it available for delivery by June 15, 1940.
- Olwell arranged for the machine to be stored in a space adjacent to Nye & Nissen's premises.
- On May 31, 1941, Nye & Nissen's treasurer, without Olwell's knowledge or consent, removed the machine from storage and began using it.
- For three years, Nye & Nissen used the machine approximately one day a week in its business.
- In January or February 1945, Olwell discovered the unauthorized use.
- Olwell offered to sell the machine to Nye & Nissen for $600, but Nye & Nissen counter-offered $50, which Olwell refused.
- Approximately one month later, Olwell sued Nye & Nissen.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court entered judgment for Olwell for $1,560 ($10 per week for 156 weeks).
- Nye & Nissen appealed.

**Issue**
- Can an owner recover the profits a tortfeasor gained from the unauthorized use of the owner's chattel, even if the owner suffered no direct loss?

**Holding**
- Olwell (plaintiff) wins. An owner can recover the profits a tortfeasor gained from the unauthorized use of the owner's chattel, as restitution for unjust enrichment. The judgment is modified to reflect the amount prayed for. Affirmed as modified.

**Rules**
- **Waiver of Tort and Suit in Assumpsit.** When a defendant tortfeasor has benefited by their wrong, the plaintiff may elect to "waive the tort" and bring an action in assumpsit for restitution.
- **Unjust Enrichment.** An action for unjust enrichment arises out of a duty imposed by law upon the defendant to repay an unjust and unmerited enrichment.
- **Benefit (Restatement of Restitution § 1(b)).** A person confers a benefit upon another if they give possession or an interest in property, perform services, satisfy a debt, or in any way add to the other's security or advantage, including saving the other from expense or loss.
- **Essence of Property.** The very essence of the nature of property is the right to its exclusive use.
- **Measure of Restitution for Tortious Conduct.** If a defendant was consciously tortious in acquiring a benefit, they are deprived of any profit derived from their subsequent dealing with it.
- **Damages Limit.** Recovery cannot exceed the amount prayed for in the complaint.

**Reasoning**
- Nye & Nissen's use of the machine saved it labor costs, which constitutes a benefit under the Restatement of Restitution.
- The unauthorized use of Olwell's machine was a wrongful invasion of his property right to exclusive use, which is a compensable loss, even if Olwell had no immediate use for the machine.
- To hold otherwise would undermine all property rights.
- Olwell had an election of remedies: he could sue in tort for damages or waive the tort and sue in assumpsit for restitution based on implied contract.
- By electing to sue in assumpsit, Olwell is entitled to restitution, which aims to restore to the plaintiff something to which they are entitled or cause the defendant to pay for the benefit received.
- Since Nye & Nissen was tortious in acquiring the benefit (using the machine without permission), it must be deprived of the profit derived from that use.
- The trial court's calculation of $10 per week was based on the savings Nye & Nissen realized, which is an appropriate measure of restitution.
- However, the judgment must be reduced to the amount prayed for in the complaint ($25 per month for 36 months, totaling $900).

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the concept of "waiving the tort and suing in assumpsit," allowing a plaintiff to recover the defendant's unjust enrichment rather than just their own damages. It connects to the idea that property rights include the right to exclusive use, and any unauthorized use, even if it causes no direct harm to the owner, can be a basis for recovery. This differs from typical tort damages, which focus on the plaintiff's loss. It shows a different way to value the "use" stick in the bundle of rights.
2. What if the defendant's use of the chattel actually improved it or maintained it in a way the owner would not have? Would the owner still be entitled to the full benefit derived by the defendant?

**Takeaway:** An owner can recover the profits a tortfeasor gained from the unauthorized use of their property, even without proving direct loss, based on the principle of unjust enrichment and the fundamental right to exclusive use.

## Pile v. Pedrick (Supreme Court of Pennsylvania, 1895)

**Facts**
- Defendants intended to build a factory wall entirely on their own land.
- Defendants hired a district surveyor to locate their property line.
- The surveyor inaccurately located the line.
- Defendants built a foundation wall that encroached on plaintiffs' land by one and three-eighths inches for about 50 feet. The brick wall above the foundation was entirely on defendants' land.
- Plaintiffs refused defendants' offer to make the wall a party wall and allow windows to remain.
- Plaintiffs insisted that the encroachment be removed.
- Defendants sought permission to enter plaintiffs' land to chip off the projecting stones, offering to pay for inconvenience, but plaintiffs refused.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs filed a bill in equity to compel removal of the wall.
- The case was referred to a master, who found the encroachment existed.
- The court referred the case back to the master to determine if the encroachment could be rectified.
- The master reported no practical method for rectification.
- The court entered a decree allowing defendants to remove the encroaching portion of the wall from their own side, without entering plaintiffs' land, and divided costs.
- Both plaintiffs and defendants appealed the decree.
- The Supreme Court of Pennsylvania affirmed the lower court's decree with a modification to the timeframe for removal.

**Issue**
Can a court compel a landowner to remove an encroaching foundation wall, even if the encroachment is small and unintentional, and the only way to remove it is to rebuild the entire wall from the landowner's side?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Pile) wins. A court can compel a landowner to remove an encroaching foundation wall, even if the encroachment is small and unintentional, and the only way to remove it is to rebuild the entire wall from the landowner's side, because the encroaching party has no right to occupy land that does not belong to them. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Trespass.** An unintentional intrusion onto another's land constitutes a trespass.
- **Right to Exclude.** Landowners have the right to exclude others from their property.
- **Equity and Costs.** Costs in equity are not automatic and are within the chancellor's discretion, to be given or withheld as equity and good conscience require.

**Reasoning**
- The wall was not a party wall because it was not intended to be and was built by defendants on what they believed was their own land.
- The foundation stones projecting onto plaintiffs' land constitute an unintentional intrusion and a trespass.
- Defendants have no legal or equitable right to occupy land that does not belong to them.
- Since plaintiffs refused permission for defendants to enter their land to chip off the stones, the only remaining option to remove the encroachment is for defendants to take down and rebuild the entire wall from their own side.
- The court's division of costs was within the chancellor's discretion, especially given the hardship to the defendants and the circumstances of the litigation.
- The decree was modified to allow one year for removal to avoid further controversy.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case strongly affirms the **right to exclude** as a fundamental stick in the bundle of property rights. It connects to cases like *Jacque v. Steenberg Homes, Inc.* (not on the list) which emphasize the importance of the right to exclude, even for minor trespasses. The court prioritizes the landowner's absolute right to their property over the hardship imposed on the encroaching party, contrasting with approaches that might consider a balancing of equities for minor encroachments, as seen in *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands*.
2. The court's refusal to allow defendants to enter plaintiffs' land to chip off the stones, even with an offer to pay for damages, highlights the absolute nature of the right to exclude in this jurisdiction. What if the cost of removal was astronomically high compared to the value of the encroached land? Would the court still order removal, or would it consider damages as a more equitable remedy?

**Takeaway:** Even a small, unintentional encroachment on another's land can compel the encroaching party to remove the entire structure if the landowner insists on their right to exclude and refuses access for less drastic remedies.

## Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands (Colorado Supreme Court, 1951)

**Facts**
- Rylands and Reid (plaintiffs) owned land with a residence, garage, and rental cottages.
- Golden Press, Inc. (defendant) constructed a business building on its adjacent property.
- During construction, the defendant's foundation and footings extended 2 to 3.5 inches onto the plaintiffs' land.
- The defendant also trespassed on the plaintiffs' property by dropping an I-beam on their garage, destroying a flower bed and fence, disturbing a driveway, and walking and digging on their land.
- Plaintiffs complained to the defendant's representatives about the trespass and the building being over the line during construction.
- The defendant's superintendent, Ernst, stated they had their own surveyor and were on the line, but later the defendant's surveyor told them to move over.
- The defendant's witness Argo testified they moved the forms for the footing to be on their side after the plaintiffs complained.
- Plaintiffs did not seek an injunction until after the foundation and upper wall were completed.
- Plaintiffs' surveyor, Coberly, conducted a survey just before trial, long after the wall was completed, which showed the encroachment.
- The wall above the foundation does not project over the property line; only the footings, 7 to 9 feet below ground, encroach.
- The encroachment does not interfere with the plaintiffs' current use of the property as a driveway and iris bed.
- Plaintiffs refused the defendant permission to enter their property to chip off the encroaching footings and demanded removal from the defendant's side, even if it meant tearing down the wall.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs sued for an injunction to remove the footings and stop trespassing, and for $1750 in damages and exemplary damages.
- The case was tried to a jury on the issue of damages for trespass, with the court reserving the injunction issue.
- The jury returned a verdict for the defendant on damages.
- The trial court found the encroachment as alleged and granted a mandatory injunction requiring removal of the footings and directing the defendant to manage parking to prevent trespass.
- The trial court set aside the jury's verdict and granted a new trial on damages.
- Defendant appealed the injunction and the setting aside of the jury verdict.

**Issue**
Can a mandatory injunction be issued to compel the removal of a slight, unintentional encroachment when the cost of removal is disproportionate to the damage caused, and the plaintiff's use of the property is not significantly affected?

**Holding**
Defendant (appellant) wins. A mandatory injunction for a slight, unintentional encroachment that causes minimal damage and does not affect the plaintiff's use, where the cost of removal is unconscionable, may be denied, and the plaintiff relegated to damages. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Mandatory Injunction for Encroachment.** Ordinarily, a mandatory injunction will issue to compel removal of encroaching structures, but it is not to be issued as a matter of course.
- **Equitable Remedies.** On appeal to the court for an equitable remedy, the court must consider the peculiar equities of the case.
- **Willful Encroachment.** Where the encroachment is deliberate and constitutes a willful and intentional taking of another’s land, equity may well require its restoration regardless of the expense of removal as compared with damage suffered therefrom.
- **Good Faith Encroachment.** Where the encroachment was in good faith, the court should weigh the circumstances so that it shall not act oppressively.
- **Relative Hardship.** While the mere balance of convenience is not the proper test, yet relative hardship may properly be considered, and the court should not become a party to extortion.
- **Denial of Mandatory Injunction.** Where defendant’s encroachment is unintentional and slight, plaintiff’s use not affected and his damage small and fairly compensable, while the cost of removal is so great as to cause grave hardship or otherwise make its removal unconscionable, mandatory injunction may properly be denied and plaintiff relegated to compensation in damages.
- **Clarity of Judgment.** A judgment must be definite and certain in itself, fixing clearly the rights and liabilities of the respective parties and being capable of performance.

**Reasoning**
- The trial court's order setting aside the jury verdict for a new trial on damages was discretionary and not a final judgment, so it cannot be reviewed on appeal.
- The portion of the injunction regarding directing drivers and removing signs is too vague and uncertain to be enforceable.
- The court found no evidence challenging the defendant's good faith in locating the footings, despite plaintiffs' complaints during construction.
    - There is a presumption that men act in good faith and intend to do what they have the right to do.
    - Conflicting survey information during construction and the defendant's adjustment of forms suggest an attempt to comply.
- The encroachment is very slight (2 to 3.5 inches) and entirely below ground (7 to 9 feet deep).
- The encroachment does not interfere with the plaintiffs' current use of the property.
- The potential future damage (if plaintiffs build a basement to the line) is minimal compared to the cost of removal.
    - The value of the encroached strip is very small (estimated at $55 for a 3.5-inch strip along the entire length).
- The expense and hardship of removing the footings (potentially requiring tearing down the wall) would be unconscionable compared to the minimal advantage gained by the plaintiffs.
- Plaintiffs delayed seeking an injunction until after construction was complete, despite continuous complaints about trespass.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the **relative hardship doctrine** (also known as the **balancing of equities** or **balancing of conveniences**) in property law, particularly concerning encroachments. It contrasts with cases like *Pile v. Pedrick* where a strict property rule (requiring removal regardless of cost) was applied. *Golden Press* shows a court's willingness to apply a liability rule (damages instead of injunction) when the cost of removal is disproportionate to the harm, and the encroachment is unintentional. This case is relevant to the "sticks in the bundle" concept, specifically the right to exclude, and how that right can be limited by equitable considerations.
2. What if the plaintiffs had sought an injunction immediately upon discovering the encroachment, before the building was completed? Would the outcome have been different regarding the "good faith" and "relative hardship" analysis?

**Takeaway:** Courts may deny a mandatory injunction for a minor, unintentional encroachment and award damages instead, especially when the cost of removal is disproportionately high compared to the harm suffered.

## eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C. (Supreme Court of the United States, 2006)

**Facts**
- MercExchange holds patents, including a business method patent for an electronic market.
- MercExchange sought to license its patent to eBay and Half.com, but they did not reach an agreement.
- MercExchange sued eBay and Half.com for patent infringement.
- A jury found MercExchange's patent valid, infringed by eBay and Half.com, and awarded damages.

**Procedural History**
- The District Court denied MercExchange's motion for a permanent injunction.
- The Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit reversed, applying a general rule that permanent injunctions issue against patent infringement absent exceptional circumstances.
- The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

**Issue**
- Does the traditional four-factor test for permanent injunctions apply to disputes arising under the Patent Act, or is there a special rule for patent cases?

**Holding**
- MercExchange (plaintiff) wins in part. The traditional four-factor test for permanent injunctions applies to patent disputes. The District Court must apply this framework. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Permanent Injunction Test.** A plaintiff seeking a permanent injunction must demonstrate: (1) irreparable injury; (2) inadequacy of legal remedies; (3) that the balance of hardships favors an equitable remedy; and (4) that the public interest would not be disserved.
- **Equitable Discretion.** The decision to grant or deny permanent injunctive relief is an act of equitable discretion by the district court, reviewable for abuse of discretion.
- **Patent Act and Injunctions.** The Patent Act provides that injunctions "may" issue "in accordance with the principles of equity."
- Rejected: **General Rule for Patent Injunctions.** A general rule that courts will issue permanent injunctions against patent infringement absent exceptional circumstances is not appropriate.
- Rejected: **Categorical Denial of Injunctions.** A categorical rule that a patent holder's willingness to license or lack of commercial activity is sufficient to deny irreparable harm is not consistent with traditional equitable principles.

**Reasoning**
- The Court has long recognized that a major departure from the long tradition of equity practice should not be lightly implied.
- Nothing in the Patent Act indicates Congress intended to depart from traditional equitable principles for injunctions.
- The Patent Act states that injunctions "may" issue "in accordance with the principles of equity."
- The creation of a right (like the right to exclude in a patent) is distinct from the provision of remedies for its violation.
- This approach is consistent with how injunctions are treated under the Copyright Act, where courts have rejected automatic injunctions.
- The District Court erred by adopting categorical principles suggesting injunctive relief could not issue in a broad swath of cases, such as when a plaintiff is willing to license or lacks commercial activity.
- The Court of Appeals erred by adopting a "general rule" unique to patent disputes that a permanent injunction will issue once infringement and validity are adjudged.
- Neither court correctly applied the traditional four-factor framework.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence (Chief Justice Roberts)**
- Agrees with the Court's holding that the four-factor test applies.
- Notes that historically, courts have granted injunctive relief in the vast majority of patent cases, which is not surprising given the difficulty of protecting a right to exclude through monetary remedies.
- This historical practice does not entitle a patentee to an automatic injunction but provides context for applying the four-factor test.

**Concurrence (Justice Kennedy)**
- Agrees with the Court's holding that the four-factor test applies without categorical rules.
- Notes that the traditional practice of issuing injunctions against patent infringers often reflected the application of the four-factor test in prevalent contexts.
- Emphasizes that trial courts should consider the changing nature of patent enforcement, especially with firms using patents primarily for licensing fees rather than production.
- In such cases, an injunction might be used as undue leverage, and legal damages might be sufficient, or the public interest might be disserved.
- The potential vagueness and suspect validity of some business method patents may also affect the four-factor calculus.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important because it clarifies that the traditional equitable principles for injunctions apply to patent law, rejecting a special rule for patent cases. It connects to the broader theme of **entitlement protection** and the choice between property rules (injunctions) and liability rules (damages), as discussed in cases like *Pile v. Pedrick* and *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands*. While *Pile* and *Golden Press* dealt with physical encroachments and the default remedy, *eBay* addresses the standard for injunctive relief in intellectual property, emphasizing judicial discretion rather than automatic injunctions. It also highlights the tension between the **right to exclude** (a core property stick) and the practical implications of enforcing that right, especially in new economic contexts.
2. The case leaves open the question of how the four-factor test should be applied in the context of "patent trolls" or non-practicing entities, where the patent holder's primary business is licensing rather than manufacturing.

**Takeaway:** The availability of injunctive relief in patent cases is governed by traditional equitable principles, requiring a case-by-case application of a four-factor test rather than a categorical rule.

## Producers Lumber & Supply Co. v. Olney Building Co. (Texas Courts of Civil Appeals, 1960)

**Facts**
- Prior to November 1, 1956, Elliott Construction Co., Inc. owned Lot 8.
- On November 1, 1956, H. P. Orts, as Assistant Secretary and Agent and Attorney in Fact for Elliott Construction Co., Inc., executed a warranty deed conveying Lot 8 to Producers Lumber & Supply Co. for $1,428. This deed was recorded.
- Producers Lumber & Supply Co. (appellant) purchased Lot 8 with the intention for its general manager, George R. Montgomery, and his wife to build a home there.
- Montgomery and his wife had the lot graded and planted trees and grass on it.
- On or about February 27, 1958, Orts and Elliott decided to construct nine dwellings, including one on Lot 8.
- Orts called A. L. Burden, secretary-treasurer of Olney Building Company (appellee), and Burden, after consulting a map, assured Orts that Lot 8 had not been sold.
- Orts inspected Lot 8 and noticed the planted trees, thinking it was nice of someone to plant them.
- Construction of the nine houses began shortly thereafter.
- On April 1, 1958, Olney Building Company ordered a Title Binder for all nine lots from Stewart Title Company for interim financing.
- On April 14, 1958, Orts learned from the Title Company that Lot 8 had been sold to Producers Lumber & Supply Co.
- The dwelling on Lot 8 was almost completed when the discovery was made.
- Orts notified Montgomery of the circumstances, which was the first notice to Producers Lumber & Supply Co. that construction had begun on its lot.
- The house was constructed without the knowledge or consent of Producers Lumber & Supply Co. and contrary to Montgomery's plans for his own home.
- Orts began negotiations with Montgomery to reach a settlement, telling Montgomery that Montgomery had him "at his mercy."
- Various offers and counter-offers were made, but no settlement was reached.
- On April 22, 1958, Orts broke off negotiations and sent his construction superintendent with a large crew and heavy equipment to Lot 8.
- Orts demolished the dwelling constructed on Lot 8, leaving only a heap of building material and debris.
- The demolition involved a bulldozer, a dozen or more men, and later a crane with a drop hammer, air hammers, and torches to cut steel for the foundation.
- Orts testified that $2,768 "went down the drain" in the destruction.
- The parties stipulated that the dwelling had a value of $5,000 and had enhanced the value of Lot 8 by $5,000.

**Procedural History**
- Producers Lumber & Supply Co. sued Olney Building Co. for damages.
- A jury found that Orts acted in good faith in erecting the building (Question No. 1: Yes), that the cost to restore the lot was $600 (Question No. 2: $600), that Orts acted maliciously in removing the building (Question No. 3: Yes), and awarded $300 in exemplary damages (Question No. 4: $300).
- The trial court disregarded the jury's answers to Questions Nos. 3 and 4 (malice and exemplary damages) and rendered judgment for Producers Lumber & Supply Co. for $600.
- Producers Lumber & Supply Co. appealed, contending the judgment should have been $5,900.

**Issue**
Can a builder who mistakenly constructs a dwelling on another's land, and then maliciously demolishes it without the landowner's consent, avoid paying the landowner for the value of the destroyed dwelling?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Producers Lumber & Supply Co.) wins. A builder who mistakenly constructs a dwelling on another's land, and then maliciously demolishes it without the landowner's consent, commits waste and must pay the landowner for the value of the destroyed dwelling. Reversed and amended.

**Rules**
- **Fixtures.** Where a person erects a building upon the land of another without his knowledge and consent, the building becomes a fixture and belongs to the owner of the land, and the builder is without remedy.
- **Good Faith Improver.** It is only where a person places permanent improvements upon land belonging to another in a good faith belief that he is the owner of the land, that he has any remedy at all.
- **Good Faith Improver's Remedy.** A court of equity may grant relief to a good faith improver, such as permitting removal if possible without great injury, or allowing recovery for the enhanced value of the land, or ordering sale and division of proceeds, or granting a lien for the enhanced value.
- **Duty to Investigate Records.** A person contemplating the improvement of land is under a duty to investigate the records before commencing work, as recorded deeds impart notice of outstanding claims.
- **Waste.** An improver is not authorized to go upon the land of another, without his knowledge and consent, and demolish improvements he has mistakenly placed thereon; if he does so, he commits waste and can be required to pay the landowner for such waste.
- **Unclean Hands Doctrine.** A party who resorts to self-help and takes the law into their own hands, causing great destruction of property before a court can determine rights, cannot come into court with "unclean hands" to seek equitable relief like reimbursement for enhanced value.

**Reasoning**
- The jury's findings of malice and exemplary damages were supported by the evidence and the trial court erred in setting them aside.
- Orts, as president of Olney Building Co., knew the deed conveying Lot 8 to Producers Lumber & Supply Co. was on record, even if he had forgotten signing it.
- Even if the jury's finding of good faith in building was sufficient, Olney Building Co. cannot prevail on a plea of good faith improvement due to the malicious destruction of the dwelling.
- When Orts demolished the dwelling without the appellant's knowledge or consent, he committed waste.
- The dwelling, once constructed, belonged to the landowner (Producers Lumber & Supply Co.) under the law of fixtures.
- By resorting to self-help and destroying the property, Olney Building Co. cannot now seek equitable reimbursement for the enhanced value of the lot.
- Producers Lumber & Supply Co. is suing for damages for willful destruction of its property, not seeking equitable relief, so the "unclean hands" doctrine applies to Olney Building Co.
- Producers Lumber & Supply Co. was not required to pay Olney Building Co. anything until a court determined the good faith issue, and its failure to settle on Orts' terms does not make its hands unclean.

**Dissent (Barrow)**
- The majority incorrectly allows Producers Lumber & Supply Co. to recover $5,000 for a house it did not build and had no investment in.
- The jury found Olney Building Co. built the house in good faith and was not negligent, and this finding should stand.
- Texas law allows a good faith improver to obtain relief, and this right is not lost by removing the improvements.
- The property "demolished" belonged to Olney Building Co., not Producers Lumber & Supply Co., because Olney was a good faith improver.
- Producers Lumber & Supply Co. would have had to pay Olney Building Co. for the enhancement in value if the house had remained.
- Olney Building Co. sought to make an amicable settlement, offering to buy the lot, trade lots, or sell the incomplete house, but Producers Lumber & Supply Co. attempted to drive an "unconscionable bargain."
- The dissent argues that the majority's decision imposes additional punitive damages beyond what the jury awarded and makes a "donation" to Producers Lumber & Supply Co.
- Equity should prevent unjust enrichment, and Producers Lumber & Supply Co. suffered no injury regarding the house itself.

**NOTES:**
1. This case highlights the tension between the **fixtures** doctrine (improvements become part of the land) and the **good faith improver** doctrine (equity may provide relief to those who mistakenly improve another's land). It connects to **Pile v. Pedrick** and **Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands** by showing how courts deal with mistaken improvements, but here, the improver's self-help complicates the equitable solution. The court emphasizes the importance of judicial process over self-help, similar to **Berg v. Wiley** in the landlord-tenant context, where a landlord's self-help eviction was condemned.
2. The case raises the question of what constitutes "good faith" when a deed is recorded. The jury found good faith despite the recorded deed, but the majority opinion casts doubt on this, suggesting a duty to investigate records. This creates a potential tension regarding the standard for good faith.

**Takeaway:** A mistaken improver who resorts to self-help and destroys the improvement on another's land forfeits any equitable claim for reimbursement and is liable for waste.

## Edwards v. Allouez Mining Co. (Michigan Supreme Court, 1878)

**Facts**
- In 1874, Allouez Mining Company (defendant) built a stamp mill on Hill Creek at a cost of $60,000.
- The mill's operations caused large quantities of sand to be carried down the creek and deposited on downstream bottom lands.
- In 1875, Edwards (complainant) purchased a piece of land downstream from the mill, through which the creek ran, and upon which sand was being deposited.
- Edwards did not purchase the land for use or occupation, but for speculation, intending to force the defendant to buy it at a large advance.
- Edwards offered to sell the land to Allouez Mining Co. soon after purchase, valuing it at three to five times his purchase price.
- Allouez Mining Co. declined to purchase the land.
- Edwards filed a bill seeking an injunction to stop the defendant from depositing sand on his land and polluting the stream.

**Procedural History**
- The circuit court refused the injunction but ordered a reference to a jury for an assessment of damages.
- Complainant appealed.

**Issue**
Can a landowner obtain an injunction against a mining company for depositing sand on his land if he purchased the land solely for speculative purposes, intending to force the company to buy it?

**Holding**
Defendant (Allouez Mining Co.) wins. An injunction will not be granted when the complainant purchased the land for speculative purposes, and the injury can be adequately compensated by damages. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Injunctions are discretionary.** The allowance of an injunction is discretionary and not a matter of right.
- **Irreparable mischief.** Injunctions are intended to prevent irreparable mischief, where the consequences could not be adequately compensated by damages.
- **Balancing inconveniences.** A court considering an injunction will balance the inconveniences of granting or denying the writ.
- **Good faith.** The petitioner for an injunction must act in good faith.
- **Motive of the complainant.** If a complainant demands more than strict legal rights, their motives may become important.
- **Adequate legal remedy.** If the common law provides an adequate remedy in damages, equity will generally not intervene with an injunction.

**Reasoning**
- The defendant's operations constitute an appropriation of the complainant's property, for which the complainant is entitled to legal redress.
- However, an injunction is a powerful remedy that should not be lightly ordered, especially when it could force the defendant to purchase the complainant's land at an inflated price or sacrifice their valuable property.
- The purpose of an injunction is to prevent irreparable injury, which is not present here.
    - Irreparable injury typically involves destroying the comfortable occupation of a homestead, breaking up a business, or situations where damages are impossible to measure.
    - The land in question was not purchased for a homestead or business, but for speculation.
- The complainant bought the land with the preconceived purpose of forcing a sale to the defendant.
    - While motives generally don't affect lawful actions, they become relevant when seeking the "gracious ear of equity" for a special remedy beyond strict legal rights.
    - The complainant "invited an injury" by purchasing the land for this purpose.
- A legal award of damages would be a suitable and just redress for land bought for speculation, as it furthers the purpose of the purchase.
- The defendant is not alleged to be irresponsible, so damages are likely collectible.
- The elements of irreparable injury are entirely absent from this case.

**Dissent (Campbell, C. J.)**
- The defendant is continuously invading the complainant's freehold and rights in the water without color of right.
- Rights to equitable relief, when it is the only adequate remedy, are as absolute as rights to legal relief.
- No legal remedy is adequate for such a grievance because it cannot secure the complainant the use of his own property.
- The complainant's speculative motives should not lessen his claims to protection; it is a dangerous principle to hold that a civil wrong can be lessened by the motives of the injured party.
- Property is entitled to protection regardless of the owner's motives, otherwise private interests would be subject to judicial discretion.
- The court should have granted a perpetual injunction.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the limits of injunctive relief in property disputes, particularly when the plaintiff's motives are questionable. It connects to the idea of "coming to the nuisance" and the balancing of equities, which is also seen in cases like *Pile v. Pedrick* (where an injunction was granted despite high cost to defendant) and *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands* (where an injunction was denied due to minimal damage and high cost). The court's focus on the plaintiff's motive and the adequacy of damages contrasts with a strict property rule approach that would prioritize the owner's right to exclude, as advocated by the dissent.
2. The case raises the question of whether a property owner's subjective intent or motive in acquiring property should influence the remedies available for trespass or nuisance. Does this open the door for courts to judge the "worthiness" of property owners?

**Takeaway:** Courts may deny injunctive relief for property invasions if the plaintiff's primary motive for acquiring the property was speculative and damages can adequately compensate the injury, balancing the equities between the parties.

## Pocono Springs Civic Association v. MacKenzie (Superior Court of Pennsylvania, 1995)

**Facts**
- On October 14, 1969, the MacKenzies purchased a vacant lot in Pocono Springs Development.
- In 1987, the MacKenzies decided to sell their vacant lot.
- An offer to purchase the lot was conditioned on its suitability for an on-lot sewage system.
- An inspection determined the lot had inadequate soil for proper percolation, and the sale was lost.
- The MacKenzies attempted to turn the lot over to the Pocono Springs Civic Association (Association), but the Association declined.
- The MacKenzies tried to persuade the Association to accept the lot as a gift for a park, but the Association again declined.
- In 1986, the MacKenzies stopped paying real estate taxes on the lot.
- In 1988, the Wayne County Tax Claim Bureau offered the property for sale due to delinquent taxes, but there were no purchasers.
- In 1990, the lot was again offered for sale by the Tax Claim Bureau, but it was not sold and was placed on its "repository" list.
- The MacKenzies signed a notarized statement expressing their desire to abandon the lot and mailed it to "all interested parties."
- The MacKenzies stopped accepting mail regarding the property and had not visited the lot or used the development's services since 1986.
- The Association sued the MacKenzies for unpaid association fees.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court granted summary judgment in favor of the Association, finding the MacKenzies had not abandoned their property and were still obligated to pay association fees.
- The MacKenzies appealed.

**Issue**
- Can owners of real property held in fee simple abandon that property to avoid paying association fees?

**Holding**
- The Association wins. Owners of real property held in fee simple cannot abandon their property under Pennsylvania law. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Abandonment of Property.** Abandoned property is that to which an owner has voluntarily relinquished all right, title, claim, and possession with the intention of terminating ownership, without vesting it in any other person, and with the intention of not reclaiming further possession or resuming ownership, possession, or enjoyment.
- **Abandonment of Real Property.** Under Pennsylvania law, real property owned in fee simple with perfect title cannot be abandoned.
- **Rejected: Intent to Abandon.** An owner's intent to abandon real property is irrelevant if the law states that such property cannot be abandoned.
- **Rejected: Non-payment of Taxes/Non-use.** Neither refusal to pay taxes nor non-use of real property constitutes abandonment of perfect title.

**Reasoning**
- The MacKenzies still hold perfect title to the lot, as evidenced by their recorded deed, and have not relinquished their rights, title, claim, and possession.
- Pennsylvania law explicitly states that perfect title to real property cannot be abandoned.
    - The doctrine of abandonment applies only to imperfect titles or inchoate rights, not to perfect titles.
    - No legal authority in Pennsylvania supports the abandonment of real property held in fee simple.
- The MacKenzies' actions, such as attempting to give the lot away, ceasing tax payments, and signing a statement of abandonment, do not legally constitute abandonment of fee simple real property.
- The MacKenzies' intent to abandon is irrelevant because the law dictates that real property cannot be abandoned, leaving no factual issue for a jury.
- The MacKenzies are bound by the covenant in their deed to pay association fees as long as they retain title.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes a bright-line rule that fee simple ownership of real property cannot be abandoned, contrasting with the abandonment of personal property or lesser interests like easements (as seen in cases involving railway lines). It reinforces the idea of the permanence of fee simple title and the obligations that come with it. This case could be compared to cases like *Eyerman v. Mercantile Trust Co.* where the court considered limitations on an owner's right to destroy property, but here the owners are trying to shed obligations rather than destroy the property.
2. What if the property became a public nuisance or environmental hazard? Would the state have any mechanism to force the MacKenzies to take action or to take title itself, even if they cannot "abandon" it?

**Takeaway:** Under Pennsylvania law, an owner cannot abandon real property held in fee simple, regardless of their intent or efforts to relinquish it, and thus remains responsible for associated obligations.

## Eyerman v. Mercantile Trust Co. (Missouri Court of Appeals, 1975)

**Facts**
- Louise Woodruff Johnston owned a house at #4 Kingsbury Place in St. Louis.
- Johnston died on January 14, 1973.
- Johnston's will directed her executor to raze her home at #4 Kingsbury Place, sell the land, and transfer the proceeds to the residue of her estate.
- Kingsbury Place is a "private place" established in 1902 by a trust indenture, which requires it to be maintained as desirable private residences of the highest class.
- The subdivision consists of handsome, spacious homes, with one existing vacant lot.
- The St. Louis Commission on Landmarks and Urban Design designated Kingsbury Place as a landmark.
- Neighboring property owners and trustees for the Kingsbury Place Subdivision filed a petition seeking an injunction to prevent the demolition.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court dissolved a temporary restraining order and found all issues against the plaintiffs.
- Plaintiffs appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a testamentary direction to destroy a valuable house be enjoined as contrary to public policy when it causes significant loss to the estate, harms the community, and benefits no one?

**Holding**
- Plaintiffs (neighboring property owners and subdivision trustees) win. A testamentary direction to destroy a valuable house is void as against public policy when it causes significant loss to the estate, harms the community, and benefits no one. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Standing for Public Policy Issues.** Individuals have standing to raise public policy issues if they allege a legally protectible interest and will be perceptibly harmed by the challenged action.
- **Testamentary Freedom.** While living, a person may manage, use, or dispose of their property with fewer restraints than a decedent by will.
- **State Power over Inheritance.** The state has inherent sovereign power to determine what becomes of a person's property upon death and can impose conditions on the right to inherit or devise.
- **Public Policy.** Public policy conflicts with the morals of the time and contravenes an established interest of society, or has a mischievous tendency injurious to the interests of the state.
- **Judicial Determination of Public Policy.** In cases of first impression without guiding statutes, judicial decisions, or constitutional provisions, a judicial determination of public policy must be "so plainly right as to be supported by the general will."
- **Capricious Destruction by Will.** It is against public policy to allow an executor to exercise a power to destroy property if the purpose is merely capricious and confers no other interest in the property.
- **Rejected: Absolute Right to Untrammeled Use.** The general principle of an owner's "free and untrammeled" use of real property is narrowed by competing interests of the community and other owners.

**Reasoning**
- Plaintiffs have standing because they pleaded and proved facts showing a personal, legally protectible interest.
    - Demolition would result in an unwarranted loss to the estate ($39,350 loss from a $40,000 asset).
    - Demolition would depreciate adjoining property values by an estimated $10,000.
    - The house is in an area of high architectural significance, designated a landmark.
    - Demolition would create a "missing front tooth" effect, detracting from the urban design and potentially leading to detrimental uses.
    - There is a pressing need for dwelling units in St. Louis, as shown by census figures.
- The testamentary condition for destruction is capricious and serves no good purpose.
    - No reason for the eccentric condition is suggested by the will or record.
    - The destruction harms neighbors, detrimentally affects the community, causes monetary loss to the estate, and benefits no one.
- The state's power over inheritance allows it to impose conditions on testamentary dispositions.
    - While a living person has broad rights, a will's directions are subject to public policy.
    - Courts can intervene when a condition in a will is against the public good.
- Precedent supports invalidating capricious destructive conditions in wills.
    - English cases (Egerton v. Brownlow, M’Caig’s Trustees, Brown v. Burdett) and the Restatement (Second) of Trusts illustrate that directions for wasteful destruction are void.
    - Colonial Trust Co. v. Brown invalidated restrictions on property use that were against public policy and harmful to beneficiaries and public welfare.
- Enjoining the destruction will not defeat the testatrix's trust purposes, as the proceeds from the sale of the property will still pass to the residual estate.

**Dissent (Clemens)**
- The majority's opinion is emotional and assumes psychic knowledge of the testatrix's motives, characterizing her disposition as "capricious," "unwarranted," "senseless," and "eccentric" without evidence.
- The majority's decision confers a "benefit" on beneficiaries who have not litigated or protested.
- The public policy grounds are "wispy," vaguely pleaded, not in evidence, and sketchily briefed.
- The trust indenture does not bar razing, as it acknowledges vacant lots and empowers trustees to maintain them.
- The claim of nuisance is based on conjecture, not certainty, and there is no reason to presume a second vacant lot would be untended or that private police would cease patrolling.
- The court should not create its own public policy; public policy should be found in the Constitution, statutes, and judicial decisions, not the "varying opinions of laymen, lawyers, or judges."
- The law favors freedom in testamentary disposition and the free and untrammeled use of real property.
- Mrs. Johnston had the right to raze her house during her lifetime, and nothing precludes her right to order her executor to do so upon her death.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for illustrating the limits of **owner autonomy** and the **right to destroy** property, especially post-mortem. It connects to **Eyerman v. Mercantile Trust Co.** itself, as the case. It contrasts with cases like *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* where the court was hesitant to restrict a living person's control over their body parts, suggesting a distinction between living and dead owners. It also touches on the concept of **waste**, similar to how courts might intervene in cases of waste by a life tenant (though here it's a testamentary direction).
2. The dissent raises a valid point about the potential for judicial overreach in defining "public policy," especially when it's not clearly established by statute or prior case law. This raises the question of how much discretion courts should have to override a testator's wishes based on evolving societal values.

**Takeaway:** A testamentary direction to destroy valuable property can be voided as against public policy if it is capricious, causes significant economic and community harm, and benefits no one.

## ProCD Inc. v. Zeidenberg (United States Court of Appeals, Seventh Circuit, 1996)

**Facts**
- ProCD compiled a database of information from over 3,000 telephone directories, which cost over $10 million to create and maintain.
- ProCD sold this database on CD-ROMs, called SelectPhone, with a "shrinkwrap license" that limited use to noncommercial purposes. This license was printed in the manual, encoded on the CD-ROMs, and displayed on the user's screen every time the software ran.
- ProCD used price discrimination, selling the database at a low price for personal use and a higher price for commercial use.
- Matthew Zeidenberg bought a consumer package of SelectPhone in 1994.
- Zeidenberg ignored the license and formed Silken Mountain Web Services, Inc., to resell the information from the database on the Internet for a price lower than ProCD's commercial rate.
- Zeidenberg purchased two additional SelectPhone packages with updated databases and continued to make the information available online through his corporation.
- ProCD sued Zeidenberg seeking an injunction against further dissemination that exceeded the rights specified in the licenses.

**Procedural History**
- The district court held that the shrinkwrap licenses were not enforceable because they were not contracts and because federal law (Copyright Act § 301(a)) preempted their enforcement.
- ProCD appealed.

**Issue**
- Are shrinkwrap licenses enforceable contracts, and if so, are they preempted by federal copyright law?

**Holding**
- ProCD (plaintiff) wins. Shrinkwrap licenses are enforceable contracts and are not preempted by federal copyright law. Reversed and Remanded.

**Rules**
- **Contract Formation (UCC § 2-204(1)).** A contract for the sale of goods may be made in any manner sufficient to show agreement, including conduct by both parties which recognizes the existence of such a contract.
- **Acceptance of Goods (UCC § 2-606(1)(b)).** A buyer accepts goods when, after an opportunity to inspect, they fail to make an effective rejection.
- **Preemption (Copyright Act § 301(a)).** State law rights are preempted if they are "equivalent to any of the exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright" for works within the subject matter of copyright.
- **Rejected: Contract Formation (Hidden Terms).** A contract cannot include terms that were secret at the time of purchase.
- **Rejected: Preemption (Contractual Rights).** Rights created by contract are "equivalent to any of the exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright."

**Reasoning**
- **Contract Formation:**
    - The UCC permits contracts to be formed in ways other than simply paying the price and leaving the store.
    - A vendor, as master of the offer, can invite acceptance by conduct and propose limitations on that conduct.
    - ProCD proposed a contract that a buyer would accept by using the software after having an opportunity to read the license.
    - Zeidenberg accepted by using the software after the license was displayed on the screen and he had the opportunity to read it.
    - The ability to return the software if the terms are unacceptable is a common and efficient way of doing business, similar to insurance policies, airline tickets, and concert tickets.
    - The UCC does not require special prominence for ordinary terms in shrinkwrap licenses.
    - Competition among vendors, not judicial revision of contract terms, protects consumers in a market economy.
- **Copyright Preemption:**
    - The database is "within the subject matter of copyright" even if it is not sufficiently original to be copyrighted itself.
    - Rights created by contract are generally not "equivalent to any of the exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright" because contracts affect only their parties, not strangers.
    - Copyright is a right against the world, while contracts bind only the parties to the agreement.
    - Enforcing contracts about intellectual property, like trade secrets, does not withdraw information from the public domain.
    - Enforcement of the shrinkwrap license does not interfere with the attainment of national objectives under copyright law.
    - The contract between ProCD and Zeidenberg does not prevent anyone from copying or disseminating the underlying public domain information.
    - Licenses can facilitate distribution and serve procompetitive functions.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding how **freedom of contract** and **owner autonomy** can be used to protect intellectual property interests, even when those interests might not be fully protected by federal copyright law (as seen in *Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.* regarding the copyrightability of factual compilations). It highlights the tension between federal intellectual property law and state contract law.
2. This case raises the question of how far a vendor can go in imposing terms through "clickwrap" or "browsewrap" agreements, where acceptance might be less explicit than in ProCD's case.

**Takeaway:** Shrinkwrap licenses are generally enforceable contracts under the UCC and are not preempted by federal copyright law, allowing vendors to use contractual terms to protect their intellectual property and implement business strategies like price discrimination.

## Allen v. Hyatt Regency-Nashville Hotel (Tennessee Supreme Court, 1984)

**Facts**
- Hyatt Regency-Nashville Hotel operated a multi-story parking garage with a single entrance and exit, open to the public.
- On February 12, 1981, Edwin Allen drove his wife's 1981 automobile into the garage, took a ticket from a machine, drove to the fourth floor, parked, locked the car, and kept the ignition key.
- Allen left the garage and returned several hours later to find the car gone.
- Allen reported the theft to the attendant at the exit booth, who said the car did not exit there.
- Allen reported the theft to hotel security, who told him a lady had reported someone "messing with a car" earlier that day, but a guard investigating found nothing unusual.
- The car was never recovered.
- The parking ticket stated charges were for space only, the hotel assumed no responsibility for loss, cars were parked at the owner's risk, and parkers should lock their vehicles.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court found a bailment was created and held the hotel liable.
- The intermediate appellate court affirmed.
- The hotel appealed to the Tennessee Supreme Court.

**Issue**
- Is a commercial parking garage operator liable for the theft of a vehicle when the owner parks and locks it in the garage, keeps the keys, and receives a ticket from a machine?

**Holding**
- Plaintiff (Allen) wins. A bailment for hire was created, making the hotel liable for the theft. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Bailment for Hire.** A bailment for hire is created when personal property is delivered by one person (bailor) to another (bailee) for a specific purpose, with an express or implied contract that the property will be returned or accounted for.
- **Presumption of Negligence (T.C.A. § 24-5-111).** Upon proof of nondelivery of bailed property, a bailee is presumed negligent.
- **Rejected: Bailment requires physical delivery of keys.** The fact that an owner locks a vehicle and keeps the keys does not necessarily prevent the creation of a bailment.
- **Rejected: Exculpatory clauses on tickets.** Language on a parking ticket disclaiming liability is insufficient to exonerate a bailee if the customer is not shown to have read it or had it called to their attention.

**Reasoning**
- Tennessee courts generally analyze such cases using the concept of an implied bailment for hire.
- The garage was enclosed, indoor, and attended, with an attendant controlling the exit and security personnel patrolling.
- The operator of such a garage is expected to provide attendants and protection, and practically assumes control and custody by limiting access and requiring a ticket for exit.
- The attendant's statement that the car did not exit through his controlled gate, combined with no other exit, suggests inattention or absence from his post.
- The hotel made no effort to rebut the statutory presumption of negligence.
- The court declined to depart from prior decisions or place the risk of loss on the public in such commercial parking establishments.
- The facts are consistent with a bailment for hire, not merely a license to park.

**Dissent (Drowota, J.)**
- A bailment requires possession and control to pass from the bailor to the bailee, excluding the owner's possession and giving the bailee sole custody and control.
- In this "park and lock" situation, the owner parked, locked the car, and kept the keys, retaining control.
- The ticket was for fee computation, not vehicle identification, and the cashier's role was only to collect payment, not to identify or return a specific vehicle.
- The hotel could not move the car, did not know who owned it, or when it would be reclaimed, indicating a lack of sole custody and control.
- The circumstances of modern automated parking facilities differ from traditional bailment scenarios where attendants took keys and moved vehicles.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the concept of **bailment** as a property relationship, specifically a bailment for hire, and how courts adapt traditional legal concepts to modern commercial practices. It contrasts with cases like *Rhodes v. Pioneer Parking Lot, Inc.* (cited in the opinion), where an open, unattended lot did not create a bailment. The court here emphasizes the "realities of the situation" and the expectations of the parties, similar to how courts might consider custom or public policy in other property contexts.
2. The dissent raises a tension between applying traditional, rigid definitions of property concepts (like "delivery" for bailment) and adapting them to new technologies and consumer expectations. Is it better to stretch an old doctrine or create a new one (e.g., a duty of reasonable care, as suggested by *McGlynn v. Parking Authority of City of Newark*, cited in the opinion)?

**Takeaway:** A bailment for hire can be created in a commercial "park and lock" garage, even when the owner retains keys, if the operator exercises sufficient control over access and exit, implying an expectation of protection.

## Cowen v. Pressprich (New York Supreme Court, Appellate Term, 1922)

**Facts**
- Plaintiffs, stockbrokers, agreed to sell an Oregon Short Line Railroad bond to defendants, also stockbrokers.
- A third bond house mistakenly sent plaintiffs an Oregon and California Railroad bond instead.
- Plaintiffs, also by mistake, sent this Oregon and California bond to defendants via a 17-year-old messenger, Goldberg, along with a slip describing an Oregon Short Line bond.
- Goldberg dropped the envelope containing the bond and slip through a slot in defendants' delivery window.
- Goldberg waited about 1 to 1.5 minutes, told the person at the desk he would return for a check, and left.
- Within 15 seconds of the bond arriving, Mr. Quackenbush, a member of defendants' firm, opened the window, saw the bond was an Oregon and California bond (not the Oregon Short Line bond they expected), and yelled "Cowen."
- An unidentified boy, not Goldberg, stepped up, mumbled "All right," took the bond, and left.
- The bond was a bearer type, fully negotiable.
- Defendants refused to compensate plaintiffs for the loss of the bond.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs sued defendants for conversion in Municipal Court.
- The Municipal Court, without a jury, found in favor of the plaintiffs.
- Defendants appealed to the New York Supreme Court, Appellate Term.

**Issue**
- Are involuntary bailees absolutely liable for conversion when they mistakenly deliver the bailed item to the wrong person after exercising dominion over it?

**Holding**
- Plaintiffs (respondents) win. Defendants, as involuntary bailees, became absolutely liable for conversion when they undertook to redeliver the bond and delivered it to the wrong person. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Voluntary Bailment Duty.** A voluntary bailee has an absolute duty to deliver the bailed article to the right person, and delivery to the wrong person is not excused by care, good faith, or innocence.
- **Involuntary Bailee Custody.** An involuntary bailee, as long as their lack of volition continues, is not under a duty to care for or guard the subject of the bailment and cannot be held liable for negligence in custody.
- **Involuntary Bailee Dominion.** If an involuntary bailee exercises any dominion over the bailed thing, they become as responsible as if they were a voluntary bailee and are absolutely liable in conversion for delivery to the wrong person.
- Rejected: **Involuntary Bailee Reasonable Care (Heugh v. L. & N. W. R. R. Co.).** An involuntary bailee is only required to exercise reasonable and proper care in delivering the bailed item.

**Reasoning**
- The defendants became involuntary bailees when the wrong bond was delivered to them by mistake.
- The defendants exercised dominion over the bond by opening the window, examining the bond, and then handing it to an unidentified boy.
- This exercise of dominion transformed their responsibility from that of a mere involuntary bailee (with no duty of care) to that of a voluntary bailee.
- As voluntary bailees, they had an absolute duty to deliver the bond to the rightful owner (plaintiffs) or their authorized agent.
- Delivering the bond to an unidentified boy, who was not the plaintiffs' messenger, constituted a misdelivery.
- The defendants could have protected themselves by telephoning the plaintiffs or returning the bond via their own messenger.
- The court distinguishes *Krumsky v. Loeser* because in that case, the defendants' possession was due to fraud and they lacked knowledge of the true owner, whereas here, the defendants knew the plaintiffs were the true owners.
- The court also distinguishes *Morris v. Third Ave. R. R. Co.* by noting that while that case found only a duty of reasonable care, it was based on the defendant not knowing the true owner, which is not the case here.

**Dissent (Lehman, J.)**
- The defendants were put in possession of the bond by the plaintiffs' mistake and did not consent to accept it as a deposit.
- An "involuntary bailee" incurs no responsibility unless they commit an "overt act" of interference inconsistent with the true owner's complete right of dominion.
- The defendants' act of attempting to return the bond to what they believed was the plaintiffs' messenger was an honest attempt to restore possession to the true owners, not an exercise of dominion inconsistent with the plaintiffs' ownership.
- The defendants did not claim title or right of possession; they merely transferred physical possession.
- If the defendants used unreasonable means to rid themselves of possession, they might be liable for negligence, but not conversion, as they did not intend to accept a bailment or deny the plaintiffs' ownership.
- There is no evidence of negligence to sustain the judgment.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes a key distinction in bailment law, particularly for **involuntary bailees**. It highlights that while an involuntary bailee initially has minimal duties, any exercise of "dominion" over the property can elevate their responsibility to that of a voluntary bailee, imposing strict liability for misdelivery. This contrasts with cases like *Krumsky v. Loeser* (as interpreted by the majority), where fraud and lack of knowledge of the true owner might alter the duty. It also touches on the concept of **conversion**, where an unauthorized act of dominion over another's property leads to liability.
2. The dissent raises an interesting point about the nature of "dominion" and whether an attempt to return property, even if mistaken, should be considered an act inconsistent with the owner's rights, or merely a negligent act. This tension could be explored in class.

**Takeaway:** An involuntary bailee who exercises any dominion over the bailed property becomes strictly liable for its misdelivery, even if acting in good faith.

## Williams v. Estate of Williams (Tennessee Supreme Court, 1993)

**Facts**
- G.A. Williams executed a will on July 18, 1933.
- G.A. Williams died on November 17, 1944.
- The will was probated on November 24, 1944.
- G.A. Williams was survived by nine children, including three daughters named in the will: Ida Williams, Mallie Williams, and Ethel Williams.
- The will stated: "At my death I want Ida Williams, Mallie Williams, and Ethel Williams, three of my daughters to have my home farm where I now live, consisting of one hundred and eighty-eight acres, to have and to hold during their lives, and not to be sold during their lifetime. If any of them marry their interest ceases and the ones that remain single have full control of same. I am making this will because they have stayed at home and taken care of the home and cared for their mother during her sickness, and I do not want them sold out of a home."
- Ethel Williams, the plaintiff, was 92 years old when the complaint was filed and is the only surviving named daughter.
- Ethel Williams maintained possession of the farm since the testator's death, jointly with Ida Williams and Mallie Williams until their deaths.
- None of the three named daughters ever married.

**Procedural History**
- Ethel Williams filed a complaint seeking construction of the will.
- The trial court initially found a life estate with a remainder interest by intestate succession, then on rehearing, found a fee simple interest.
- The Court of Appeals affirmed the trial court's finding of a fee simple interest.
- The defendants (Estate of G.A. Williams, et al.) appealed to the Tennessee Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Did the will of G.A. Williams grant his three daughters a fee simple interest or a determinable life estate in the home farm?

**Holding**
The defendants (Estate of G.A. Williams, et al.) win. The will granted the daughters a determinable life estate, not a fee simple interest. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Will Construction.** The function of a suit to construe a will is to ascertain and effect the intention of the testator.
- **Predominant Intention.** When a controlling or predominant purpose of the testator is expressed, it is the duty of the court to effectuate that purpose and to construe all subsidiary clauses so as to bring them into subordination to such purpose.
- **Testator's Intention.** The intention of the testator must be ascertained from what the testator has written into the will and not from what some interested party supposes that he intended to do.
- **Rules of Construction.** Under no circumstances will a rule of construction be applied that would operate to defeat the clear intention of the testator.
- **Devise for Life or Until Marriage.** A gift to A for life or until her marriage, or during her widowhood, gives a life estate to A, which is defeasible or determinable upon the marriage of A.
- **Rejected: Statutory Presumption of Fee Simple.** A will shall convey all real estate belonging to the testator unless a contrary intention appears by its words in context. (Rejected as controlling because a contrary intention appeared in the will).

**Reasoning**
- The predominant intention of the testator was to provide a residence and support for his three unmarried daughters who had cared for their mother.
    - The will explicitly states the farm is "to have and to hold during their lives" and "not to be sold during their lifetime," and "I do not want them sold out of a home."
    - The provision that "If any of them marry their interest ceases" further indicates a limited interest tied to their unmarried status and need for support.
    - The testator's reason for the devise was that the daughters "stayed at home and taken care of the home and cared for their mother," implying the devise was a substitute for other means of support.
- The testator did not intend to make an absolute gift (fee simple) to the daughters.
    - The limitations on the devise ("during their lives," "if any of them marry their interest ceases") are inconsistent with a fee simple.
    - The will created an interest not readily alienable and not subject to partition, which is characteristic of a limited estate.
- The will's language, though not from a skilled legal draftsman, clearly indicates a limited estate.
    - The testator used terms like "last will and testament" and "to have and to hold," suggesting familiarity with legal concepts.
    - If a fee simple was intended, the testator would have expressed it clearly.
- The daughters received a **determinable life estate**, defeasible upon marriage.
    - Each daughter also held an **executory interest** in the other two daughters' shares, vesting if another life tenant died or married while she remained unmarried.
    - The testator's heirs-at-law held a **reversion in fee simple**, subject to these determinable life estates and executory interests.
- The case is distinguishable from *White v. Brown* because a clear contrary intention appeared in this will, particularly the marriage condition.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the importance of a testator's intent in will construction, particularly when dealing with potentially ambiguous language regarding the duration of an estate. It contrasts with *White v. Brown* by finding a clear "contrary intention" that prevents a statutory presumption of a fee simple, emphasizing that specific conditions (like marriage) can define a lesser estate. It connects to the concept of **defeasible fees** and **life estates**, showing how conditions can terminate an interest.
2. What if one of the daughters had married and then divorced? Would her interest have been reinstated, or would it have been permanently terminated by the marriage? [CHECK: This is a good question for class discussion.]

**Takeaway:** A will's specific conditions and the testator's predominant intent can create a determinable life estate, even when general statutory presumptions might otherwise suggest a fee simple.

## Brokaw v. Fairchild (New York Supreme Court, 1929)

**Facts**
- In 1886, Isaac V. Brokaw bought a plot of land in Manhattan.
- In 1887, Isaac V. Brokaw built a residence, No. 1 East Seventy-ninth street, on a portion of the plot for his own occupancy.
- Isaac V. Brokaw died in 1913.
- Isaac V. Brokaw's will devised the residence at No. 1 East Seventy-ninth street to his son, George Tuttle Brokaw (plaintiff), for life.
- The will also devised other contiguous properties to other children for life, with specific restrictions on those properties.
- Since 1913, the neighborhood around the residence has changed, with many apartments being built and few private residences.
- The plaintiff attempted to rent the residence for $25,000 to $30,000 per year but received no offers.
- The annual taxes on the property are $16,881, plus $750 for repairs and $300 for a watchman.
- The plaintiff claims his operating expenses for the unproductive residence are $70,542 greater than if he lived in an apartment.
- The plaintiff proposed to demolish the residence and erect a thirteen-story apartment building, which his expert testified would be suitable and profitable.
- The adult remaindermen (defendants) objected to the demolition and proposed construction.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiff brought an action under the Civil Practice Act for a declaration that he has the right to remove the current structures and erect new ones.
- Defendants moved to dismiss.
- The court denied the motions to dismiss.
- The court ruled in favor of the defendants.

**Issue**
- Can a life tenant demolish a valuable residence and replace it with an apartment building, against the objections of the remaindermen, even if the new building would be more profitable and the neighborhood has changed?

**Holding**
- Defendant remaindermen win. A life tenant cannot demolish the existing residence and erect a new structure, as this would constitute waste by permanently injuring the inheritance. The court denied the plaintiff's request for a declaration of his right to do so.

**Rules**
- **Waste.** Any act of a life tenant that does permanent injury to the inheritance is waste.
- **Life Tenant's Use.** The law intends that a life tenant shall enjoy their estate in such a reasonable manner that the land shall pass to the reversioner or remainderman as nearly as practicable unimpaired in its nature, character, and improvements.
- **Life Tenant's Rights.** A life tenant may do whatever is required for the general use and enjoyment of the estate as they received it.
- **No Act of Ownership.** A life tenant has no right to exercise an act of dominion or ownership over the property.
- **Material Change as Waste.** If a tenant materially changes the nature and character of the buildings, it is waste, even if the value of the property should be enhanced by the alteration.
- **Tenant's Authority.** A tenant has no authority to assume the right of judging what may be an improvement to the inheritance; they must confine themselves to the conditions of their lease (or, by extension, the terms of the devise).
- **Rejected: Ameliorating Waste.** The court will not permit demolition if it would permanently change the nature of the property, even if it might be financially beneficial to the remaindermen, especially when the remaindermen object.

**Reasoning**
- The testator's will repeatedly used the words "my residence," emphasizing that the inheritance was the specific building, not just the land.
- Demolishing the existing building and erecting a new one would fundamentally change the "inheritance" that the remaindermen are entitled to receive.
- The court is not concerned with the financial wisdom or motives of the remaindermen in objecting.
- The proposed demolition would destroy a building that cost over $300,000 to erect and would cost at least as much to replace, which is a clear act of waste.
- The cases cited by the plaintiff (Melms v. Pabst Brewing Co., N. Y., O. & W. Ry. Co. v. Livingston, Doherty v. Allman) are distinguishable:
    - *Melms* involved a property that had become valueless as a residence due to surrounding industrial development, a situation not present here. The Brokaw residence is on Fifth Avenue, opposite Central Park, and surrounded by other palatial homes.
    - *N. Y., O. & W. Ry. Co.* was a condemnation proceeding, dealing with compensation, not a life tenant's right to alter.
    - *Doherty* involved an injunction request where the proposed changes were internal and easily reversible, and the court left the aggrieved party to an action for damages, not permitting a fundamental change to the inheritance.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a classic example of **ameliorating waste** being rejected in favor of protecting the remaindermen's right to receive the property in its original character. It contrasts with cases like *Melms v. Pabst Brewing Co.* where significant changes in the surrounding environment justified alterations to the property. It highlights the tension between a life tenant's desire for productive use and the remaindermen's interest in preserving the inheritance as it was devised.
2. This case raises the question of how much change in surrounding circumstances is enough to justify a life tenant altering the property, and whether the court should consider the financial benefit to the remaindermen if they are being "unwise" in their objections.

**Takeaway:** A life tenant generally cannot make substantial alterations to property that fundamentally change its character, even if such changes would increase its value or productive use, especially if the remaindermen object.

## Mountain Brow Lodge No. 82 v. Toscano (Court of Appeal of the State of California, 1967)

**Facts**
- On April 6, 1950, James V. Toscano and Maria Toscano (grantors) conveyed a parcel of real property by gift deed to Mountain Brow Lodge No. 82, Independent Order of Odd Fellows (appellant), a nonprofit corporation.
- The deed contained a habendum clause stating: "Said property is restricted for the use and benefit of the second party, only; and in the event the same fails to be used by the second party or in the event of sale or transfer by the second party of all or any part of said lot, the same is to revert to the first parties herein, their successors, heirs or assigns."
- James V. Toscano was an active member of the lodge at the time of his death.
- The appellant sought to quiet its title to the property.

**Procedural History**
- Appellant filed an action to quiet title against the respondents, who are the trustees and administrators of the deceased grantors' estates.
- The trial court rendered judgment in favor of respondents, determining that the conditions in the deed were not void.
- Appellant appealed.

**Issue**
Can a deed validly impose a condition that property revert to the grantors if it is not used by the grantee for its organizational purposes, even if a separate condition against sale or transfer is void as a restraint on alienation?

**Holding**
Respondents (grantors' estates) win. The condition restricting the use of the property to the grantee's organizational purposes created a valid fee simple subject to a condition subsequent, which is severable from the void condition against alienation. Affirmed as modified.

**Rules**
- **Restraints on Alienation.** Conditions restraining alienation, when repugnant to the interest created, are void. (California Civil Code section 711)
- **Severability of Conditions.** An invalid restraint against alienation does not necessarily affect or nullify a condition on land use if the conditions are in the disjunctive and clearly severable.
- **Fee Simple Subject to Condition Subsequent.** No formal language is necessary to create a fee simple subject to a condition subsequent as long as the intent of the grantor is clear.
- **Intent of Grantor.** The object in construing a deed is to ascertain the intention of the grantor from the words employed and from surrounding circumstances.
- **Distinction between Use Restriction and Restraint on Alienation.** A condition that restricts land use and creates a defeasible estate is distinct from a covenant that restrains the alienation of a fee simple absolute.

**Reasoning**
- The condition prohibiting sale or transfer is an absolute restraint on alienation and is void under Civil Code section 711.
- However, the conditions regarding sale/transfer and use are in the disjunctive ("or") and are clearly severable. Therefore, the invalidity of the restraint on alienation does not invalidate the use condition.
- The court interpreted the use restriction ("restricted for the use and benefit of the second party, only") in light of the surrounding circumstances, including the fact that the appellant is a nonprofit lodge and the grantor was an active member.
- This interpretation leads to the conclusion that the grantors intended the land to be used for the lodge's fraternal and similar purposes.
- This type of use restriction creates a **fee simple subject to a condition subsequent**, allowing for reverter if the land ceases to be used for those purposes.
- The court distinguishes between a restriction on *who* can use the land and a restriction on *how* the land can be used, finding that restrictions on use, even if they implicitly limit who can use it, are generally valid.
- Upholding such use restrictions is consistent with established California law and cases from other jurisdictions involving conveyances to cities, school districts, or churches for specific purposes.

**Dissent (Stone, J.)**
- The entire habendum clause should be invalid as a restraint upon alienation.
- The use restriction, which states the property may be used by only Lodge No. 82, prevents the grantee from conveying the property just as effectively as the explicit condition against "sale or transfer."
- The effect of language should be judged by what it does; if two terms generate the same legal result, they should be treated alike.
- The policy against restraints on alienation (Civil Code section 711) is important, especially considering that organizations may cease to exist, leading to fragmented titles among numerous heirs.
- A restriction on *who* may use the land should be distinguished from a restriction on the *kind* of use. A restriction on the kind of use does not restrain alienation because the property can still be conveyed, subject to the restriction.
- The dissent argues that this restriction on "who" can use the property is analogous to racial restrictions, which are prohibited by Civil Code section 782, as it creates an "inverse exclusionary clause."
- The restraint serves no reasonable purpose to justify the indefinite suspension of alienation.

**NOTES:**
1. This case highlights the distinction between a direct restraint on alienation and a condition subsequent that restricts the use of property, even if the latter indirectly impacts alienability. It connects to the broader theme of **owner autonomy** versus societal interests in free alienability of land. It contrasts with cases like *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* where the court was hesitant to recognize property rights that would unduly restrict the free flow of goods (human cells in that case). The court here is willing to uphold a restriction on use, which can be seen as a limitation on the grantee's autonomy, in favor of the grantor's intent.
2. The dissent raises an interesting point about the practical effect of a "use by only" restriction being functionally equivalent to a restraint on alienation. This tension between the form of a restriction and its practical impact is worth discussing. Does the majority's distinction create a loophole for grantors to effectively restrain alienation through cleverly worded use restrictions?

**Takeaway:** A condition in a deed restricting the use of property to the grantee's organizational purposes can create a valid fee simple subject to a condition subsequent, even if an accompanying explicit restraint on alienation is void.
