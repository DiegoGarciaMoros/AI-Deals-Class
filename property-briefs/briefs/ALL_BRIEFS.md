## Strain v. Green (Washington Supreme Court, 1946)

**Facts**
- On August 7, 1943, Jacob Green agreed to sell his waterfront home to William Strain for $35,000, with Greens having 60 days to move out.
- On August 20, 1943, Green and wife executed a warranty deed to Strain and wife.
- On August 27, 1943, the deed was delivered to the Strains, and they paid the full purchase price.
- At some point after delivery of the deed, the Greens moved out, taking with them a hot water tank, Venetian blinds, lighting fixtures, and three mirrors. They replaced the original hot water tank with an older, smaller one and replaced crystal light fixtures with plastic imitations.
- On November 8, 1943, the Strains' attorney demanded the return of the removed property.
- The Greens did not comply with the demand.

**Procedural History**
- The Strains sued the Greens in March 1944, seeking return of the property or damages.
- The trial court held the hot water tank and Venetian blinds were fixtures and ordered their return, awarding damages for reinstallation.
- The trial court held the light fixtures and mirrors were personal property that the Greens had a right to remove.
- The Strains appealed the trial court's holding regarding the light fixtures and mirrors.

**Issue**
- Are electric light fixtures and large ornamental mirrors attached to walls by the owner considered fixtures that transfer with the property upon sale, despite the owner's secret intent to remove them?

**Holding**
- Strain (plaintiff) wins in part. Electric light fixtures and two of the three mirrors were fixtures and should not have been removed. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Fixture Test.** The true criterion of a fixture is the united application of three requisites: (1) actual annexation to the realty, or something appurtenant thereto; (2) application to the use or purpose to which that part of the realty with which it is connected is appropriated; and (3) the intention of the party making the annexation to make a permanent accession to the freehold.
- **Intent for Fixtures.** The intent of the party making the annexation is not to be gathered from testimony of the actual state of their mind, but is to be inferred from the nature of the article affixed, the relation and situation to the freehold of the party making the annexation, the manner of the annexation, and the purpose for which it is made.
- **Secret Intent.** The secret intention of the owner who affixed the disputed article does not, of itself, determine whether or not it was a fixture or a mere personal chattel.
- **Presumption of Intent (Owner vs. Tenant).** When an annexation is made by a tenant or licensor, the presumption is that they did not intend to enrich the freehold. When an annexation is made by the owner of the property, the presumption is that they intended to enrich the freehold.
- **Rejected: Outmoded Precedent.** Earlier cases holding items like mantels, bathtubs, or electric hot water heaters not to be fixtures are outmoded due to changes in times and household appliances becoming "built-in" necessities.

**Reasoning**
- The court rejects the Greens' argument that their secret intention to remove the items should control.
    - Allowing secret intent would encourage dishonest sellers to remove movable articles.
    - Intent must be inferred from objective circumstances, not subjective, uncommunicated thoughts.
- The light fixtures meet the three-part fixture test:
    - They were actually annexed to the realty (walls and ceiling).
    - They were applied to the use of the house (to make it livable).
    - The presumption is that the owner intended a permanent accession, which is not overcome by secret intent.
    - The fact that they are called "light fixtures" and were replaced by the Greens implies they are considered essential parts of a complete house.
- Two of the mirrors meet the fixture test:
    - They were attached to plywood that was firmly nailed to the plastered walls.
    - Removing them would leave damaged walls (plywood could not be removed without damaging plaster, or leaving nail holes and discolored wall).
    - The mirror and its plywood backing should be regarded as one article, forming a portion of the house walls.
- The powder room mirror was not proven to be physically annexed to the realty.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case clarifies the **fixture** doctrine, emphasizing an objective test for intent rather than subjective, secret intent. It updates the understanding of what constitutes a fixture, moving away from older precedents that might consider items like bathtubs or mantels as personal property. This connects to the broader theme of how courts determine what constitutes "real property" versus "personal property," which is fundamental to property law. It also shows how the law adapts to changing societal norms and technology ("built-in" era).
2. What if the Greens had explicitly told the Strains before the sale that they intended to remove the chandelier and mirrors? Would that have changed the outcome, or would the objective annexation still control?

**Takeaway:** Whether an item is a fixture depends on objective factors of annexation, adaptation, and the presumed intent of the annexor, not on their secret, uncommunicated intentions.

## Nebraska v. Iowa (Supreme Court of the United States, 1892)

**Facts**
- Iowa was admitted to the Union in 1846, with its western boundary defined as the middle of the main channel of the Missouri River.
- Nebraska was admitted in 1867, with its eastern boundary likewise defined as the middle of the channel of the Missouri River.
- Between 1861 and 1877, the Missouri River channel near Omaha underwent significant changes.
- In 1877, the river above Omaha, which had followed an ox-bow course, suddenly cut through the neck of the bow, forming a new channel.
- Nebraska filed an original suit against Iowa to determine the boundary line between the two states.
- Iowa answered, claiming the disputed ground was part of its territory, and filed a cross-bill for affirmative relief.

**Procedural History**
- Nebraska filed an original suit in the Supreme Court of the United States.
- Iowa answered and filed a cross-bill.
- Replications were filed and proofs taken.

**Issue**
Does the boundary line between two states, defined by a river, change when the river's course shifts due to gradual accretion or sudden avulsion?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Nebraska) and Defendant (Iowa) both win in part. The boundary line between Iowa and Nebraska is a varying line where the river changes by accretion, but remains fixed in the old channel where the river changes by avulsion. Remanded for further proceedings to determine the exact boundary.

**Rules**
- **Accretion.** When land borders on running water and the banks change by gradual accretion, the riparian owner's boundary line remains the stream, including the accumulated soil.
- **Avulsion.** When a boundary stream suddenly abandons its old bed and seeks a new course, the boundary remains as it was, in the center of the old channel.
- **State Boundaries.** The rules of accretion and avulsion apply to states when a river forms one of their boundary lines.
- **Missouri River Accretion.** The law of accretion controls on the Missouri River, despite its rapid and great changes, because the process of deposition is still gradual and imperceptible.
- **Accretion Test.** The test for what is gradual and imperceptible is that though witnesses may see progress over time, they could not perceive it while the process was actually occurring.

**Reasoning**
- The court relies on established common law principles regarding riparian boundaries.
- **Accretion:**
    - When changes are gradual and imperceptible, the boundary shifts with the stream.
    - This rule is based on the idea that a proprietor subject to loss by gradual erosion should also benefit from gradual gain.
    - The Missouri River, despite its rapid changes, still experiences accretion because the earth particles disintegrate and deposit gradually, not as solid masses.
- **Avulsion:**
    - When a stream suddenly changes its course, the boundary remains in the center of the old, abandoned channel.
    - This is because a sudden, violent change does not alter the established boundary.
    - The 1877 change in the Missouri River, where it cut through an ox-bow, was an avulsion.
- These principles apply equally to boundaries between private landowners and between states or nations.
- The court cites numerous authorities, including international law scholars and civil law, to support the universal recognition of these rules.
- The court previously held in *Jefferis v. East Omaha Land Co.* that the general law of accretion applies to the Missouri River, and the current evidence supports this.
- The 1877 change in the river's course was a clear case of avulsion, fixing the boundary in the old channel.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is foundational for understanding how **riparian boundaries** are determined, particularly the distinction between **accretion** and **avulsion**. It connects to the concept of **owner sovereignty** by defining how property lines shift or remain fixed in response to natural changes in water bodies. It builds on the idea that property rights are not always static but can be influenced by natural phenomena, as seen in cases like *Ghen v. Rich* (custom in whaling) or *Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois* (public trust doctrine over submerged lands).
2. The court's definition of "imperceptible" for accretion, allowing for discernment "at intervals of not less than three or more months," seems to stretch the common understanding of the word. This raises the question of how frequently observation can occur before a change is no longer considered "imperceptible" and thus not accretion.

**Takeaway:** The boundary between properties or states defined by a river shifts with gradual changes (accretion) but remains fixed in the old channel if the river suddenly changes course (avulsion).

## Scott v. Anderson-Tully Co. (Mississippi Court of Appeals, 2015)

**Facts**
- In 1925, Stewart Scott Jr. and Willie Scott inherited an undivided one-half interest in a 584.6-acre tract of land.
- In 1944, a plat prepared for a timber deed from the Scotts noted a wire fence on the east side of the property and stated that "20 1/10 [acres] outside of fence is included in total acreage," but also that the property "outside of fence & may be disputed."
- In 1954, Richard Scott, an heir, rebuilt the wire fence, moving it slightly west along the tree line.
- In 1969, Anderson-Tully Company purchased land adjacent to the Scotts' property via quitclaim deed.
- In 1969, Anderson-Tully's surveyor, E.C. Burkhardt, surveyed the property and determined the boundary line ran along the wire fence, making the disputed twenty acres part of Anderson-Tully's property.
- From 1969 to 2010, Anderson-Tully marked its perceived boundary line along the wire fence with blue paint, repainting it in 1986 and 1998.
- From 1969 to 2010, Anderson-Tully maintained the disputed acreage, cut and harvested timber in 1990, 1999, and 2010, and issued five hunting licenses for the property.
- In 2003, Scott objected to Anderson-Tully's possession.
- In 2007, Scott cancelled a hunting lease with the Linwood Hunting Club, which had leased a portion of the Scotts' property.
- In December 2008, Anderson-Tully's attorney sent Scott a letter stating Anderson-Tully owned the disputed tract.
- In early 2010, Anderson-Tully harvested timber on the disputed land.
- On March 19, 2010, Scott filed suit.

**Procedural History**
- Scott filed suit in Jefferson County Chancery Court seeking damages for timber removal, to quiet and confirm title, and to enjoin Anderson-Tully from entering the land.
- Anderson-Tully counterclaimed to quiet and confirm its own title, arguing it owned the property by deed or, alternatively, by adverse possession.
- The chancellor dismissed Scott's claim, finding Anderson-Tully had acquired title through adverse possession.
- Scott filed a Rule 60(b) motion for relief from judgment or a new trial, which the chancellor denied.
- Scott appealed to the Mississippi Court of Appeals.

**Issue**
Can a party acquire title to land through adverse possession if they continuously and openly possess the land for ten years, even if their deed does not clearly describe the property?

**Holding**
Defendant (Anderson-Tully) wins. A party can acquire title to land through adverse possession by proving all six elements by clear and convincing evidence, even if their deed does not accurately describe the disputed property. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Adverse Possession.** To establish adverse possession, the claimant must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the possession was (1) under claim of ownership; (2) actual or hostile; (3) open, notorious, and visible; (4) continuous and uninterrupted for a period of ten years; (5) exclusive; and (6) peaceful.
- **Claim of Ownership.** The possessor must "fly [his] flag over the property" in such a way as to put the actual owner on notice that the property is "being held under an adverse claim of ownership." The quality, not the quantity, of acts must be considered.
- **Rejected: Mere presence of a fence.** The mere presence of a fence, without more, is not sufficient to sustain a claim of adverse possession.
- **Rejected: Payment of taxes alone.** The payment of taxes alone is not dispositive of the claim of ownership.
- **Actual or Hostile Possession.** Possession is hostile and adverse when the adverse possessor intends to claim title notwithstanding that the claim is made under a mistaken belief that the land is within the calls of the possessor's deed. The adverse possessor must possess the property without the owner's permission.
- **Possessory Acts for Wild Lands.** Possessory acts necessary to establish a claim of adverse possession may vary with the characteristics of the land, and adverse possession of "wild" or unimproved lands may be established by evidence of acts that would be wholly insufficient in the case of improved or developed lands. Possession of such property may be established by a continued claim evidenced by public acts of ownership.
- **Open, Notorious, and Visible Possession.** The possessor "must unfurl his flag on the land, and keep it flying, so that the actual owner may see, and if he will, that an enemy has invaded his domains, and planted the standard of conquest."
- **Continuous and Uninterrupted Possession.** Adverse possession requires continuous and uninterrupted possession of a disputed property for at least ten years.
- **Exclusive Possession.** Exclusive possession means that the possessor "evinces an intention to possess and hold land to the exclusion of, and in opposition to, the claims of all others, and the claimant’s conduct must afford an unequivocal indication that he is exercising the dominion of a sole owner." It does not mean that no one else can use the property.
- **Peaceful Possession.** An adverse possessor’s use of a claimed property must be peaceful. Expected disputes associated with the use or ownership of the property are not indicative of the possession not being peaceful.

**Reasoning**
- The chancellor's findings of fact are reviewed for manifest error or clear erroneousness, and will be affirmed if supported by substantial evidence.
- **Claim of Ownership:** Anderson-Tully's actions of marking borders with blue paint, repainting, cutting timber, performing timber-stand improvements, and issuing hunting licenses were sufficient to "fly its flag" and put the owner on notice. Scott's payment of taxes was not dispositive.
- **Actual or Hostile:** Anderson-Tully's marking, managing, harvesting timber, and issuing hunting licenses without permission demonstrated hostile possession. Given the "wild" nature of the land, these public acts of ownership were sufficient.
- **Open, Notorious, and Visible:** The blue paint lines, timber harvesting, and hunting licenses were visible and known in the community, fulfilling the requirement to "unfurl his flag."
- **Continuous and Uninterrupted:** Anderson-Tully's actions from 1969 to 2010, including marking boundaries and managing timber, demonstrated continuous possession for more than the ten-year statutory period. Scott's objections in 2003 and 2007 came after Anderson-Tully's claim had already ripened.
- **Exclusive:** No evidence showed the Scotts or anyone else used the property east of the fence after 1969. Anderson-Tully leased the land for hunting and its employees were not notified of others crossing the line.
- **Peaceful:** Anderson-Tully's possession was peaceful because no objections or interference occurred until 2003, well after the adverse possession period had ripened.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the practical application of **adverse possession** elements, particularly how the nature of the land (e.g., "wild" or unimproved) can affect the "quantum of proof" required for certain elements. It connects to cases like *Howard v. Kunto* in discussing the continuity of possession, though *Kunto* focused on tacking and seasonal use. It also highlights the importance of visible acts of ownership, similar to the concept of "flying the flag" seen in other adverse possession cases. The court's discussion of the fence as a "convenience fence" versus a boundary marker is a common issue in boundary disputes.
2. The chancellor's finding that neither deed "accurately describe[s] the disputed tract to any certainty" but then finding adverse possession raises an interesting point about the relative importance of record title versus actual possession, especially when boundaries are unclear. What if the original deed *had* clearly included the disputed twenty acres for Scott? Would Anderson-Tully's adverse possession claim still have been as strong, or would the record title have carried more weight?

**Takeaway:** Adverse possession can establish title even when record title is ambiguous, provided the claimant's actions clearly demonstrate all elements of adverse possession for the statutory period.

## Carpenter v. Ruperto (Iowa Supreme Court, 1982)

**Facts**
- In July 1951, Virginia Carpenter and her husband moved into a home in Des Moines.
- Carpenter's lot was 40 feet by 125 feet.
- A larger, undeveloped lot, owned by defendants and their predecessors, bounded Carpenter's property to the north.
- The disputed property is the south 60 feet of the defendants' lot, adjacent to Carpenter's northern boundary.
- When Carpenter moved in, the lot to the north was a cornfield.
- Carpenter knew her lot's dimensions and that it did not include the cornfield.
- In 1952, Carpenter and her husband cleared several feet of the property to the north, graded it, and planted grass seed.
- Carpenter used the land as an extension of her yard, planting peony bushes in the 1950s, installing a propane tank 30 feet north of her lot in 1964, constructing a dirt bank in 1965, and putting in a driveway infringing five feet onto the land in 1975.
- The Rosenfelds owned the defendants' lot from July 1960 to February 1978, storing junk and debris on it and paying all taxes.
- Carpenter and her husband once obtained the Rosenfelds' permission to keep a horse on the lot.
- In the 1960s, Carpenter examined the plat of the defendants' lot at the courthouse to see if it extended to a street to the north.
- In 1978, defendant McCormick purchased his interest in the lot, aware of a potential boundary dispute due to Carpenter's propane tank and driveway.
- Efforts to settle the dispute failed.

**Procedural History**
- Carpenter sued to quiet title based on adverse possession.
- The trial court found Carpenter did not establish adverse possession because she lacked a good faith claim of right.
- The trial court ordered defendants to deed a strip of land for Carpenter's driveway and pay to move her propane tank.
- Carpenter appealed the adverse possession ruling.
- Defendants cross-appealed the equitable relief granted to Carpenter.

**Issue**
Can a person acquire title by adverse possession under a claim of right if they knew they had no legal right to the property when they took possession?

**Holding**
Defendant (record title holder) wins. A claim of right for adverse possession requires good faith, which is absent when the possessor knows they have no legal right to the property. Affirmed on the appeal; dismissed on the cross-appeal.

**Rules**
- **Adverse Possession Elements.** One claiming title by adverse possession must establish hostile, actual, open, exclusive, and continuous possession, under a claim of right or color of title, for at least ten years, by clear and positive proof.
- **Presumption of Regular Title.** The law presumes possession under regular title, so the doctrine of adverse possession is strictly construed.
- **Claim of Right (Good Faith).** Good faith is an essential component of a claim of right in adverse possession.
- **Knowledge of No Title.** When knowledge of lack of title is accompanied by knowledge of no basis for claiming an interest in the property, a good faith claim of right cannot be established.
- **Rejected: Squatter's Rights.** To permit a squatter to assert a claim of right would put a premium on dishonesty.
- **Rejected: Mere Knowledge of Defect.** Knowledge of a defect in title is not alone sufficient to preclude proof of good faith. *(dicta)*

**Reasoning**
- The court found Carpenter knew her lot did not include the cornfield to the north and that someone else had title to it, and she had no interest or claim to it.
- This was not a case of confusion or mistake.
- At the time she entered possession, Carpenter knew she had no legal right to do so.
- Allowing acquisition of a claim of right by merely entering possession would recognize squatter's rights, which the law does not permit.
- The good faith requirement prevents mere squatters from benefiting from adverse possession.
- The court distinguished cases where knowledge of a defect in title does not preclude good faith from situations where there is knowledge of no basis for claiming an interest.
- The cross-appeal was dismissed because it was not filed within the mandatory five-day period after the appeal was taken, as required by Iowa R.App. 5(a).

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the "claim of right" element of adverse possession, particularly the requirement of good faith. It contrasts with cases where a mistaken belief about a boundary line might still satisfy the claim of right, as seen in cases like *Howard v. Kunto* (though *Howard* focused more on continuity of possession and privity). *Carpenter* emphasizes that a knowing trespasser, or "squatter," cannot establish a claim of right.
2. The court's distinction between "knowledge of a defect in title" (which might be okay) and "knowledge of no basis for claiming an interest" (which is not okay) could be a point of tension. How clear must the knowledge be? What if Carpenter genuinely believed the land was "waste land" as in *Goulding v. Shonquist*?

**Takeaway:** A knowing trespasser cannot establish a good faith claim of right for adverse possession in Iowa.

## Howard v. Kunto (Washington Court of Appeals, 1970)

**Facts**
- As early as 1932, McCall resided in a house on a 50-foot parcel of land on Hood Canal, but his deed described an adjacent 50-foot parcel to the west.
- Over the years since 1946, several conveyances occurred, using the same incorrect legal description, with each new owner taking possession of the house and land McCall occupied.
- The Millers, Kunto's immediate predecessors, had a survey done to build a dock, which mistakenly indicated their deed description and physical occupation were in conformity.
- The Kuntos took possession of the disputed property under a deed from the Millers in 1959.
- In 1960, the Howards, who owned land east of the Kuntos, had a survey performed to convey an interest to the Yearlys.
- The Howards' survey revealed that the deed descriptions and land occupancy of the parties did not coincide: the Howards were record owners of the land occupied by the Moyers, and the Moyers held record title to the land occupied by the Kuntos.
- Howard approached Moyer, and Moyer conveyed to the Howards record title to the land upon which the Kunto house stood.
- Until April 1960, neither Moyer nor his predecessors asserted ownership rights to the property possessed by Kunto and his predecessors.
- The Howards and Yearlys initiated an action to quiet title in August 1960.
- At the time of the action, the Kuntos had been in occupancy of the disputed property for less than a year.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court quieted title in the plaintiffs (Howards and Yearlys).
- The defendants (Kuntos) appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a person establish adverse possession by tacking the periods of possession of predecessors in interest when the deed mistakenly describes an adjacent parcel, and when the occupancy was limited to summer use?

**Holding**
- The defendants (Kuntos) win. A person can establish adverse possession by tacking the periods of possession of predecessors in interest when there is a reasonable connection between successive occupants, even if the deed mistakenly describes an adjacent parcel, and summer occupancy can constitute continuous possession. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Adverse Possession Elements.** To constitute adverse possession, there must be actual possession which is uninterrupted, open and notorious, hostile and exclusive, and under a claim of right made in good faith for the statutory period.
- **Continuity of Possession.** The requisite possession requires such possession and dominion "as ordinarily marks the conduct of owners in general, in holding, managing, and caring for property of like nature and condition."
- **Summer Occupancy.** Continuity of possession may be established although the land is used regularly for only a certain period each year, provided there is no break so as to sever two possessions.
- **Tacking.** Tacking of adverse possession is permitted if the successive occupants are in "privity."
- **Privity (Traditional).** The deed running between the parties purporting to transfer the land possessed traditionally furnishes the privity of estate which connects the possession of the successive occupants.
- **Rejected: Privity (Strict Construction).** Where the deed does not describe any of the land which was occupied, the actual transfer of possession is insufficient to establish privity.
- **Privity (Modern).** The requirement of "privity" is no more than judicial recognition of the need for some reasonable connection between successive occupants of real property so as to raise their claim of right above the status of the wrongdoer or the trespasser.

**Reasoning**
- The court rejected the conclusion that summer occupancy only of a summer beach home destroys the continuity of possession.
    - The nature and condition of the property (a summer recreational retreat) must be considered.
    - Occupancy during the period the land is capable of use is sufficient continuity.
- The court found sufficient privity of estate to permit tacking.
    - The traditional requirement of privity had roots in preventing a succession of trespasses from defeating record title.
    - There is a substantial difference between a squatter and a property purchaser who, due to an inaccurate survey, occupies and improves property adjacent to what their deed describes.
    - There is a strong public policy favoring early certainty as to the location of land ownership.
    - Exact determination of land locations and boundaries on the irregular perimeters of Puget Sound is difficult and expensive, and it is not reasonable or customary for every purchaser to engage a surveyor.
    - The technical requirement of "privity" should not upset long periods of occupancy by those who in good faith received an erroneous deed description.
    - A reasonable connection exists where successive purchasers received record title to tract A under the mistaken belief they were acquiring tract B, and possession of tract B was transferred and occupied continuously for more than 10 years by successive occupants.
    - This application of the privity requirement is especially relevant where the holder of record title to tract B acquired it with knowledge of the discrepancy.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the **adverse possession** elements of "continuity" and "privity." It relaxes the strict interpretation of these elements, particularly in the context of mistaken boundaries and seasonal use, connecting to the idea of protecting good faith possessors over strict adherence to record title. It contrasts with cases like *Carpenter v. Ruperto* which emphasize the "good faith" requirement for adverse possession, though *Howard v. Kunto* still requires a "claim of right made in good faith."
2. The court's reasoning regarding "privity" suggests a policy preference for protecting long-term, good-faith possessors over strict adherence to deed descriptions, especially when surveys are difficult and errors are common. This raises the question of how far this relaxation of privity could extend. What if the mistake wasn't immediately contiguous, or if the chain of title was broken by a period of non-occupancy?

**Takeaway:** Adverse possession requirements for continuity and privity can be flexible to accommodate the nature of the property and good-faith mistakes in deed descriptions, allowing for tacking of possession across successive owners.

## Songbyrd Inc. v. Estate of Grossman (United States Court of Appeals, Second Circuit, 2000)

**Facts**
- In the early 1970s, Henry Roeland Byrd ("Professor Longhair") made master recording tapes in Baton Rouge.
- Arthur Davis and Parker Dinkins, acting for Byrd, sent these master tapes to Albert Grossman, president of Bearsville Records, Inc., in Woodstock, New York, as demonstration tapes only, without intent to transfer ownership.
- The tapes remained in Grossman's possession.
- In 1975, Dinkins sent two letters to Bearsville Records, Inc. requesting the return of the master recordings, but received no response.
- Grossman died in 1985. Bearsville Records, Inc. was dissolved, but Grossman's estate ("the Estate") continued doing business as Bearsville Records and possessed the tapes.
- In 1986, the Estate licensed some of the Byrd master recordings to Rounder Records Corporation for an advance against royalties.
- In 1987, Rounder released an album of Byrd's recordings, which won a posthumous Grammy Award.
- In 1991, the Estate licensed some master recordings to Rhino Records, which released an album.
- In 1993, SongByrd, Inc. was incorporated as a successor-in-interest to Byrd's intellectual property rights.

**Procedural History**
- SongByrd filed a "Petition in Revendication" against the Estate in Louisiana state court in 1995, seeking a declaration of ownership, return of tapes, and damages.
- The Estate removed the case to the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Louisiana.
- The Louisiana District Court initially dismissed the suit as time-barred, explicitly declining to consider personal jurisdiction.
- The Fifth Circuit reversed the dismissal, remanding for consideration of the time-bar defense and personal jurisdiction.
- On remand, the Louisiana District Court found it lacked personal jurisdiction over the Estate and sua sponte transferred the case to the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of New York.
- The New York District Court (Magistrate Judge Homer) granted summary judgment for the Estate, finding the suit time-barred under New York law.
- SongByrd appealed to the Second Circuit.

**Issue**
Is SongByrd's claim for wrongful possession of master recording tapes time-barred under New York law, given that the case was transferred from Louisiana due to a lack of personal jurisdiction?

**Holding**
Defendant (Estate of Grossman) wins. SongByrd's claim is time-barred under New York law because the cause of action for conversion accrued when the Estate licensed the master tapes in 1986, more than three years before the suit was filed. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Transfer for Lack of Personal Jurisdiction.** A district court lacking personal jurisdiction over a defendant may transfer the case to a district where personal jurisdiction can be exercised.
- **Law of Transferee Jurisdiction.** When a case is transferred due to a lack of personal jurisdiction in the transferor court, the law of the transferee jurisdiction applies.
- **New York Statute of Limitations for Conversion.** The relevant statute of limitations for conversion and recovery of chattels in New York is three years.
- **Accrual of Conversion Claim (New York).** A cause of action for conversion accrues when the possessor begins using the plaintiff's property as its own, not each time the property is subsequently used.
- **Accrual of Replevin Claim against Good-Faith Purchaser (New York).** A cause of action for replevin against the good-faith purchaser of a stolen chattel accrues when the true owner makes demand for return of the chattel and the person in possession refuses to return it.
- **Demand and Refusal Rule (New York).** The demand and refusal rule, which delays accrual of a claim for the benefit of the true owner, is not required for a conversion claim against a possessor who openly deals with the property as its own.
- **Unreasonable Delay in Demand (New York).** An owner, having discovered the location of lost property, cannot unreasonably delay making demand upon the person in possession of that property.

**Reasoning**
- The transfer from Louisiana to New York was proper because the Louisiana court lacked personal jurisdiction over the Estate.
    - Grossman's contacts with Louisiana were "extremely scant" and insufficient to establish personal jurisdiction.
    - His invitation to Byrd to come to New York was too insubstantial a contact for specific jurisdiction.
    - General jurisdiction was not satisfied.
- New York law applies because the transfer was due to lack of personal jurisdiction in Louisiana.
- Under New York law, the three-year statute of limitations for conversion applies.
- SongByrd's cause of action accrued no later than August 1986.
    - The Estate began using the master tapes as its own when it licensed them to Rounder in 1986.
    - This act constituted conversion, as established in *Sporn v. MCA Records, Inc.*, where commercial exploitation of master recordings triggered accrual.
    - The demand-and-refusal rule, as applied in *Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation v. Lubell* and *Hoelzer v. City of Stamford*, does not apply here because the Estate openly dealt with the property as its own, changing the character of its possession.
    - Even if a demand were required, SongByrd's predecessors unreasonably delayed making a demand after 1975 and certainly after the 1987 Grammy Award made the licensing widely known.
- Since the suit was filed in 1995, more than three years after the 1986 accrual, the claim is time-barred.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case highlights the importance of the **statute of limitations** in property disputes, particularly for chattels. It distinguishes between the accrual rules for conversion and replevin under New York law, building on *Sporn v. MCA Records, Inc.* and *Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation v. Lubell*. It shows that openly treating property as one's own can trigger the statute of limitations for conversion, even without a formal demand and refusal. This contrasts with cases like *Moore v. 2. The court's discussion of the transfer order and the applicable law is important. It clarifies that when a case is transferred for lack of personal jurisdiction, the law of the transferee court applies, which can be outcome-determinative for the statute of limitations. This could lead to forum shopping or strategic choices about where to file. **Takeaway:** A claim for conversion of a chattel accrues in New York when the possessor openly treats the property as its own, triggering the statute of limitations, and the demand-and-refusal rule does not apply in such circumstances.

## Moore v. Regents of the University of California (Supreme Court of California, 1990)

**Facts**
- In October 1976, John Moore (Plaintiff) sought treatment for hairy-cell leukemia at UCLA Medical Center.
- Dr. David Golde (Defendant), Moore's physician, confirmed the diagnosis and recommended a splenectomy, which Moore consented to.
- Before the splenectomy, Golde and researcher Shirley Quan (Defendant) planned to obtain portions of Moore's spleen for research unrelated to his medical care, but did not inform Moore.
- On October 20, 1976, surgeons removed Moore's spleen.
- Between November 1976 and September 1983, Moore made several follow-up visits at Golde's direction, during which Golde withdrew additional samples of blood, bone marrow, skin, and sperm, representing these were necessary for Moore's health.
- During this period, defendants conducted research on Moore's cells and planned to financially benefit from exploiting them, concealing these activities from Moore.
- Sometime before August 1979, Golde established a cell line from Moore's T-lymphocytes.
- On January 30, 1981, the Regents of the University of California (Defendant) applied for a patent on the cell line, listing Golde and Quan as inventors, with a policy for them to share in royalties.
- On March 20, 1984, the patent was issued to the Regents, naming Golde and Quan as inventors.
- Golde negotiated commercial development agreements with Genetics Institute, Inc. (Defendant) and Sandoz Pharmaceuticals Corporation (Defendant), becoming a paid consultant and acquiring stock rights, with payments also made to the Regents.

**Procedural History**
- Moore filed a complaint alleging 13 causes of action, including conversion, breach of fiduciary duty, and lack of informed consent.
- The superior court sustained all defendants' demurrers to the entire complaint, reasoning that the conversion claim was defective and the other claims incorporated those defects.
- The superior court later sustained demurrers from Genetics Institute and Sandoz without leave to amend, finding no cause of action for conversion and secondary liability allegations too conclusory.
- The Court of Appeal reversed, holding that the complaint stated a cause of action for conversion, but agreed the allegations against Genetics Institute and Sandoz were insufficient, directing leave to amend. The Court of Appeal also directed the superior court to rule on the remaining causes of action.
- Defendants appealed to the Supreme Court of California.

**Issue**
Can a patient sue his physician and other parties for conversion of his cells used in medical research without his permission, or for breach of fiduciary duty and lack of informed consent due to undisclosed research and economic interests?

**Holding**
Moore (Plaintiff) wins in part. The complaint states a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty and lack of informed consent, but not for conversion. Affirmed in part and reversed in part.

**Rules**
- **Informed Consent.** A physician has a fiduciary duty to disclose all information material to a patient's decision to consent to medical treatment, including personal interests unrelated to the patient's health (research or economic) that may affect professional judgment.
- **Conversion.** To establish conversion, a plaintiff must prove actual interference with their ownership or right of possession of personal property.
    - Rejected: A patient retains sufficient ownership interest in excised cells to support a cause of action for conversion.
    - Rejected: The tort of conversion should be extended to cover the use of human cells in medical research.

**Reasoning**
- **Breach of Fiduciary Duty and Lack of Informed Consent:**
    - A physician must disclose personal interests (research or economic) unrelated to the patient's health that may affect their professional judgment.
    - Failure to disclose such interests can lead to a cause of action for performing medical procedures without informed consent or breach of fiduciary duty.
    - Moore alleged Golde had undisclosed research interests in his cells before the splenectomy and concealed economic interests during postoperative procedures.
    - These allegations are sufficient to state a cause of action against Golde.
    - The other defendants (Regents, Quan, Genetics Institute, Sandoz) are not physicians and did not have a direct fiduciary duty to Moore; their liability would depend on secondary liability theories.
- **Conversion:**
    - Moore's claim for conversion fails because he does not retain an ownership interest in his excised cells.
        - No reported judicial decision supports such a claim.
        - California statutory law (Health and Safety Code § 7054.4) drastically limits a patient's control over excised cells by requiring their disposal after scientific use, eliminating many rights ordinarily attached to property.
        - The patented cell line and products derived from it are factually and legally distinct from Moore's original cells, as the patent rewards inventive effort, not the discovery of naturally occurring raw materials.
    - Extending conversion liability to this context is inappropriate due to policy considerations:
        - It would threaten socially useful medical research with disabling civil liability, as conversion is a strict liability tort that would impact innocent researchers.
        - Uncertainty about legal title to biological materials would hinder research and product development in the biotechnology industry.
        - The issue is better suited for legislative resolution, given the complex policy choices involved and the legislature's ability to gather evidence and hear from all interested parties.
        - The tort of conversion is not necessary to protect patients' rights, as the existing fiduciary duty and informed consent theories adequately protect patients by requiring full disclosure.

**Concurrence (Arabian, J.)**
- Joins the majority but emphasizes the moral issue of treating human tissue as a commercial commodity.
- Believes the question of whether cells should be treated as property is not for the courts to decide due to the profound moral, philosophical, and religious values at stake.
- Suggests the Legislature is the proper forum to create a licensing scheme for profit sharing.

**Concurring and Dissenting (Broussard, J.)**
- Concurs with the breach of fiduciary duty holding, noting the unusual allegations that the doctor knew the cells' value *before* removal.
- Dissents on the conversion claim, arguing that a patient has a right to determine the use of a body part *before* its removal.
- The Uniform Anatomical Gift Act supports the principle of "donor control" over the use of donated body parts.
- Traditional conversion principles protect against unauthorized use or interference with the right to control property.
- The majority's policy concerns about hindering research are overstated and do not justify abrogating all conversion liability, especially against parties who directly misappropriate a patient's control over their body parts.

**Dissent (Mosk, J.)**
- Disagrees with the majority's conclusion that Moore has no cause of action for conversion.
- Argues that Moore retained an ownership interest in his cells, as "property" is a broad concept, and limitations on certain rights do not destroy ownership.
- California statutory law does not preclude Moore's ownership; Health and Safety Code § 7054.4's "scientific use" does not encompass commercial exploitation.
- The patented cell line is not so factually or legally distinct from Moore's cells to negate his claim, especially since the unauthorized use began before the patent.
- Policy considerations weigh in favor of recognizing conversion:
    - It respects the human body and prevents unjust enrichment of researchers at the patient's expense.
    - The fear of hindering research is overstated; proper record-keeping can mitigate liability, and the biotechnology industry already restricts access to cell lines through patents and trade secrets.
- The Legislature's competence does not relieve courts of their duty to fashion remedies. Existing statutes on organ and blood sales do not prohibit compensation for tissue used in research.
- The nondisclosure cause of action is inadequate because it is difficult to prove causation, only grants a right to refuse consent (not to share in profits), and fails to reach all parties involved in commercial exploitation.
- Criticizes the majority's inclusion of "amateur biology lecture" as improper and irrelevant.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is crucial for understanding the limits of **property rights** in one's own body and the tension between individual autonomy and the advancement of scientific research. It connects to **Moore v. Regents of the University of California** itself, as it is the case. It contrasts with cases like *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* (the case itself) where the court limits property rights in biological materials, but upholds rights related to informed consent. It also touches on the concept of **conversion**, typically applied to tangible personal property, and the court's reluctance to extend it to novel contexts due to policy concerns, similar to how courts might be hesitant to expand other torts.
2. What if Moore had explicitly consented to the use of his cells for research but had stipulated that he would receive a percentage of any profits? Would that create a property interest the court would protect, or would the policy concerns still outweigh it?

**Takeaway:** While a patient does not retain property rights in excised biological materials for conversion claims, physicians have a fiduciary duty to disclose all personal research and economic interests that may influence medical decisions, ensuring informed consent.

## Hecht v. Superior Court (Court of Appeal of the State of California, 1993)

**Facts**
- William E. Kane (decedent) deposited 15 vials of his sperm at California Cryobank, Inc. in October 1991.
- On September 24, 1991, Kane signed a "Specimen Storage Agreement" with the sperm bank, instructing it to continue storing or release specimens to the executor of his estate upon his death. He also authorized release of his specimens to Deborah Ellen Hecht (petitioner), his girlfriend.
- On September 27, 1991, Kane executed a will naming Hecht as executor and bequeathing all rights to his stored sperm to her, stating his wish that she use it to have his child.
- On October 21, 1991, Kane wrote a letter to his children, stating his intention for Hecht to have a child by him after his death using his frozen sperm samples.
- Kane committed suicide on October 30, 1991.
- Robert L. Greene was appointed special administrator of Kane's estate on November 18, 1991.
- Kane's adult children, William E. Kane, Jr. and Katharine E. Kane (real parties in interest), filed will contests on December 3, 1991.
- A "First Settlement Agreement" was orally placed on the record in December 1991 to resolve estate matters, but it did not explicitly mention the sperm. Hecht later alleged this agreement was breached and unenforceable.
- In October 1992, the administrator filed a petition for approval of a "Second Settlement Agreement" which would assign any estate interest in the sperm to Hecht, but this agreement was not finalized due to an objection from a creditor.
- On November 5, 1992, the administrator filed a "Petition for Alternative Relief," seeking instructions from the court regarding the disposition of the sperm, including destruction, distribution to the children, or distribution to Hecht.
- The children argued for destruction of the sperm on public policy grounds, citing concerns about the welfare of potential children and disruption to existing families.
- Hecht argued the sperm was a gift to her, or if an estate asset, should be distributed to her per the will and settlement agreements, and that destruction would violate her procreative rights.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court ordered the sperm destroyed.
- Hecht filed a petition for peremptory writ of mandate/prohibition to vacate the order.

**Issue**
Can a probate court order the destruction of a decedent's cryogenically preserved sperm when the decedent's will bequeathed it to his girlfriend for her use in artificial insemination?

**Holding**
Petitioner (Hecht) wins. The trial court abused its discretion in ordering the destruction of the decedent's sperm. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Probate Court Jurisdiction.** The power of the probate court extends only to the property of the decedent.
- **Property Interest in Sperm.** A decedent has an interest in his stored sperm, in the nature of ownership, to the extent of decision-making authority over its use for reproduction, which constitutes "property" under Probate Code section 62.
- **Public Policy on Artificial Insemination of Unmarried Women.** California public policy does not prohibit artificial insemination of unmarried women.
- **Public Policy on Postmortem Artificial Insemination.** California public policy does not prohibit postmortem artificial insemination.
- **Legislative Role in Reproductive Technology.** It is not the role of the judiciary to inhibit the use of reproductive technology when the Legislature has not done so.

**Reasoning**
- The probate court had jurisdiction over the sperm because the decedent had an ownership interest in it, making it "property" of his estate under Probate Code section 62.
    - The court rejects the argument that *Moore v. Regents of University of California* means the decedent had no ownership interest, as that would remove the sperm from probate jurisdiction.
    - Human biological materials, including sperm, are *sui generis* and not simply abandoned to general personal property law, but they do carry an interest in the nature of ownership for decision-making.
- The trial court's order to destroy the sperm was an abuse of discretion because it was not based on the will, settlement agreements, or any valid public policy.
    - The will clearly expressed the decedent's intent for Hecht to receive the sperm.
    - The court did not adjudicate the validity of the will or settlement agreements, nor did it hold any evidentiary hearing on claims of unsound mind or undue influence.
    - The court rejects the argument that public policy forbids artificial insemination of an unmarried woman.
        - Civil Code section 7005, subdivision (b) was amended to remove the word "married," indicating legislative intent to allow unmarried women to use artificial insemination.
        - There is no state policy preventing the formation of single-parent families.
    - The court rejects the argument that public policy forbids postmortem artificial insemination.
        - There is no California statute or public policy that justifies infringing on gamete-providers' decisional authority regarding postmortem use of sperm.
        - Concerns about psychological or financial burdens on existing family members or society are speculative and not supported by legal authority.
        - The Uniform Status of Children of Assisted Conception Act suggests that posthumously conceived children generally do not inherit from the deceased parent unless explicitly provided for in a will, which addresses some concerns.
- The court emphasizes that it is not adjudicating the validity of the will, sperm bank contract, or settlement agreements, nor is it ordering distribution of the sperm to Hecht, as those issues remain to be resolved.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the evolving concept of "property" in the context of human biological materials, particularly reproductive cells. It distinguishes itself from *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* by finding a sufficient "ownership interest" in sperm for probate jurisdiction, whereas *Moore* found no conversion claim for excised cells used in research. It highlights that while not traditional property, gametes are treated with "special respect" due to their potential for human life. The case also touches on the limits of judicial intervention in reproductive choices when the legislature has not acted.
2. This case leaves open the question of what would happen if the decedent's intent regarding the sperm was unclear or contested, or if there were no will. What if the sperm bank contract had explicitly stated that sperm would be destroyed upon death without further instructions?

**Takeaway:** Cryogenically preserved sperm is considered "property" for probate purposes, and courts generally will not interfere with a decedent's expressed intent for its postmortem use for procreation unless there is a clear, established public policy against it.

## Flynn v. Holder (United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 2012)

**Facts**
- The National Organ Transplant Act (NOTA) was enacted in 1984, prohibiting compensation for "human organs," including "bone marrow."
- At the time NOTA was passed, bone marrow was extracted by aspiration, a painful procedure involving needles inserted into hip bones.
- Over the last twenty years, a new method called "peripheral blood stem cell apheresis" developed, which extracts hematopoietic stem cells from a donor's bloodstream after medication stimulates stem cell production. This method does not involve extracting the soft, fatty marrow from bones.
- Plaintiffs include parents of sick children needing bone marrow transplants, a physician, and a nonprofit organization (MoreMarrowDonors.org).
- MoreMarrowDonors.org proposes a pilot program to offer $3,000 awards (scholarships, housing allowances, or charitable gifts) to minority and mixed-race donors of bone marrow cells, acknowledging this would be "valuable consideration" under NOTA.
- Plaintiffs filed a complaint challenging the constitutionality of NOTA's ban on compensation for bone marrow donations, particularly as applied to peripheral blood stem cell apheresis.

**Procedural History**
- The district court dismissed the complaint for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted.
- Plaintiffs appealed.

**Issue**
Can the National Organ Transplant Act's prohibition on compensating "bone marrow" donors be applied to donations of hematopoietic stem cells extracted from peripheral blood via apheresis?

**Holding**
Plaintiffs (appellants) win. The National Organ Transplant Act does not prohibit compensation for donations of hematopoietic stem cells extracted from peripheral blood via apheresis. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Statutory Interpretation.** Courts must construe the words of a statute to see what they imply, considering ordinary usage.
- **Rational Basis Review.** A legislative classification must have a rational basis, but does not need to fit perfectly with that rational basis, and the basis need merely be rational, not persuasive to all.
- **Rejected: Statutory Definition of "Bone Marrow" Includes Peripheral Blood Stem Cells.** The government's argument that "bone marrow" includes hematopoietic stem cells in the veins as "subparts" of bone marrow is rejected because it would also prohibit compensation for blood donations, which the government concedes are not prohibited.
- **Rejected: Later Statutory Definition Applies.** A definition of "bone marrow" in a later statute (Stem Cell Therapeutic and Research Act of 2005) that includes cells found in peripheral blood is limited to provisions "in this part" of that statute and does not extend to NOTA.

**Reasoning**
- The court construes "bone marrow" in NOTA to mean the soft, fatty substance in bone cavities, consistent with ordinary usage at the time the statute was passed.
- The apheresis method of extracting hematopoietic stem cells does not involve removing the soft, fatty marrow from bones; it extracts cells from the bloodstream.
- The government concedes that compensation for blood donations is not prohibited by NOTA, and hematopoietic stem cells, when in the bloodstream, are a "subpart" of the blood, not the bone marrow.
- If the government's argument that anything originating from bone marrow is a "subpart" of bone marrow were accepted, it would illogically prohibit compensation for all blood donations, as red and white blood cells also originate in the bone marrow.
- The legislative history of NOTA indicates that Congress viewed replenishable tissues like blood as falling outside the definition of "human organ."
- While the prohibition on compensation for bone marrow donations by the aspiration method has a rational basis (policy concerns about commodification, exploitation, and philosophical concerns about bodily integrity), this reasoning does not apply to peripheral blood stem cell apheresis.
- The court does not need to address the constitutional challenge regarding apheresis donations because the statute does not prohibit compensation for them.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding how courts interpret statutory language, especially when new technologies emerge that were not contemplated at the time of enactment. It connects to the concept of **property rights in one's body** and the limits of commodification, similar to issues raised in *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* and *Hecht v. Superior Court*, though *Flynn* focuses on statutory interpretation rather than common law property rights. The court's discussion of the rational basis for prohibiting compensation for traditional bone marrow donations touches on the philosophical and policy arguments against commodification of body parts, which is a recurring theme in property law.
2. The court explicitly avoids deciding the constitutionality of prohibiting compensation for apheresis donations, instead finding that the statute simply doesn't apply. This leaves open the question of whether Congress *could* constitutionally prohibit such compensation if it chose to amend the statute.

**Takeaway:** The National Organ Transplant Act's ban on compensating "bone marrow" donors does not apply to hematopoietic stem cells collected from peripheral blood via apheresis, as these are considered part of the blood, not bone marrow, under the statute's ordinary meaning.

## Ghen v. Rich (United States District Court for the District of Massachusetts, 1881)

**Facts**
- In the early spring months, fin-back whales frequent the easterly part of Massachusetts Bay.
- Fishermen from Provincetown pursue these whales in open boats and shoot them with bomb-lances.
- When killed, the whales sink but rise to the surface in one to three days.
- The person who finds a stranded whale usually sends word to Provincetown and receives a small salvage payment.
- Each boat's crew has a unique mark on its lances, identifying who killed a whale.
- The usage on Cape Cod for many years has been that the person who kills a whale in this manner owns it.
- On April 9, 1880, the libellant shot and instantly killed a fin-back whale with a bomb-lance near Cape Cod.
- The whale sank immediately.
- On April 12, the whale was found stranded on a beach in Brewster, 17 miles from where it was killed, by one Ellis.
- Ellis advertised the whale for sale at auction and sold it to the respondent.
- The respondent shipped off the blubber and tried out the oil.
- On April 13, the libellant heard the whale had been found and sent his men to claim it.
- Neither the respondent nor Ellis knew the libellant had killed the whale, but they knew or could have known it was killed by a bomb-lance by someone in the business.

**Procedural History**
- The libellant brought a libel action to recover the value of the whale.
- The court found for the libellant.

**Issue**
Can a whaler claim ownership of a whale they killed, even if it was found and processed by another party, based on a local custom?

**Holding**
Libellant (whaler) wins. The usage among whalers on Cape Cod, where the killer of a fin-back whale owns it, is reasonable and valid, establishing the libellant's property right. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **First Possession (Wild Animals).** A wild animal does not become property until firm possession has been established by the taker.
- **Usage/Custom.** A long-standing, reasonable custom that embraces an entire business and is generally concurred in by those engaged in the trade can establish property rights.
- Rejected: **Finder's Keepers (Wild Animals).** The mere finding of a wild animal, even if adrift, does not automatically confer ownership if a prior taker has established possession.

**Reasoning**
- The court found the usage on Cape Cod, where the killer owns the whale, to be reasonable and valid.
    - The usage requires the only act of appropriation possible given the nature of fin-back whaling (killing with a bomb-lance).
    - Without this usage, the industry would cease because the fruits of labor could be appropriated by chance finders.
    - The usage provides reasonable salvage for securing or reporting the property.
    - The usage has been recognized and acquiesced in for many years, demonstrating its practical effectiveness.
    - Its application is extremely limited and affects few people, so it does not disturb general understanding.
- The court distinguished this case from others where actual physical possession or firm attachment was maintained.
    - In *Taber v. Jenny*, the whale was anchored and marked, showing appropriation.
    - In *Bartlett v. Budd*, the first taker had killed and taken actual possession, and ownership vested.
- The court referenced *Swift v. Gifford*, which upheld a custom that "the iron holds the whale," finding it reasonable and valid.
    - This custom, like the one in the present case, is limited in application and specific to the whaling industry.
- The court noted that even without usage, common law might reach the same result, as doing all that is possible to make the animal one's own could be sufficient for ownership. *(dicta)*
- The respondent was liable for conversion because the libellant had property in the whale.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a foundational case for **first possession** of wild animals, particularly in the context of custom. It contrasts with cases like *Pierson v. Post* (not on our list, but a classic comparison) which emphasizes actual physical capture, by showing how custom can define what constitutes "possession" or "appropriation" in specific industries. It also connects to the idea of **constructive possession** where physical control isn't always necessary if intent and effort are clear.
2. This case raises the question of how far custom can extend to define property rights, especially when it might seem to contradict a more general common law principle (like "finder's keepers"). What if the custom was less reasonable or only benefited a very small, exclusive group?

**Takeaway:** Local custom can define what constitutes sufficient appropriation for establishing property rights in wild animals, especially when that custom is reasonable, long-standing, and essential for the industry's viability.

## United States v. Corrow (United States Court of Appeals, Tenth Circuit, 1997)

**Facts**
- Ray Winnie, a Navajo religious singer, used Yei B'Chei (ceremonial masks) for over 25 years.
- Traditionally, Yei B'Chei are passed to family members or loaned to other clans.
- When Mr. Winnie died in 1991, he made no provision for the Yei B'Chei, and no family member requested them.
- Richard Corrow, owner of Artifacts Display Stands, visited Mr. Winnie's widow, Mrs. Fannie Winnie, in August 1993.
- Mr. Corrow inquired about the Yei B'Chei, and the Winnie family eventually showed him 22 masks.
- Mr. Corrow offered $5,000 for the Yei B'Chei, claiming he would deliver them to a young Navajo chanter in Utah to keep them sacred.
- Mr. Corrow agreed to the family's price of $10,000 for the Yei B'Chei, five headdresses, and other artifacts.
- Mrs. Winnie, who spoke no English, placed her thumbprint on a receipt after her granddaughter read it to her in Navajo.
- In November 1994, Mr. Corrow was contacted by the East-West Trading Company about a wealthy Chicago surgeon interested in purchasing Yei B'Chei.
- The purported buyer was an undercover National Park Service ranger, James Tanner, investigating questionable trade.
- Agent Tanner saw photographs of 17 of the 22 Yei B'Chei Mr. Corrow purchased, noting eagle and owl feathers.
- Agent Tanner agreed to a purchase price of $70,000 for the Yei B'Chei, with $50,000 for Mr. Corrow.
- On December 9, 1994, Mr. Corrow arrived in Albuquerque with suitcases and a box containing Navajo religious objects, bundles, herbs, prayer sticks, and Yei B'Chei adorned with eagle and owl feathers.
- FBI agents executed a search warrant in Santa Fe and found the items.

**Procedural History**
- Mr. Corrow was charged with trafficking in Native American cultural items (Count 1) and selling protected bird feathers (Count 2).
- The district court denied Mr. Corrow's pretrial motion to dismiss Count 1, which alleged the definition of "cultural patrimony" in NAGPRA was unconstitutionally vague.
- The jury convicted Mr. Corrow of Count 1 (illegal trafficking in cultural items).
- The jury acquitted Mr. Corrow of Count 2 (selling protected feathers) but found him guilty of the lesser included offense of possession of protected feathers.
- The district court denied Mr. Corrow's post-trial motion challenging the constitutionality of NAGPRA and the sufficiency of the evidence.
- Mr. Corrow was sentenced to two concurrent five-year probationary terms and 100 hours of community service.
- Mr. Corrow appealed to the Tenth Circuit.

**Issue**
Is the definition of "cultural patrimony" in the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA) unconstitutionally vague as applied to Mr. Corrow?

**Holding**
The government (appellee) wins. The definition of "cultural patrimony" in NAGPRA is not unconstitutionally vague as applied to Mr. Corrow. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Void-for-Vagueness Doctrine.** A penal statute must define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness for ordinary people to understand what conduct is prohibited and in a manner that does not encourage arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement.
- **Vagueness Challenges (Non-First Amendment).** Vagueness challenges to statutes not involving First Amendment freedoms must be examined in light of the facts of the case at hand.
- **Facial Challenge (Non-First Amendment).** In a facial challenge raising no First Amendment or other claim that the act reaches constitutionally protected conduct, the complainant must demonstrate that the law is impermissibly vague in all of its applications.
- **NAGPRA Definition of Cultural Patrimony.** An object is "cultural patrimony" if it has ongoing historical, traditional, or cultural importance central to the Native American group or culture itself, rather than property owned by an individual Native American, and which, therefore, cannot be alienated, appropriated, or conveyed by any individual regardless of whether or not the individual is a member of the Indian tribe or Native Hawaiian organization, and such object shall have been considered inalienable by such Native American group at the time the object was separated from such group.
- **Scienter Requirement.** A statutory requirement that an act must be willful or purposeful may mitigate a criminal law's vagueness by ensuring it punishes only those who are aware their conduct is unlawful.
- **Migratory Bird Treaty Act (MBTA) Misdemeanor Violations.** Misdemeanor violations under 16 U.S.C. § 703 are strict liability crimes, meaning proof of scienter is not required.

**Reasoning**
- Mr. Corrow cannot meet the burden of demonstrating the law is impermissibly vague in all its applications because his conduct falls squarely within the core of the Act.
- **Fair Notice:**
    - The district court found Mr. Corrow knowledgeable about Navajo traditions and culture and aware that tribal members viewed ownership differently.
    - Mr. Corrow had inquired about prices of Navajo artifacts from an author who expressed objections to commercial sales of cultural objects and reminded him of NAGPRA.
    - Mr. Corrow's representation that he wanted to buy the Yei B'Chei to pass them to another chanter suggests he understood their inherent inalienability in Navajo culture.
    - While experts differed on alienability, none testified it was acceptable to sell Yei B'Chei to non-Navajos for profit.
    - The Yei B'Chei were powerfully connected to Navajo religion and culture, and Mr. Corrow had notice of this.
    - The statute's scienter requirement ("knowingly sells, purchases, uses for profit") further ensures that only those aware of their unlawful conduct are punished.
- **Arbitrary and Discriminatory Enforcement:**
    - The statute, as applied, requires law enforcement officers to consult with tribal officials to determine if an item is cultural patrimony.
    - In this case, a National Park Service officer examined photographs and discussed the Yei B'Chei's significance with knowledgeable Park Service officers and Navajo Nation representatives before determining they were cultural patrimony.
    - This consultation process provides sufficient guidance to law enforcement, dispelling fears of subjective enforcement.
- The evidence was sufficient for a rational jury to find beyond a reasonable doubt that the Yei B'Chei are cultural patrimony.
- The court joins other circuits in holding that misdemeanor violations under MBTA § 703 are strict liability crimes, meaning scienter is not required for possession.
    - The plain language of § 703 makes simple possession unlawful.
    - The penalties are small, and there is no grave harm to an offender's reputation.
    - Even if scienter were required, the evidence showed Mr. Corrow knowingly possessed the feathers.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the **Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA)** and the concept of **cultural patrimony**. It highlights how a statute can define property rights in a way that prioritizes communal, inalienable cultural significance over individual ownership, even when individual tribal members might believe they have the right to alienate such items. This contrasts with traditional Western property law concepts of individual ownership and alienability. It also touches on the **void-for-vagueness doctrine** in criminal law, which is relevant to how property rights and restrictions are defined.
2. The conflicting expert testimony regarding the alienability of the Yei B'Chei within Navajo culture raises a tension: how should courts reconcile differing interpretations of cultural practices when applying a statute that relies on those practices for its definitions? What if the "group itself cannot agree on that point," as Mr. Corrow argued?

**Takeaway:** The court upheld the constitutionality of NAGPRA's definition of "cultural patrimony" against a vagueness challenge, emphasizing that the defendant had sufficient notice of the cultural significance and inalienability of the items, and that the statute's enforcement mechanisms provided adequate guidance.

## Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois (Supreme Court of the United States, 1892)

**Facts**
- In 1818, Illinois was admitted to the Union on equal footing with the original states.
- In 1850, Congress granted Illinois a right of way and land to aid in the construction of a railroad from the Illinois and Michigan Canal to Cairo, with branches to Chicago and Dubuque.
- In 1851, the Illinois legislature accepted the Congressional grant and incorporated the Illinois Central Railroad Company (ICRR). ICRR's charter granted it a right of way up to 200 feet wide and the right to use state lands and waters for railroad purposes, but prohibited it from interrupting navigation of streams and from locating its road within any city without the city's consent.
- In 1852, the City of Chicago passed an ordinance granting ICRR permission to lay tracks along the lakefront, specifying the location and requiring ICRR to build a breakwater and other structures to protect the city from the lake. The ordinance also stated that ICRR could not occupy or use the "Lake Park" area except for construction or repair, nor obstruct the view of the lake. ICRR accepted this ordinance and entered into a contract with the city.
- ICRR constructed its tracks and breakwater as permitted, reclaiming land from the lake.
- In 1855 and 1856, Chicago passed additional ordinances granting ICRR permission to curve its tracks and use additional space for its station grounds.
- In 1869, the Illinois legislature passed the "Lake Front Act," which confirmed ICRR's existing rights and granted it in fee "all the right and title of the State of Illinois in and to the submerged lands constituting the bed of Lake Michigan, and lying east of the tracks and breakwater of the Illinois Central Railroad Company, for the distance of one mile." The act stipulated that ICRR could not sell the fee, and that the grant would not authorize obstructions to Chicago harbor or impair public navigation rights. ICRR accepted this act in 1870.
- In 1873, the Illinois legislature repealed the Lake Front Act.
- ICRR constructed several piers between 1872 and 1885.
- In 1883, the State of Illinois filed a lawsuit against ICRR and the City of Chicago to determine the rights to the reclaimed and submerged lands.

**Procedural History**
- The State of Illinois filed a bill in equity in Illinois Circuit Court against ICRR and the City of Chicago.
- ICRR removed the case to the Circuit Court of the United States for the Northern District of Illinois.
- The City of Chicago filed a cross-bill against the State and ICRR.
- The Circuit Court of the United States issued a decree.
- ICRR, the State, and the City each appealed the final decree to the Supreme Court of the United States.

**Issue**
Can the State of Illinois grant a private railroad corporation title to a large portion of the submerged lands under Lake Michigan in Chicago's harbor, thereby relinquishing its control over those lands?

**Holding**
The State (plaintiff) wins. The State cannot abdicate its trust over the submerged lands of Lake Michigan in Chicago's harbor, and the attempted grant to ICRR was invalid. Affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Public Trust Doctrine.** The ownership of and dominion and sovereignty over lands covered by navigable waters belong to the respective states, held in trust for the public to enjoy navigation, commerce, and fishing, free from private obstruction.
- **Navigable Waters.** The doctrine of state ownership and sovereignty over lands under navigable waters applies to the Great Lakes, just as it does to tide waters.
- **State Legislative Power over Public Trust.** The State can grant parcels of submerged lands for the improvement of public interests (e.g., wharves, docks, piers) or when such disposition does not substantially impair the public interest in the remaining lands and waters.
- **Abdication of Public Trust.** A state cannot abdicate its general control over lands under the navigable waters of an entire harbor or bay, or of a sea or lake, as this is inconsistent with its trust to preserve such waters for public use.
- **Revocable Grants.** Any grant by the State that attempts to abdicate its public trust over navigable waters is necessarily revocable, and the State can resume the exercise of that trust at any time.
- **Riparian Rights.** Riparian rights are incident to riparian ownership and attach to land contiguous to navigable water, allowing the owner to access the navigable part of the water and construct wharves or piers for their own or public use, subject to legislative regulation and terminating at the point of navigability.

**Reasoning**
- The State of Illinois holds title to the submerged lands under Lake Michigan in trust for the public, similar to how states hold title to tidelands under common law.
- This public trust requires the State to preserve these waters for public navigation, commerce, and fishing.
- While the State can grant small parcels of submerged lands for improvements that aid navigation and commerce, or when such grants do not substantially impair the public interest, it cannot abdicate its general control over an entire harbor.
- The 1869 Lake Front Act, which granted ICRR over a thousand acres of submerged lands, effectively transferred control of nearly the entire harbor to a private corporation. This is an abdication of the State's public trust.
- Such an extensive grant to a private corporation, especially one created for a different purpose (railroad transportation), is inconsistent with the State's governmental trust and cannot be defended.
- The grant was not for a limited period or for specific improvements that would benefit the public interest without substantial impairment of the remaining public domain.
- The limitations in the 1869 act (no obstructions, no impairment of navigation, regulation of wharfage rates) did not prevent the abdication of control, as ICRR could still indefinitely delay improvements or construct works for its own profit.
- The 1873 repealing act was valid and effective because the 1869 act, to the extent it attempted to abdicate the State's sovereignty and dominion over the submerged lands, was inoperative. There can be no irrepealable contract in a conveyance of property by a grantor in disregard of a public trust.
- ICRR's existing structures (tracks, breakwater) built under the 1852 ordinance are permissible because they were constructed under legal authority and do not interfere with useful freedom in the use of the lake for commerce.
- ICRR's riparian rights, derived from its ownership of certain lakefront lots, allow it to construct piers and docks up to the point of navigability. The lower court must determine if ICRR's piers extend beyond this point.
- The City of Chicago, as riparian owner of public grounds on the lakefront, has the power to construct wharves, docks, and levees, subject to state and federal authority.

**Dissent (Mr. Justice Shiras, Mr. Justice Gray, Mr. Justice Brown)**
- The dissent argues that a State's ownership of lands under navigable waters is as complete as its ownership of other public lands, and it has the full power to convey such lands.
- The validity of a grant should not depend on the size of the parcel, and the grant to ICRR, though large, was not unreasonable given the railroad's need for terminal facilities.
- The extent of such a grant and its effect on public interests are matters of legislative discretion.
- The 1869 act constituted a valid contract, and the State cannot arbitrarily revoke it without infringing the Contract Clause of the U.S. Constitution.
- The limitations in the grant (no obstruction to harbor, no impairment of navigation) are incidents of the grant and do not defeat it.
- If the State wishes to regain control, it must do so through constitutional condemnation and compensation, not by simply repealing the grant.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a foundational case for the **Public Trust Doctrine** in American property law. It establishes that states hold title to navigable waters and the lands beneath them in trust for the public, and this trust cannot be entirely alienated to private parties. This connects to cases like *Ghen v. Rich* and *United States v. Corrow* in discussing public rights and common resources, but here the focus is on the state's role as trustee. It also touches on the concept of **riparian rights**, which we saw in *Evans v. Merriweather* and *Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.*, but here in the context of a large commercial entity and a public trust.
2. The dissent raises the question of whether the majority's ruling on the revocability of the grant, even if it is an abdication of public trust, conflicts with the Contract Clause of the U.S. Constitution. This tension between state sovereignty/public trust and the protection of contracts is a key point for discussion.

**Takeaway:** States hold navigable waters and their submerged lands in an inalienable public trust, meaning they cannot grant away control of an entire harbor to a private entity.

## State ex rel. Thornton v. Hay (Oregon Supreme Court, 1969)

**Facts**
- William and Georgianna Hay (defendants) owned a tourist facility at Cannon Beach, Oregon.
- Their property included a "dry-sand area" between the sixteen-foot elevation contour line and the ordinary high-tide line of the Pacific Ocean.
- The public had used this dry-sand area for recreational purposes, such as picnics, gathering wood, and building fires, since the beginning of Oregon's political history.
- State and local officers policed the dry sand, and municipal sanitary crews maintained it.
- The dry-sand area is unstable, unsafe during winter storms, and generally unfit for permanent structures.
- In 1967, the Oregon legislature passed ORS 390.610, declaring a public policy to preserve public use of the seashore and ocean beaches.
- The Hays sought to construct fences or other improvements in the dry-sand area of their property.
- The State of Oregon, through Robert Y. Thornton (plaintiff), sought to enjoin the Hays from constructing these improvements.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court found in favor of the state, ruling that the public had acquired an easement for recreational purposes in the dry-sand area.
- The Hays appealed.
- The Oregon Supreme Court affirmed the trial court's decree.

**Issue**
Can the state prevent landowners from enclosing the dry-sand area of their ocean-front property if the public has historically used the area for recreation?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (State) wins. The public has a right to use the dry-sand area of ocean beaches for recreational purposes based on the **doctrine of custom**. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Public Easement by Custom.** A public right to use land can be established by long-standing custom if the custom is ancient, exercised without interruption, peaceable and free from dispute, reasonable, certain, obligatory, and not repugnant to other laws or customs.
- Rejected: **Implied Dedication.** This doctrine requires an intent to dedicate, which was not present here.
- Rejected: **Prescriptive Easement for the Public.** While the public can acquire prescriptive easements in Oregon, the court found a "better legal basis" in custom for this case, which applies more broadly than tract-by-tract prescription.

**Reasoning**
- The dry-sand area has been used by the general public for recreational purposes since the beginning of Oregon's political history, satisfying the "ancient" requirement of custom.
- The public's use of the dry-sand area has been continuous and uninterrupted by private landowners.
- The use has been peaceable and free from dispute, with municipal officers intervening when inappropriate uses occurred.
- The use is reasonable, appropriate to the land, and consistent with community usages.
- The boundaries of the dry-sand area are visible and certain, and the character of the land limits its use to foreshore-connected recreational activities.
- The custom is obligatory, as the public's use has never been questioned by upland owners as long as they remained on the dry sand.
- The custom is not repugnant to other laws or customs.
- The public's long-standing use of the dry-sand area was so notorious that landowners must be presumed to have notice of it.
- The unique nature of the land (unstable, unsafe for construction) and the uniform treatment of ocean-front lands across the state make custom a more appropriate doctrine than prescription.
- The court did not need to consider the constitutionality of ORS 390.640 as a zoning regulation because the public easement was established. *(dicta)*

**Concurrence (Denecke, J.)**
- Disagrees with basing the decision on the English doctrine of "customary rights" because the facts don't fit its ancient outlines.
- Believes "custom" as an analogy, combined with long public usage, universal belief in the right to use, long acquiescence by owners, and the extreme desirability of the right, establishes the public's right.
- Cites cases where public rights in navigable waters and tidelands (jus publicum) persist despite private ownership, even without explicit reservation in title.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for introducing the **doctrine of custom** as a means for the public to acquire rights in private land, particularly in the context of environmental protection and public access to natural resources. It contrasts with **prescriptive easements** (which are typically for specific individuals or a defined group) and **implied dedication** (which requires owner intent). It connects to cases like *Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois* by demonstrating how public rights can limit private property interests, even those held by record title.
2. The court explicitly leaves open the effect of "consent" on prescription, suggesting that if landowners tacitly allow public use, it might not be considered "adverse" for prescription purposes, but it is consistent with custom. This raises a question about the interplay between these doctrines.

**Takeaway:** The public can acquire recreational rights in private land through long-standing custom, even if the landowner holds record title.

## Evans v. Merriweather (Illinois Supreme Court, 1842)

**Facts**
- In 1834, Smith & Baker bought land with a branch (small stream) and built a steam mill, relying on a well and the branch for water.
- One or two years later, John Evans bought land on the same branch, immediately upstream from Smith & Baker, and built a steam mill, also relying on a well and the branch for water.
- In 1836 or 1837, Smith & Baker sold their mill to Merriweather.
- In the fall of 1837, during a drought, the branch did not provide enough water to continuously run Evans's upstream mill.
- Evans instructed his workers not to stop or divert the water in the branch.
- One of Evans's workers built a dam across the branch below Evans's mill, diverting all the water into Evans's well.
- Evans lived half a mile from the mill and was frequently there.
- After the diversion, the branch below Evans's mill went dry, and Merriweather's mill could only run about one day a week, supplied by his well.
- Merriweather sued Evans three or four weeks after the dam was built.
- The branch usually provided enough water for both mills without significantly affecting the current.
- At the time of the grievance, Evans also had water hauled to supply his boilers.
- The dam diverted both the natural branch water and the hauled water poured into the branch above Evans's well.

**Procedural History**
- Merriweather (plaintiff) sued Evans (defendant) in the Greene Circuit Court.
- The jury returned a verdict for Merriweather for $150 damages, and judgment was entered.
- Evans appealed to the Illinois Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Can an upstream riparian proprietor divert an entire stream for manufacturing purposes if it deprives a downstream proprietor of water necessary for their manufacturing, even if the upstream proprietor instructed their worker not to divert the water?

**Holding**
Merriweather (appellee) wins. An upstream riparian proprietor cannot entirely consume the water of a stream for artificial purposes if it injures a downstream proprietor. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Riparian Rights (Usufructuary).** The property in water, by virtue of riparian ownership, is usufructuary, consisting of the advantage of its impetus rather than the fluid itself.
- **Reasonable Use Doctrine.** A riparian proprietor has a right to use the water of a stream but must do so without injury to other riparian proprietors.
- **Natural vs. Artificial Wants.** Human wants for water are categorized as natural (absolutely necessary for existence, e.g., drinking, household, livestock) or artificial (increase comfort and prosperity, e.g., irrigation, manufacturing).
- **Priority of Natural Wants.** If a stream is small and does not furnish enough water for all proprietors' natural wants, none can use it for irrigation or manufacturing.
- **Shared Benefit for Artificial Wants.** Where water is not needed for natural wants, and there is not enough for all proprietors' manufacturing purposes, all have a right to participate in its benefit, and no one has a right to use all the water.
- **Jury Determination of Reasonable Use.** When all have a right to participate in a common benefit and no exclusive enjoyment, a jury must determine if a party has used more than their just proportion.
- **Employer Liability for Servant's Illegal Act.** If a party avails themselves of the illegal act of their servant, the law presumes they authorized it.
- **Appellate Review of Damages.** Where damages are small and justice has been done, the Supreme Court ought not to remand a cause even if jury instructions were not perfectly correct.

**Reasoning**
- The court applies the **reasonable use doctrine**, stating that a riparian proprietor must use water in a way that causes as little injury as possible to those below, consistent with a valuable benefit to themselves.
- The court distinguishes between natural and artificial wants for water.
    - Natural wants (drinking, household, livestock) are essential for existence.
    - Artificial wants (irrigation, manufacturing) increase comfort but are not essential.
- An upstream proprietor can consume all water for natural wants if necessary.
- However, for artificial wants, if there is a lower proprietor whose natural wants are essential, the upstream proprietor must leave enough water for them.
- When water is not needed for natural wants, and there isn't enough for all proprietors' manufacturing, no single proprietor can use all the water; all have a right to participate.
- The complete diversion of the stream by Evans for his mill's artificial use, which deprived Merriweather's mill of water, was an illegal injury.
- Even though Evans claimed he forbade the diversion, the jury could infer his knowledge and acquiescence because he lived nearby and was often at the mill.
- By benefiting from the diversion, Evans is presumed to have authorized it.
- The court affirmed the judgment despite potential issues with jury instructions because the damages were small and justice was served. *(dicta)*

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes the **reasonable use doctrine** for riparian rights in Illinois, distinguishing between natural and artificial uses of water. It contrasts with the "natural flow" theory (which would allow no diminution) and the "prior appropriation" doctrine (which grants rights based on first use). This case is foundational for understanding how common law balances competing interests in shared resources. It connects to **Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.** which adopted the prior appropriation doctrine, highlighting the different approaches states take to water rights.
2. The court states that "no rule... can be laid down, as to how much each may use" for artificial wants when there isn't enough for all, leaving it to the jury. This leaves significant ambiguity regarding the precise allocation of water rights in such scenarios. [CHECK: How does a jury determine "just proportion" without a clear rule?]

**Takeaway:** Riparian owners have a right to reasonable use of water, prioritizing natural needs over artificial ones, and cannot entirely consume water for artificial purposes to the detriment of downstream users.

## Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co. (Colorado Supreme Court, 1882)

**Facts**
- Appellee (Left Hand Ditch Co.) appropriated water from the south fork of the St. Vrain creek and diverted it via a ditch to James creek, then to Left Hand creek, where it was used to irrigate lands.
- Appellants (Coffin et al.) owned lands along the St. Vrain creek below the south fork, which were naturally irrigated by the St. Vrain.
- In 1879, there was not enough water in the St. Vrain to supply both the appellee's ditch and irrigate the appellants' lands.
- Appellants tore out a portion of appellee's dam, interfering with its water diversion.

**Procedural History**
- Appellee sued appellants for damages from trespass and for injunctive relief.
- Appellants raised several defenses, including a prior agreement and riparian rights.
- The trial court sustained demurrers to all defenses except a general denial.
- A jury trial resulted in a verdict and judgment for appellee for damages to the dam.
- Appellants appealed.

**Issue**
- Does the common law doctrine of riparian rights apply in Colorado, or does the doctrine of prior appropriation govern water rights?

**Holding**
- Appellee wins. The doctrine of prior appropriation, not riparian rights, governs water rights in Colorado, and the location of water use does not affect the right. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Prior Appropriation Doctrine.** The first appropriator of water from a natural stream for a beneficial purpose has a prior right to that water, to the extent of the appropriation.
- **Riparian Rights (Rejected).** The common law doctrine giving riparian owners a right to the flow of water in its natural channel, even without beneficial use, is inapplicable in Colorado.
- **Locus of Use.** The right to water acquired by prior appropriation is not dependent on the location where the water is applied to beneficial use.
- **Protection of Water Rights.** The right to water by priority of appropriation is protected even after the land over which the stream flows is patented to a third party, regardless of whether it's mentioned in the patent.

**Reasoning**
- Colorado's dry climate makes artificial irrigation an absolute necessity for agriculture.
    - Water, when appropriated, becomes a distinct usufructuary estate, not merely an incident to the soil.
    - Denying prior appropriation would destroy the value of vast property developed with the understanding that such rights would be protected.
- The doctrine of prior appropriation existed in Colorado from the earliest appropriations, predating specific legislation and the constitution.
    - Congressional acts protecting appropriated water rights recognized pre-existing local customs and laws, rather than creating new rights.
- The common law doctrine of riparian proprietorship is unsuitable for Colorado due to imperative necessity.
- The territorial legislature recognized the doctrine of prior appropriation in statutes, even if not explicitly stated in the earliest ones.
    - Statutes referring to "legal rights" and "priority of right" imply recognition of prior appropriation.
- The right of prior appropriation is not dependent on the location of the beneficial use.
    - It would be inequitable to deprive someone of water simply because they transported it over a watershed to cultivate land in another valley.
    - Topography, distance, expense, and water quantity might make it impossible or impracticable to use water from an adjacent stream.
    - Applying a location-based rule would prevent productive cultivation and waste water on sterile lands.
- Statutory provisions regarding water use "along the line of said stream" or "on the bank, margin or neighborhood" refer to existing uses and detriments at the time of diversion, not future potential uses.
    - "Detriment" only exists if water was previously appropriated or used.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is foundational for understanding the **prior appropriation doctrine** in water law, which contrasts sharply with the **riparian rights** doctrine. It establishes that in arid regions, the first to divert and use water beneficially gains a superior right, regardless of land ownership along the stream. This is a significant departure from common law principles, emphasizing the practical needs of a specific environment over traditional property concepts. It connects to cases like *Evans v. Merriweather* which discusses reasonable use under riparian rights, showing a different approach to water allocation.
2. What if a prior appropriator's use becomes wasteful over time, or a new, more efficient use emerges? Does the "beneficial purpose" requirement evolve, and if so, how is that determined without undermining the certainty of prior rights?

**Takeaway:** In arid regions like Colorado, the necessity of irrigation led to the adoption of the prior appropriation doctrine, granting superior water rights to the first beneficial user, irrespective of land ownership along the stream or the location of water use.

## Higday v. Nickolaus (Kansas City Court of Appeals, 1971)

**Facts**
- Plaintiffs own about 6000 acres of farmland in the McBaine Bottom, overlying an alluvial water basin.
- This basin contains a large underground reservoir of percolating water, which maintains a high water table, benefiting plaintiffs' crops and providing water for personal and livestock use.
- Since 1948, the City of Columbia has sought a new water source due to dwindling supplies.
- The City decided to withdraw water from the McBaine Bottom via shallow wells and transport it 12 miles away for sale.
- In December 1966, City voters approved a bond issue for this project.
- Scientific analysis showed the water table is normally 10 feet below the surface, moves at 2 feet per day, and displaces 10.5 million gallons daily.
- The City acquired five well sites totaling 17.25 acres from some plaintiffs through threat of condemnation.
- The City plans to extract 11.5 million gallons of groundwater daily for sale, which will lower the water table from 10 feet to 20 feet below the surface.
- Plaintiffs allege this will deprive them of water for their crops, livestock, and personal use, eventually making their land arid.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs filed a Petition for Declaratory Judgment and Injunction in trial court.
- The trial court dismissed the petition based on the City's motion, which argued the petition failed to plead a justiciable controversy or a claim for relief.
- Plaintiffs appealed the dismissal.

**Issue**
- Can a city extract percolating groundwater from land it owns for sale and transport away from the land, if doing so will injure adjacent landowners by lowering the water table and depriving them of water for beneficial use of their own land?

**Holding**
- Plaintiffs (landowners) win. The City may not extract percolating groundwater for sale away from the land if it injures adjacent landowners' beneficial use of their land. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Declaratory Judgment.** A petition for declaratory judgment must allege facts showing a subsisting justiciable controversy between parties regarding their rights and duties, allowing for specific, conclusive relief.
- **Injunction.** An injunction requires a substantial legal right to be protected, a wrongful and injurious invasion (or threatened invasion) of that right, and often irreparable harm with no adequate legal remedy.
- **Percolating Waters.** Waters that pass through the ground without a definite channel, oozing, seeping, or filtering through subsurface strata. All underground waters are presumed to be percolating.
- **English Common Law Rule (Percolating Waters).** A landowner has absolute ownership of percolating waters under their land and may withdraw any quantity for any purpose without liability, even if it drains water from adjoining lands.
- **Rejected: English Common Law Rule (Percolating Waters).** The court rejects the application of the English rule in Missouri.
- **American Rule / Rule of Reasonable Use (Percolating Waters).** An overlying landowner has a proprietary interest in the water under their land, but this ownership is usufructuary and restricted. They may use groundwater freely without liability to an adjoining owner only if the use is for purposes incident to the beneficial enjoyment of the land from which the water was taken.
    - Under this rule, an overlying owner, including a municipality, may not withdraw percolating water and transport it for sale or other use away from the land if it impairs an adjoining landowner's supply to their injury. Such a use is unreasonable.
- **Correlative Rights Rule.** (Distinguished from reasonable use) A doctrine based on proportionate sharing of withdrawals among landowners overlying a common basin, where overlying owners have no proprietary interest in the water itself. (California rule).
- **Comparative Injury Doctrine (Injunctions).** When considering injunctive relief, a court must weigh the relative convenience and inconvenience, and the comparative injuries to the parties and the public. An injunction may be denied if it would cause great public harm compared to the injury complained of, even if the legal remedy is inadequate.

**Reasoning**
- The petition sufficiently pleads a justiciable controversy because it describes an actual, existing dispute where the City's planned actions will inevitably damage plaintiffs, creating uncertainty about their rights.
    - The City has committed significant resources and acquired well sites, indicating the threat is impending and real, not hypothetical.
- The court rejects the City's argument that the English common law rule of absolute ownership applies in Missouri, which would make plaintiffs' alleged damage *damnum absque injuria*.
    - The English rule was established in 1843, after Missouri adopted common law in 1816, so it is not binding.
    - The only Missouri case on point, *Springfield Waterworks Co. v. Jenkins* (1895), did not definitively adopt the absolute ownership rule and its language actually recognized limitations akin to reasonable use.
    - Modern scientific understanding discredits the common law's premises about the unknowable nature of groundwater.
    - Missouri's increasing population and water demands necessitate a more equitable allocation standard.
- The court adopts the **rule of reasonable use** for percolating waters.
    - This rule aligns with the maxim that one must use their property so as not to injure another.
    - It allows use for beneficial enjoyment of the overlying land but prohibits withdrawal for sale or transport away from the land if it injures an adjoining landowner.
    - A municipality acting as a private owner cannot claim special rights to extract water at the expense of adjoining landowners.
- Plaintiffs' petition sufficiently alleges a property right to reasonable use of percolating waters threatened by wrongful invasion, thus stating a claim for injunctive relief.
    - The City's threatened appropriation of private property for public use without authority or compliance with constitutional conditions is an irremediable invasion of rights, justifying injunctive consideration without typical requirements of irreparable damage or inadequate legal remedy.
- While injunctive relief is discretionary, the trial court must consider the **comparative injury** to all parties and the public.
    - The public's need for water is vital, and the court may deny an injunction if it would cause great public harm compared to the plaintiffs' injury.
    - The trial court could condition an injunction on the City exercising its power of eminent domain to acquire the water rights, or plaintiffs could pursue inverse condemnation.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important because it rejects the **English Common Law Rule** of absolute ownership for percolating groundwater and adopts the **American Rule of Reasonable Use**. This shifts the focus from unlimited extraction to a more equitable balancing of landowners' rights, especially when a municipality seeks to export water. It connects to broader themes of property rights limitations and the evolving understanding of natural resources. It contrasts with cases like *Edwards v. Allouez Mining Co.* where the court refused to enjoin a nuisance, but here the court is willing to consider an injunction for water rights.
2. The court leaves open the question of whether an injunction will ultimately be granted, emphasizing the **comparative injury** doctrine. This raises a tension: how much weight should be given to public need (municipal water supply) versus private property rights (farmers' water for crops)? What if the City's entire water supply depends on this source?

**Takeaway:** Missouri adopts the reasonable use rule for percolating groundwater, meaning landowners (including municipalities) cannot extract water for sale away from the land if it harms adjacent landowners' beneficial use of their property.

## Intel Corp. v. Hamidi (Supreme Court of California, 2003)

**Facts**
- Intel Corporation (Intel) maintained an electronic mail system for employees, allowing reasonable nonbusiness use.
- Kourosh Kenneth Hamidi, a former Intel employee, formed FACE-Intel to criticize Intel's employment practices.
- Over 21 months, Hamidi sent six mass emails to thousands of Intel employees using Intel's email system.
- Hamidi did not breach Intel's computer security to obtain addresses and offered to remove recipients from his mailing list upon request.
- Hamidi's emails did not physically damage or functionally disrupt Intel's computers, but they caused discussion among employees and managers.
- Intel demanded Hamidi stop sending emails in March 1998, but he sent another mass mailing in September 1998.
- Intel staff spent time trying to block Hamidi's messages, and some employees asked management to stop them.

**Procedural History**
- Intel sued Hamidi and FACE-Intel for trespass to chattels and nuisance, seeking damages and an injunction.
- Intel later dismissed the nuisance claim and waived damages.
- The trial court granted Intel's motion for summary judgment and permanently enjoined Hamidi from sending unsolicited emails to Intel's systems.
- A divided Court of Appeal affirmed the injunction.
- Hamidi appealed to the Supreme Court of California.

**Issue**
Can sending unsolicited emails to a company's computer system constitute trespass to chattels if the emails cause no physical damage or functional disruption to the system, but distract employees and consume company resources to block them?

**Holding**
Defendant (Hamidi) wins. Sending electronic communications that cause no physical damage or functional disruption to a computer system does not constitute actionable trespass to chattels under California law. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Trespass to Chattels.** An intentional interference with the possession of personal property that proximately causes injury.
- **Injury Requirement (Trespass to Chattels).** For interference with personal property not amounting to conversion, the owner can recover only actual damages suffered by reason of the impairment of the property or the loss of its use.
- **Restatement (Second) of Torts § 218.** Dispossession alone is actionable, but other forms of interference require harm to the personal property or the possessor's interests in it. Intermeddling is actionable only if it is harmful to the possessor's materially valuable interest in the physical condition, quality, or value of the chattel, or if the possessor is deprived of its use for a substantial time.
- **Rejected: Trespass to Chattels without Harm.** A harmless use or touching of personal property, while a technical trespass, is not actionable without a showing of harm under modern California and broader American law.
- **Injunctions.** To obtain injunctive relief, a plaintiff must ordinarily show that the defendant's wrongful acts threaten to cause irreparable injuries that cannot be adequately compensated in damages.

**Reasoning**
- Modern trespass to chattels requires actual injury to the chattel or the possessor's rights in it, unlike trespass to land.
- Intel presented no evidence that Hamidi's emails caused physical damage, functional disruption, slowing, or impairment to its computer system.
- The economic damage Intel claimed (lost productivity from employees reading emails, staff time blocking messages) is not an injury to the computer system itself, which worked as intended.
- Prior cases finding trespass to chattels for electronic contact (e.g., spamming, robotic data collection) involved actual or threatened interference with the computer system's functioning (e.g., overburdening servers, reducing memory/processing power).
    - Hamidi's occasional messages, though numerous, were minuscule compared to commercial spam volumes that caused functional burdens.
- The court rejects the idea that unauthorized use alone is actionable without injury; temporary use of computer resources is not enough without measurable loss.
- The court rejects "impairment by content" as a basis for trespass to chattels, as it would stretch the tort far beyond its purpose of protecting possessory interests in property.
    - Injuries from unwelcome communications due to their content are protected by other torts (e.g., defamation, interference with economic relations), not trespass to chattels.
- Intel cannot assert a property interest in its employees' time; employees are not chattels.
- The time Intel staff spent blocking messages cannot be bootstrapped into an injury to Intel's possessory interest in its computers.
- The court declines to extend California common law to cover harmless electronic communications whose contents are objectionable, noting the complex policy debate about "propertization" of the Internet.
    - Treating servers as real property for trespass purposes would be a rash judicial act with potentially high social costs.
    - The Legislature is better suited to address these issues.
- Constitutional arguments are not reached because the injunction is improper on common law grounds.

**Concurrence (Kennard, J.)**
- Agrees that trespass to chattels requires damage to equipment or significant impairment of its usefulness/availability.
- Intel has not shown such damage or impairment from Hamidi's emails.
- Intel is not helpless: employees can delete messages and request removal from mailing lists; other tort theories (defamation, interference with economic interests) might apply; and the Legislature can address bulk emails.

**Dissent (Brown, J.)**
- Intel invested in its computer system for productivity, not as a public forum. Hamidi's emails diverted employees and undermined system utility.
- Intel's objection is to Hamidi's use of its property, not just the message content. Private property owners can exclude unwanted mail for any reason.
- Analogizes to spray-painting a car bumper: the time and expense to remove paint is an injury, even if the car's market value isn't reduced.
- Cites cases where unauthorized use of a system (e.g., searching for phone codes) was trespass even without physical damage or significant burden.
- Argues the law protects a person's right not to listen, and Intel, as property owner, can make that decision for its system.
- Finds actual injury:
    - Economic loss from employee time spent dealing with emails and company resources spent blocking them.
    - Impairment of the chattel's subjective utility to the owner (like a used toothbrush), not just market value.
    - Unlawful appropriation of Intel's resources for Hamidi's own use (like using another's mooring buoy).
- Argues that injunctive relief is proper for repetitive trespasses even without actual damages, especially when damages are impractical to recover.

**Dissent (Mosk, J.)**
- Hamidi's repeated bulk emails to Intel's proprietary confidential email lists, despite demands to cease, constituted actionable trespass to chattels.
- Distinguishes between open communication on the public Internet and unauthorized intrusion into a private intranet.
- Hamidi's actions are like intruding into a private office mailroom and distributing unwanted materials.
- Intel's security measures were circumvented, leaving it with no recourse unless a malfunction or crash occurs.
- The law of trespass to chattels is not universally limited to physical damage; it applies when a proprietary system is used contrary to the owner's purposes and self-help is ineffective.
- Intel suffered substantial harm from blocking efforts and diminished employee productivity, which impaired the quality and value of its system.
- Other courts have applied trespass to chattels to similar electronic intermeddling without requiring physical damage or system overload.
- Intel's connection to the Internet for business does not forfeit its right to control access to its private system.
- Trespass to chattels is the most efficient remedy, as it focuses on unauthorized use of property without evaluating content.
- The law should evolve to protect property interests in the digital age.
- There is no constitutional right to trespass.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is crucial for understanding the **trespass to chattels** doctrine in the context of intangible digital interactions. It clarifies that unlike trespass to land, trespass to chattels generally requires actual harm to the chattel or the possessor's interest in it, not just unauthorized contact. It distinguishes itself from cases like *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C.* (though *eBay* is cited in the opinion, the *eBay* case you studied was about patent injunctions, not trespass to chattels, so this is a different *eBay* case) and *CompuServe Inc. v. Cyber Promotions, Inc.* (cited in the opinion), where functional impairment of computer systems due to high volume was present. This case limits the expansion of trespass to chattels to situations where the "harm" is merely the content of a message or the distraction it causes, rather than a physical or functional impact on the property itself.
2. This case raises the question of how property law should adapt to new technologies. If a company's private network is constantly bombarded with unwanted messages that cause significant productivity loss but no technical damage, what legal recourse should exist? Is the majority's reliance on other torts sufficient, or does it leave a gap in protecting proprietary digital infrastructure?

**Takeaway:** For trespass to chattels involving electronic communications, California law requires actual physical damage or functional impairment to the computer system, not just economic harm from the content of the messages or the effort to block them.

## Berg v. Wiley (Minnesota Supreme Court, 1978)

**Facts**
- On November 11, 1970, Wiley (landlord) and a predecessor to Berg (tenant) executed a lease for a restaurant space in Osseo, Minnesota.
- The lease was for a 5-year term starting December 1, 1970, and required the tenant to bear repair costs, make no structural changes without written authorization, and operate the restaurant lawfully.
- The lease also allowed Wiley to retake possession if the tenant failed to meet lease conditions.
- In early 1971, Kathleen Berg took assignment of the lease and opened "A Family Affair Restaurant."
- In January 1973, Berg incorporated the restaurant and assigned her interest to the corporation, continuing to act as the sole shareholder.
- In June and July 1973, Wiley objected to Berg's remodeling without permission and alleged health code violations.
- On June 29, 1973, Wiley's attorney sent a letter to Berg, alleging breaches of the lease for unauthorized structural changes and an unclean kitchen, demanding completion of remodeling items by July 13, 1973, or Wiley would retake possession.
- A June 13, 1973, inspection by the Minnesota Department of Health also ordered changes to comply with health codes by July 15, 1973.
- Berg continued to operate the restaurant during the 2-week deadline.
- At the close of business on Friday, July 13, 1973, Berg dismissed employees, closed the restaurant, and placed a "Closed for Remodeling" sign in the window.
- Earlier on July 13, Berg testified that Wiley attempted to change the locks in her absence but left them unchanged when she returned and asserted her right to possession.
- Later on July 13, Berg observed Wiley peering into the restaurant and then pounding on the back door, leading her to call the sheriff.
- Wiley testified he saw Berg and friends removing paneling, called the police, and an agreement was mediated to preserve the status quo until Monday, July 16, 1973.
- On Monday, July 16, 1973, Wiley, accompanied by a police officer and locksmith, entered the premises in Berg's absence and without her knowledge, and changed the locks.
- Berg found herself locked out later that day.
- The lease was not due to expire until December 1, 1975.
- The premises were re-let to another tenant around August 1, 1973.

**Procedural History**
- Berg brought a damage action against Wiley and others on July 27, 1973.
- All defendants except Wiley and Wiley Enterprises, Inc. were dismissed at the close of Berg's case.
- The trial court found as a matter of law that Wiley locked the tenant out and that the lockout was wrongful.
- The jury found no liability on Berg's claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress and no liability on Wiley's counterclaim for damages to the premises.
- The jury awarded Berg $31,000 for lost profits and $3,540 for loss of chattels due to the wrongful lockout.
- The jury specifically found that Berg neither abandoned nor surrendered the premises.
- The trial court granted Wiley's post-trial motion for indemnification for mechanics lien liability by way of set-off from Berg's judgment.
- Wiley appealed the damages award for wrongful eviction.

**Issue**
Can a landlord use self-help to retake leased premises from a tenant who has not abandoned or surrendered the property, even if the landlord has a right to possession?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Berg) wins. A landlord may not use self-help to dispossess a tenant who has not abandoned or voluntarily surrendered the premises but claims possession adversely to the landlord's claim of breach of a written lease; judicial process is the exclusive lawful means. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Common Law Self-Help (Rejected).** A landlord may rightfully use self-help to retake leased premises from a tenant in possession without liability for wrongful eviction if the landlord is legally entitled to possession and the means of reentry are peaceable.
- **Rejected: Peaceable Reentry (Common Law).** Reentry by a landlord in the tenant's absence by picking locks and locking the tenant out, even without actual violence, is not peaceable under the common law.
- **Exclusive Judicial Remedy.** The only lawful means to dispossess a tenant who has not abandoned or voluntarily surrendered but who claims possession adversely to a landlord’s claim of breach of a written lease is by resort to judicial process.
- **Statutory Remedies.** Minnesota Statutes §§ 566.02 to 566.17 provide an adequate and speedy judicial remedy for landlords to regain possession.

**Reasoning**
- The jury's finding that Berg did not abandon or surrender the premises was supported by sufficient evidence, as she could reasonably have intended to retain possession and close temporarily for remodeling.
- The court's long-standing policy is to discourage landlords from taking the law into their own hands due to the potential for breaches of the peace.
- Minnesota statutory law provides a summary procedure for landlords to recover possession through judicial process (Minn.St. 566.02 to 566.17), which is intended to prevent self-help.
- Wiley's reentry was not peaceable under the common-law rule because Berg was in possession, claiming a right to continue, and Wiley retook possession in her absence by changing locks, despite a history of vigorous dispute and animosity.
- Approving such a lockout merely because no actual violence erupted in the tenant's absence would encourage tenants to be vigilant, setting the stage for public disturbances.
- The court adopts the modern view that self-help is never available to dispossess a tenant who is in possession and has not abandoned or voluntarily surrendered the premises.
    - This rule recognizes the inherent potential for violent breach of peace in such situations.
    - Adequate and speedy judicial means are available for removing a tenant peacefully.
- The rule is fairly applied to Wiley because, even under the older common-law rule, his lockout would be considered non-peaceable.
- Equity dictates that Wiley, who performed the act causing damage, must bear the loss.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important because it represents a significant shift from the traditional common law rule regarding landlord self-help, moving towards an exclusive judicial remedy for eviction. It connects to *Berg v. Wiley* itself, which is the case being briefed, and establishes a modern trend that contrasts with older approaches to property rights and possession. It also relates to the general theme of protecting possession and discouraging self-help, similar to the policy considerations in cases like *Ploof v. Putnam* (necessity as a defense to trespass) or *Williams v. Ford Motor Credit Co.* (limitations on self-help repossession of chattels).
2. The opinion mentions that the issue of whether Berg had actually breached the lease was not judicially determined. This raises the question of whether a landlord's right to possession, even if clear, can ever justify self-help under this new rule.

**Takeaway:** Landlords in Minnesota (and many other states following the modern trend) must always use judicial process to evict a tenant in possession, even if the tenant has breached the lease, to prevent wrongful eviction.

## Williams v. Ford Motor Credit Co. (United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, 1982)

**Facts**
- In July 1975, David Williams purchased a Ford Mustang, and the title was in the names of both David and Cathy Williams.
- The car was financed through a Ford dealer, who assigned the paper to Ford Motor Credit Company (FMCC).
- In 1977, Cathy and David Williams divorced, and Cathy was granted title to the car, with David ordered to continue payments to FMCC.
- David defaulted on the payments and signed a voluntary repossession authorization for FMCC.
- Cathy Williams was informed of the delinquency and stated she was trying to get David to make payments.
- On December 1, 1977, at approximately 4:30 a.m., Cathy Williams was awakened by a noise and saw a wrecker truck hooking up to her car.
- She went outside, hollered at the men, and the truck stopped.
- She told them the car was hers and asked what they were doing.
- One man, Don Sappington, identified himself as repossessing the vehicle for FMCC.
- Williams explained she was trying to bring payments up to date and stated the car contained personal items.
- Sappington retrieved the personal items from the car and handed them to her.
- Williams made no further complaint, and Sappington drove off with the car.
- Williams testified that Sappington was polite and did not threaten her or cause her to fear physical harm.
- The car was parked in an unenclosed driveway.
- Williams called the police and reported her car stolen.

**Procedural History**
- Cathy Williams sued FMCC for conversion.
- A jury awarded Williams $5,000 in damages.
- FMCC moved for judgment notwithstanding the verdict (JNOV).
- The district court initially ordered a nonsuit without prejudice for Williams to refile in state court.
- On FMCC's appeal, the Eighth Circuit reversed and remanded with directions to rule on the JNOV motion.
- The district court entered JNOV for FMCC.
- Williams appealed the JNOV.

**Issue**
Can a secured party repossess a vehicle without judicial process when the debtor is present and objects, but does not physically resist, without breaching the peace?

**Holding**
Defendant (FMCC) wins. A repossession does not breach the peace if the debtor does not object to the taking or if the confrontation does not involve force, threats of force, or risk of invoking violence. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **UCC § 9-503 (now § 9-609).** Unless otherwise agreed, a secured party has on default the right to take possession of the collateral, and in taking possession, a secured party may proceed without judicial process if this can be done without breach of the peace.
- **Breach of the Peace (Arkansas Pre-Code Cases).** A finding of conversion based on repossession occurred only where force, or threats of force, or risk of invoking violence, accompanied the repossession.
- **Rejected: Breach of the Peace (Dissent).** A breach of the peace occurs when a night raid is detected and a confrontation ensues, requiring the repossessor to retreat and turn to judicial process.

**Reasoning**
- The Arkansas Supreme Court's interpretation of "breach of the peace" in pre-Code cases, which are still relevant, requires force, threats of force, or risk of invoking violence.
- In *Manhattan Credit Co. v. Brewer*, a breach of peace occurred because the debtor and her husband confronted the agent and clearly objected.
- In *Rutledge v. Universal C.I.T. Credit Corp.*, no breach of peace occurred when the debtor did not give permission but also did not object.
- Williams did not object to the taking of the car after Sappington retrieved her personal items.
- Williams herself testified that the repossessors were polite and did not make any threats or cause her to fear physical harm.
- The repossession was accomplished without any incident that might tend to provoke violence.
- While the night-time taking is not commendable, the facts, even viewed most favorably to Williams, show a legal repossession under Arkansas law.

**Dissent (HEANEY, Circuit Judge)**
- The only issue is whether the repossession created a "risk of invoking violence."
- The jury found that it did, and this was a reasonable determination that should not be overturned.
- Williams did everything she could to stop them short of physical force, and the confrontation did not result in violence only because she did not take such steps and was powerless.
- The policy of UCC § 9-503 is to discourage extrajudicial acts fraught with the likelihood of violence.
- A confrontation in the dead of night, detected by the debtor, creates a risk of violence.
- When an invasion is detected and a confrontation ensues, the repossessor should be under a duty to retreat and turn to judicial process.
- The majority's rule encourages violence by allowing repossession to proceed after confrontation unless violence actually results.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the limits of **self-help repossession** under the UCC and the definition of **breach of the peace**. It can be compared to *Berg v. Wiley*, where a landlord's self-help eviction was found to be a breach of the peace due to the tenant's presence and objection, even without physical violence. Here, the court emphasizes the lack of explicit objection or threat of violence from the debtor.
2. The dissent raises a critical point about the "risk of invoking violence" versus actual violence. The majority seems to require a higher threshold for "breach of the peace" than the dissent, focusing on the debtor's actions (or inactions) rather than the inherent tension of a confrontation during a repossession. This leaves open the question of how much objection or resistance is required from a debtor to constitute a "breach of the peace" and force the creditor to resort to judicial process.

**Takeaway:** A secured creditor's self-help repossession is generally permissible under the UCC if the debtor does not physically resist or explicitly object to the taking, even if the repossession occurs at an inconvenient time and causes distress.

## Ploof v. Putnam (Vermont Supreme Court, 1908)

**Facts**
- On November 13, 1904, Ploof was sailing his loaded sloop on Lake Champlain with his wife and two minor children.
- A sudden and violent tempest arose, endangering the sloop and its occupants.
- To save them, Ploof moored his sloop to a dock owned by Putnam, which was in charge of Putnam's servant.
- Putnam, through his servant, unmoored Ploof's sloop.
- The sloop was driven ashore by the tempest, destroying the sloop and its contents, and injuring Ploof, his wife, and children.

**Procedural History**
- Ploof sued Putnam for damages in trespass and case.
- Putnam demurred to each count of the declaration.
- The trial court (Chittenden County, Haselton, J.) overruled the demurrers and adjudged each count sufficient.
- Putnam excepted and appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a person moor their vessel to another's dock during a sudden tempest to save lives and property without being liable for trespass, and can the dock owner be held liable for unmooring it?

**Holding**
- Ploof (plaintiff) wins. A person is justified by necessity in mooring to another's dock to save lives and property during a tempest, and the dock owner is liable for damages caused by unmooring the vessel. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Necessity Doctrine.** Necessity can justify actions that would otherwise be trespasses, especially to preserve human life or avoid bodily harm.
- **Trespass Justification.** An entry upon land or interference with personal property may be justified by necessity when movements are started without fault and in the proper exercise of a strict right.
- **Scope of Employment.** An allegation that a defendant acted "by his servant" implies the servant was acting within the scope of employment, even if the act was described as "wilfully and designedly" or "negligently, carelessly and wrongfully."

**Reasoning**
- The court reviews numerous cases establishing that necessity justifies actions that would otherwise be trespasses.
    - Examples include chasing trespassing sheep off land, cattle escaping while being driven to pound, travelers passing onto adjoining land due to highway obstruction, and entering land to save goods from water or fire.
    - The doctrine applies with special force to the preservation of human life, such as running through another's property to escape an assailant or sacrificing personal property to save lives (Mouse's Case).
- The declaration sufficiently alleges necessity because it states that the tempest compelled Ploof to moor to Putnam's dock to save his sloop and the people in it.
    - The details of the situation creating the necessity are matters of proof, not pleading.
    - The plaintiff does not need to explicitly negate the existence of other safe mooring options in the pleading.
- The declaration sufficiently alleges that the servant acted within the scope of employment.
    - The words "wilfully and designedly" or "negligently, carelessly and wrongfully" are applied to the defendant acting through the servant, not to the servant acting independently.
    - This implies the servant was acting under the defendant's authority.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes the **necessity doctrine** as a defense to trespass, emphasizing that property rights are not absolute and can be limited by the need to preserve life and property. It contrasts with cases like *Pile v. Pedrick* or *Edwards v. Allouez Mining Co.* where strict property rights were upheld even when it caused hardship to the trespasser, by showing that certain circumstances (like imminent danger) can override those rights.
2. What if the dock was damaged by Ploof's sloop during the tempest? Would Ploof be liable for those damages, even if his mooring was justified by necessity?

**Takeaway:** The right to use another's property without permission can be justified by necessity, especially when human life is at stake.

## Olwell v. Nye & Nissen Co. (Washington Supreme Court, 1946)

**Facts**
- On May 6, 1940, E. L. Olwell sold his interest in Puget Sound Egg Packers to Nye & Nissen Company.
- As part of the sale, Olwell retained full ownership of an "Eggsact" egg-washing machine.
- Nye & Nissen Co. promised to make the machine available for delivery to Olwell by June 15, 1940.
- Olwell arranged for the machine to be stored in a space adjacent to Nye & Nissen Co.'s premises, but not covered by their lease.
- In May 1941, Nye & Nissen Co.'s treasurer, without Olwell's knowledge or consent, ordered the egg washer taken out of storage.
- Nye & Nissen Co. used the machine approximately one day a week for three years in its business.
- Olwell discovered the use in January or February of 1945 when he heard the machine operating at the plant.
- Olwell offered to sell the machine to Nye & Nissen Co. for $600.
- Nye & Nissen Co. counter-offered $50, which Olwell refused.
- Approximately one month later, Olwell commenced an action.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court entered judgment for Olwell for $1,560, representing $10 per week for 156 weeks.
- Nye & Nissen Co. appealed the judgment, the theory of unjust enrichment, the amount of damages, and the court's refusal to find the machine's value.

**Issue**
Can a property owner recover the profits a tortfeasor gained from the unauthorized use of their property, even if the owner suffered no direct loss?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Olwell) wins. A property owner can elect to "waive the tort" and sue in quasi-contract to recover the profits a tortfeasor gained from the unauthorized use of their property, as this constitutes unjust enrichment. Affirmed as modified.

**Rules**
- **Waiver of Tort and Suit in Assumpsit.** In cases where a defendant tortfeasor has benefited by their wrong, the plaintiff may elect to "waive the tort" and bring an action in assumpsit for restitution.
- **Quasi-Contract.** An action in assumpsit for restitution arises out of a duty imposed by law upon the defendant to repay an unjust and unmerited enrichment.
- **Benefit (Restatement of Restitution).** A person confers a benefit upon another if they give to the other possession of or some other interest in money, land, chattels, or choses in action, performs services beneficial to or at the request of the other, satisfies a debt or a duty of the other, or in any way adds to the other's security or advantage, including saving the other from expense or loss.
- **Property Rights.** The essence of property is the right to its exclusive use.
- **Measure of Restitution.** If the defendant was tortious in acquiring the benefit, they are required to pay for what the other has lost, even if that is more than the recipient benefited. If they were consciously tortious in acquiring the benefit, they are also deprived of any profit derived from their subsequent dealing with it.
- **Damages Limit.** The amount of recovery cannot exceed the amount prayed for in the complaint.

**Reasoning**
- The defendant's unauthorized use of the egg-washing machine resulted in a "benefit" to the defendant, specifically a saving in labor costs.
- The court rejected the argument that Olwell suffered no loss because the machine was in storage and not being used by him.
    - The **exclusive use** of property is its essence, and any wrongful invasion of this right is a compensable loss.
    - To hold otherwise would undermine all property rights.
- Olwell had an election of remedies: he could sue in tort for damages or "waive the tort" and sue in quasi-contract for restitution.
- By choosing restitution, Olwell is entitled to recover the profit derived by Nye & Nissen Co. from the use of the machine, as this constitutes unjust enrichment.
- The trial court's judgment of $10 per week for 156 weeks ($1,560) was excessive because it exceeded the amount prayed for in the complaint ($25 per month for 36 months, or $900).

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important because it illustrates the concept of **unjust enrichment** as a remedy for the unauthorized use of property, even when the owner suffers no direct monetary loss. It connects to **Olwell v. Nye & Nissen Co.** by showing that the court will protect the owner's right to exclusive use of their property. It contrasts with cases focused on actual damages, like *Pile v. Pedrick*, by allowing recovery of the wrongdoer's gain rather than the owner's loss.
2. This case raises the question of whether the "waiver of tort" doctrine is truly about waiving the tort or simply providing an alternative measure of recovery for a tortious act.

**Takeaway:** A property owner can recover the profits gained by a tortfeasor from the unauthorized use of their property under a theory of unjust enrichment, even if the owner suffered no direct loss.

## Pile v. Pedrick (Supreme Court of Pennsylvania, 1895)

**Facts**
- Defendants intended to build a factory wall entirely on their own land.
- Defendants hired a city surveyor to locate their property line.
- Defendants built a foundation wall based on the surveyor's line.
- Subsequent surveys revealed the foundation wall encroached on plaintiffs' land by about one and three-eighths inches below the surface for a portion of its length. The brick wall above the foundation was entirely on defendants' land.
- Defendants offered to make the wall a party wall by agreement, allowing plaintiffs free use, if windows on the upper floors could remain open until plaintiffs needed the wall. Plaintiffs declined.
- Defendants sought permission to enter plaintiffs' land to chip off the projecting ends of the foundation stones, offering to pay for any inconvenience or injury. Plaintiffs refused.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs filed a bill in equity to compel removal of the wall and windows.
- The master found the foundation wall encroached on plaintiffs' property.
- The trial court ordered defendants to remove the encroaching portion of their wall, replacing it with a wall entirely on their own ground, without entering plaintiffs' land. The court divided costs equally.
- Both plaintiffs and defendants appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a court of equity order a defendant to remove an encroaching foundation wall, even if the encroachment is slight and unintentional, and the plaintiff refuses entry to allow a less costly removal method?

**Holding**
- Plaintiffs win. The court can compel removal of an encroaching foundation wall, and the defendant must remove it from their own side if the plaintiff denies entry. Affirmed (with modification to removal timeline).

**Rules**
- **Trespass.** A landowner has no right at law or in equity to occupy land that does not belong to them.
- **Equity Costs.** Costs in equity are not automatic; they are within the chancellor's discretion and may be given or withheld as equity and good conscience require.
- **Party Wall.** A wall not intended to be a party wall and built entirely on one's own land (except for an unintentional, slight subsurface encroachment) is not a party wall.

**Reasoning**
- The foundation wall was not a party wall because defendants intended to build entirely on their own land and did so, except for the unintentional subsurface encroachment.
- Defendants have no right to occupy plaintiffs' land, even if the encroachment is slight and unintentional.
- Since plaintiffs refused permission for defendants to enter their land to chip off the encroaching stones, the only remaining method to remedy the trespass is for defendants to take down and rebuild the entire wall from their own side.
- The court's division of costs was a proper exercise of discretion, given the circumstances and the parties' conduct, as equity often enforces legal rights under circumstances that cause hardship to the defendant.
- The decree was modified to allow one year for removal to avoid further controversy.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the strict protection of property boundaries, even for minor, unintentional encroachments. It contrasts with cases like *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands*, where a court might balance hardships and deny an injunction for a minor encroachment if the cost of removal is disproportionate to the injury. Here, the court emphasizes the owner's absolute right to exclude.
2. This case raises the question of whether a property owner has a duty to mitigate damages or cooperate with a trespasser to minimize the cost of removal, especially when the trespass was unintentional and minor. What if the cost of removing the entire wall was astronomically high compared to the value of the encroached land?

**Takeaway:** Even a slight, unintentional subsurface encroachment by a building's foundation constitutes a trespass that a court will order removed, and the encroacher must bear the full cost of removal, even if it means rebuilding the entire structure from their own side if the neighbor refuses access.

## Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands (Colorado Supreme Court, 1951)

**Facts**
- Rylands and Reid (plaintiffs) owned a parcel of land with a residence, garage, and rental cottages.
- Golden Press, Inc. (defendant) constructed a one-story business building on its adjoining property.
- The west wall of the defendant's building was two inches clear of the lot line at the front, on the line at the north end, and approximately 160 feet long.
- During construction, the defendant allegedly trespassed on the plaintiffs' property by dropping an I-beam on their garage roof, destroying a flower bed and fence, disturbing a driveway, and digging into their land.
- The plaintiffs and defendant each employed surveyors before construction, but their results were not fully disclosed, and the building progressed.
- During construction, plaintiff Rylands' brother, Thomas P. Nother, informed the defendant's construction superintendent that the building was about eight inches over the line, but the superintendent stated they had their own surveyor who put them on the line.
- The defendant's witness, Argo, who supervised wall construction, testified that after a complaint from the neighbor, their surveyor told them to move over about two inches, and they did.
- The plaintiffs continually complained about trespass by workmen but did not seek an injunction until after the foundation and upper wall were completed.
- A survey by Coberly, employed by the plaintiffs just before trial, showed the defendant's footings encroached two inches at the middle and three and a half inches at the north end.
- The encroaching footings were seven to nine feet below the surface and did not interfere with the plaintiffs' current use of the property as a driveway and iris bed.
- The plaintiffs refused the defendant permission to chip off the encroaching footings from their property and demanded removal from the defendant's side, even if it meant tearing down the wall.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs sued for an injunction to remove footings, an injunction against trespass, and $1750 in damages plus exemplary damages.
- The case was tried to a jury on the issue of damages for trespass, with the court reserving the injunction issue.
- The jury returned a verdict in favor of the defendant on damages.
- The trial court found encroachment, granted a mandatory injunction requiring removal of the footings, and ordered the defendant to direct drivers and remove confusing signs.
- The trial court set aside the jury's verdict and granted a new trial on damages.
- Defendant appealed the injunction and the setting aside of the jury verdict.

**Issue**
Can a mandatory injunction be issued to compel the removal of a slight, unintentional building encroachment when the cost of removal is disproportionate to the harm caused to the plaintiff?

**Holding**
Defendant (Golden Press, Inc.) wins. A mandatory injunction for the removal of a slight, unintentional encroachment should be denied where the cost of removal is greatly disproportionate to the plaintiff's damage and the plaintiff's use of the property is not significantly affected. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Mandatory Injunction for Encroachment.** Ordinarily, a mandatory injunction will issue to compel the removal of encroaching structures, but it is not to be issued as a matter of course.
- **Equitable Considerations.** On appeal to the court for an equitable remedy, the court must consider the peculiar equities of the case.
- **Willful Encroachment.** Where the encroachment is deliberate and constitutes a willful and intentional taking of another’s land, equity may well require its restoration regardless of the expense of removal as compared with damage suffered therefrom.
- **Good Faith Encroachment.** Where the encroachment was in good faith, the court should weigh the circumstances so that it shall not act oppressively.
- **Balance of Convenience (Rejected as sole test).** Rejected: The mere balance of convenience is not the proper test.
- **Relative Hardship.** Relative hardship may properly be considered, and the court should not become a party to extortion.
- **Denial of Mandatory Injunction.** Where defendant’s encroachment is unintentional and slight, plaintiff’s use not affected and his damage small and fairly compensable, while the cost of removal is so great as to cause grave hardship or otherwise make its removal unconscionable, mandatory injunction may properly be denied and plaintiff relegated to compensation in damages.
- **Clarity of Injunction.** A judgment must be definite and certain in itself, clearly fixing the rights and liabilities of the parties.

**Reasoning**
- The trial court's order setting aside the jury verdict and granting a new trial on damages was discretionary and not a final judgment, so it cannot be reviewed on appeal.
- The portion of the injunction regarding directing drivers and removing signs was too vague and indefinite to stand.
- The court found no evidence challenging the good faith of the defendant's representatives in locating the footings, despite ongoing arguments during construction.
- The encroachment was very slight (two to three and a half inches) and entirely below ground (seven to nine feet deep).
- The encroachment did not interfere with the plaintiffs' current use of the property.
- The only potential future damage was that the plaintiffs would have to detour around the footings if they built to their line with a basement.
- The value of the encroached land was very small (approximately $55 for the entire strip if taken).
- The expense and hardship of removing the footings (potentially requiring tearing down the wall) would be extremely great compared to the minimal advantage gained by the plaintiffs.
- Requiring removal would be unconscionable given the circumstances.
- The plaintiffs' delay in seeking an injunction until after construction was complete weighed against them.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the **equitable remedy** of a mandatory injunction, particularly in the context of **encroachments**. It demonstrates that courts will not always grant an injunction, even when a property right has been violated, if the hardship to the defendant is disproportionate to the benefit to the plaintiff. This contrasts with cases like *Pile v. Pedrick*, where a mandatory injunction was granted for a slight encroachment, emphasizing a stricter adherence to property lines. It also connects to the broader theme of balancing property rights with equitable considerations, similar to the discussion of remedies in *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C.* regarding patent infringement.
2. The court's finding that there was "no evidence from the record challenging the good faith of defendant’s representative in locating the footings" despite testimony about warnings and adjustments raises a question about what constitutes "good faith" in an encroachment. If the defendant was warned and made an adjustment, but still encroached, how much more evidence of bad faith is required?

**Takeaway:** Courts may deny a mandatory injunction for a minor, good-faith encroachment if the cost of removal is unconscionably high compared to the plaintiff's minimal harm, relegating the plaintiff to damages.

## eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C. (Supreme Court of the United States, 2006)

**Facts**
- MercExchange held patents, including a business method patent for an electronic market.
- MercExchange sought to license its patent to eBay and Half.com, but they failed to reach an agreement.
- MercExchange filed a patent infringement suit against eBay and Half.com.
- A jury found MercExchange's patent valid, infringed by eBay and Half.com, and awarded damages.

**Procedural History**
- The District Court denied MercExchange's motion for permanent injunctive relief.
- The Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit reversed, applying a general rule that permanent injunctions issue against patent infringement absent exceptional circumstances.
- The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

**Issue**
- Does the traditional four-factor test for permanent injunctions apply to disputes arising under the Patent Act, or is there a special rule for patent cases?

**Holding**
- MercExchange (plaintiff) wins in part. The traditional four-factor test for permanent injunctions applies to patent disputes. The judgment of the Court of Appeals is vacated.

**Rules**
- **Permanent Injunctions (Traditional Equitable Principles).** A plaintiff seeking a permanent injunction must satisfy a four-factor test: (1) irreparable injury; (2) inadequacy of legal remedies; (3) balance of hardships favors the plaintiff; and (4) public interest would not be disserved.
- **Patent Act and Injunctions.** The Patent Act provides that injunctions "may" issue "in accordance with the principles of equity."
- Rejected: **General Rule for Patent Injunctions.** A general rule that courts will issue permanent injunctions against patent infringement absent exceptional circumstances.
- Rejected: **Categorical Denial of Injunctions.** A plaintiff's willingness to license its patents and lack of commercial activity in practicing the patents are sufficient to establish that the patent holder would not suffer irreparable harm if an injunction did not issue.

**Reasoning**
- The Patent Act does not indicate Congress intended a departure from traditional equitable principles for injunctions.
    - The Patent Act states injunctions "may" issue "in accordance with the principles of equity."
    - The right to exclude granted by a patent is distinct from the remedies for its violation.
- This approach is consistent with how injunctions are treated under the Copyright Act, which also uses "may" for injunctive relief and has rejected automatic injunctions.
- The District Court erred by adopting broad principles that would categorically deny injunctive relief in certain cases, such as when a patent holder is willing to license or does not practice the patent.
    - This is in tension with *Continental Paper Bag Co. v. Eastern Paper Bag Co.*, which held that a court of equity has jurisdiction to grant injunctive relief even if a patent holder unreasonably declined to use the patent.
- The Court of Appeals erred by articulating a "general rule" unique to patent disputes that a permanent injunction will issue once infringement and validity are adjudged.
    - This categorical grant of relief departs from the four-factor test.
- The decision to grant or deny injunctive relief rests within the equitable discretion of the district courts and must be exercised consistent with traditional principles of equity.

**Concurrence (Roberts, C.J.)**
- Agrees with the Court's holding that equitable discretion applies to patent disputes.
- Notes that courts have historically granted injunctive relief in the vast majority of patent cases, which is not surprising given the difficulty of protecting a right to exclude through monetary remedies.
- This historical practice does not entitle a patentee to an automatic injunction but provides context for applying the four-factor test.

**Concurrence (Kennedy, J.)**
- Agrees that the four-factor test should be applied without categorical rules.
- Notes that the traditional practice of issuing injunctions in patent cases may not solely rest on the difficulty of monetary remedies.
- Emphasizes that trial courts should consider the changing nature of patents and patent holders.
    - An industry has developed where firms use patents primarily for licensing fees, not for producing goods.
    - Injunctions can be used as a bargaining tool to charge exorbitant fees, and legal damages may be sufficient.
    - The potential vagueness and suspect validity of some business method patents may affect the four-factor calculus.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is crucial for understanding the limits of property rights, even intellectual property rights. While a patent grants a "right to exclude," this case clarifies that the remedy for infringement is not automatic exclusion (via injunction) but is subject to equitable considerations. It connects to cases like *Pile v. Pedrick* and *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands* by showing that even when a property right is clearly violated, courts retain discretion in crafting remedies, especially when the remedy sought is an injunction. It also highlights the tension between a strong property right and the broader public interest or economic realities, a theme seen in cases like *Ploof v. Putnam* (necessity overriding property rights).
2. The concurrences highlight a tension: Chief Justice Roberts emphasizes the historical practice of granting injunctions in patent cases, while Justice Kennedy points to new economic realities (e.g., "patent trolls") that might make injunctions less appropriate. This raises the question of how much historical practice should guide equitable discretion when circumstances change significantly.

**Takeaway:** The Supreme Court held that the traditional four-factor test for permanent injunctions applies to patent infringement cases, rejecting categorical rules for or against injunctive relief.

## Producers Lumber & Supply Co. v. Olney Building Co. (Texas Courts of Civil Appeals, 1960)

**Facts**
- Prior to November 1, 1956, Elliott Construction Co., Inc. owned Lot 8.
- On November 1, 1956, H. P. Orts, as Assistant Secretary and Agent and Attorney in Fact for Elliott Construction Co., Inc., executed a warranty deed conveying Lot 8 to Producers Lumber & Supply Co. (appellant) for $1,428. This deed was properly recorded.
- Producers Lumber & Supply Co. purchased Lot 8 with the intention that its general manager, George R. Montgomery, and his wife would build a home there.
- Montgomery and his wife had the lot graded and planted trees and grass on it.
- On or about February 27, 1958, Orts and Elliott decided to construct nine dwellings, including one on Lot 8.
- Orts asked A. L. Burden, secretary-treasurer of Olney Building Co. (appellee), if Lot 8 had been sold. Burden, after consulting a map, told Orts it had not been sold.
- Orts inspected Lot 8 and noticed the planted trees, thinking it was "nice of someone to plant trees on this lot."
- Construction of the nine houses began shortly thereafter.
- On April 1, 1958, Olney Building Co. ordered a Title Binder for all nine lots from Stewart Title Company for interim financing.
- On April 14, 1958, Orts learned from the Title Company that Lot 8 had been sold to Producers Lumber & Supply Co.
- Orts notified Montgomery of the situation; this was Producers Lumber & Supply Co.'s first notice of construction on its lot.
- The dwelling on Lot 8 was almost completed when the discovery was made.
- Orts began negotiations with Montgomery to reach a settlement. Orts told Montgomery that Montgomery had him "at his mercy."
- Various offers and counter-offers were made, but no settlement was reached.
- On April 22, 1958, Orts broke off negotiations and sent his construction superintendent with a crew and heavy equipment to Lot 8.
- The dwelling on Lot 8 was demolished, leaving only debris.
- The parties stipulated that the dwelling had a value of $5,000 and enhanced the value of Lot 8 by $5,000.

**Procedural History**
- Producers Lumber & Supply Co. sued Olney Building Co. for damages.
- The jury found that Orts acted in good faith in erecting the building, that the cost to restore the lot was $600, that Orts acted maliciously in removing the building, and awarded $300 in exemplary damages.
- The trial court rendered judgment for Producers Lumber & Supply Co. for $600, disregarding the jury's findings on malice and exemplary damages.
- Producers Lumber & Supply Co. appealed, contending the judgment should have been $5,900.

**Issue**
Can a builder who mistakenly constructs a dwelling on another's land in good faith, but then maliciously demolishes it, recover for the value of the improvements or avoid liability for the demolition?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Producers Lumber & Supply Co.) wins. A builder who, after mistakenly constructing a dwelling on another's land in good faith, resorts to self-help and maliciously demolishes the dwelling, commits waste and cannot claim equitable relief for the improvements. The judgment is amended to include the stipulated value of the dwelling and exemplary damages. Affirmed as amended.

**Rules**
- **Fixtures.** Where a person erects a building upon the land of another without his knowledge and consent, the building becomes a fixture and belongs to the owner of the land, and the builder is without remedy.
- **Good Faith Improver.** It is only where a person places permanent improvements upon land belonging to another in a good faith belief that he is the owner of the land that he has any remedy at all.
- **Equitable Relief for Good Faith Improver.** A court of equity may grant relief to a good faith improver, such as permitting removal of the building if it can be done without great injury, or allowing recovery for the enhanced value of the land, or ordering sale of the land and improvements.
- **Duty to Investigate Records.** A person contemplating the improvement of land is under a duty to investigate the records before commencing work, as recorded deeds impart notice of outstanding claims.
- **Waste.** An improver is not authorized to go upon the land of another without his knowledge and consent and demolish improvements placed thereon; if he does so, he commits waste and must pay the landowner for such waste.
- **Unclean Hands Doctrine.** A party who resorts to self-help and takes the law into their own hands, causing great destruction of property, cannot come into a court of equity with "unclean hands" to seek equitable remedies like reimbursement for enhanced value.

**Reasoning**
- The jury's findings that Orts acted maliciously in removing the building and that Producers was entitled to exemplary damages were well supported by the evidence and should not have been disregarded.
- Orts' testimony detailed the extensive and destructive methods used to demolish the dwelling, including bulldozers, cranes, drop hammers, air hammers, and cutting torches, and his admission that $2,768 "went down the drain" as if he "had set a match to it."
- Even if the jury's finding of good faith in building the dwelling was supported by the evidence (despite the recorded deed conveying the lot to Producers), Olney Building Co. cannot prevail on a plea of improvement in good faith due to the malicious destruction.
- While the law provides remedies for good faith improvers, these remedies are equitable and require the improver to seek court intervention.
- Orts' actions in demolishing the dwelling without Producers' knowledge or consent constituted waste, making Olney Building Co. liable for the stipulated value of the dwelling ($5,000).
- By resorting to self-help and destroying the property, Orts (and thus Olney Building Co.) came to court with "unclean hands," precluding any equitable claim for reimbursement for the enhanced value of the lot.
- Producers' failure to settle on Orts' terms did not constitute unclean hands, as Producers was not seeking an equitable remedy but suing for damages for willful destruction of its property.

**Dissent (Barrow, Justice)**
- The dissent disagrees with allowing Producers to recover $5,000 for the value of the house, arguing that Producers had no investment in it.
- The jury's finding of good faith in building the house was supported by the evidence, and the recorded deed was only one evidentiary fact.
- Texas law allows a good faith improver to obtain relief, including removing improvements, and does not lose equitable rights by doing so.
- Olney Building Co. was not seeking reimbursement but interposing its rights as a defense to a damage suit.
- The "demolished" property belonged to Olney, not Producers, as Producers would have had to pay for the enhancement in value.
- Producers attempted to drive an "unconscionable bargain" by refusing reasonable offers and demanding a low price for the completed house, effectively having Olney "at its mercy."
- The "unclean hands" doctrine should not apply to Olney because it was exercising a right to remove its property, and it was Producers who acted inequitably.
- Awarding Producers $5,000 for a house it did not build and had no investment in constitutes an additional punitive damage and an "inequitable decision" that makes Producers richer to the damage of Olney.

**NOTES:**
1. This case highlights the tension between the common law rule that improvements become part of the land (fixtures) and equitable principles designed to prevent unjust enrichment for good faith improvers. It connects to **Pile v. Pedrick** and **Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands** by showing how courts grapple with mistaken improvements on land, but here, the improver's self-help and malicious destruction drastically alter the outcome. Unlike those cases where the improver sought to keep the improvement or be compensated, here the improver destroyed it, leading to liability for waste. The "unclean hands" doctrine is a key equitable principle applied here, similar to how equitable considerations might influence remedies in cases like **eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C.**
2. The dissent raises an interesting point about whether the "unclean hands" doctrine should apply when the improver is merely exercising a right to remove their property, even if done destructively, especially when the landowner is perceived as acting inequitably in negotiations. This could lead to a discussion about the limits of self-help even when a party might have a colorable claim to the property.

**Takeaway:** A good faith improver who resorts to self-help and maliciously destroys improvements on another's land forfeits equitable remedies and is liable for waste.

## Edwards v. Allouez Mining Co. (Michigan Supreme Court, 1878)

**Facts**
- In 1874, the defendant, Allouez Mining Company, built a stamp mill on the banks of Hill Creek at a cost of about $60,000.
- The mill's operations caused large quantities of sand to be carried down the creek and deposited on bottom lands below.
- In 1875, the plaintiff, Thomas W. Edwards, bought a piece of land downstream from the mill, through which the creek ran.
- The land was purchased for speculation, with the expectation of forcing the defendant to buy it at a higher price.
- The plaintiff offered to sell the land to the defendant, valuing it at three to five times his purchase price.
- The defendant declined to purchase the land.
- The plaintiff then filed a bill seeking an injunction.

**Procedural History**
- The circuit court refused the injunction but ordered a reference to a jury for an assessment of damages.
- The complainant appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a landowner obtain an injunction to stop a mining company from depositing sand on his land when he bought the land for speculative purposes, intending to force the company to buy it?

**Holding**
- Defendant (Allouez Mining Co.) wins. An injunction will not be granted when the complainant's purpose in acquiring the property was to force a sale, and monetary damages are an adequate remedy. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Injunctions are discretionary.** The allowance of an injunction is discretionary and not a matter of right.
- **Irreparable mischief.** Injunctions are intended to prevent irreparable mischief, meaning consequences that cannot be adequately compensated by damages.
- **Balancing inconveniences.** Courts will balance the inconveniences of granting or denying an injunction, and may withhold it if it would inflict greater injury than the grievance complained of.
- **Motive of petitioner.** Where a petitioner invites an injury and then seeks an equitable remedy, their motives can be inquired into, even if they ground themselves on a strict legal right.
- **Adequacy of legal remedy.** If the common law provides adequate redress for an injury, equity will not intervene.
- **Rejected: Absolute right to equitable relief.** The right to equitable relief, when it is the only adequate remedy, is as absolute as the right to legal relief.

**Reasoning**
- The defendant's operations constitute a legal injury to the plaintiff's property, for which he is entitled to redress.
- However, an injunction is a powerful remedy that should not be lightly ordered.
- Granting the injunction would force the defendant to either purchase the plaintiff's land at his price or sacrifice a valuable mill.
- The plaintiff bought the land for speculation, with the preconceived purpose of forcing the defendant to buy it.
- The plaintiff's motive in acquiring the land is relevant when he seeks the "special interposition of equity" rather than just strict legal rights.
- The injury to the plaintiff's land is not "irreparable" because the land was bought to make money from its sale, and a legal award of damages would further this purpose.
- The defendant is not alleged to be irresponsible, and a jury can award reasonable damages.
- The elements of irreparable injury are absent from this case.

**Dissent (Campbell, C. J.)**
- The defendant is continuously invading the complainant's freehold by depositing sand and polluting the stream, which is equivalent to taking or destroying his property.
- The right to equitable relief, when it is the only adequate remedy, is as absolute as the right to legal relief.
- No legal remedy is adequate for such a grievance because no legal remedy can secure the complainant the use of his own property.
- The complainant's speculative motives for purchasing the land should not lessen his claim to protection, as a civil wrong should not be lessened by the injured party's motives.
- Holding otherwise would make private interests subject to judicial discretion and make the judicial conscience the only arbiter of rights.

**NOTES:**
1. This case highlights the equitable doctrine of **clean hands** and the court's discretion in granting injunctive relief, especially when the plaintiff's motives are questionable. It contrasts with cases like *Pile v. Pedrick* where an injunction was granted despite significant cost to the defendant, suggesting a different approach to property rights and remedies. The court's balancing of conveniences here is also relevant to *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C.*, which discusses the four-factor test for injunctive relief.
2. The dissent argues that property rights should not be subject to judicial discretion based on the owner's motives, raising the question of whether a property owner's intent should ever diminish their right to protection against trespass or nuisance.

**Takeaway:** Courts may deny injunctive relief for property invasions if the plaintiff's primary motive for acquiring the property was to leverage the defendant's operations for a speculative sale, and monetary damages are deemed an adequate remedy.

## Pocono Springs Civic Association v. MacKenzie (Superior Court of Pennsylvania, 1995)

**Facts**
- On October 14, 1969, the MacKenzies purchased a vacant lot in Pocono Springs Development.
- In 1987, the MacKenzies attempted to sell their lot.
- A potential buyer conditioned the purchase on the lot being suitable for an on-lot sewage system.
- An inspection revealed the lot had inadequate soil for proper percolation, and the sale was lost.
- The MacKenzies attempted to give the lot to the Pocono Springs Civic Association, but the Association declined.
- The MacKenzies then tried to persuade the Association to accept the lot as a gift for a park, but the Association again declined.
- In 1986, the MacKenzies stopped paying real estate taxes on the lot.
- In 1988, the Wayne County Tax Claim Bureau offered the property for sale due to delinquent taxes, but there were no purchasers.
- In 1990, the lot was again offered for sale by the Tax Claim Bureau, but it was not sold and was placed on its "repository" list.
- The MacKenzies signed a notarized statement expressing their desire to abandon the lot and mailed it to "all interested parties."
- The MacKenzies do not accept mail regarding the property and have not visited the lot or used the development's services since 1986.
- The Pocono Springs Civic Association sued the MacKenzies for unpaid association fees.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court granted summary judgment in favor of the Pocono Springs Civic Association.
- The MacKenzies appealed to the Superior Court of Pennsylvania.

**Issue**
- Can real property owned in fee simple with perfect title be abandoned under Pennsylvania law?

**Holding**
- The Pocono Springs Civic Association wins. Real property owned in fee simple with perfect title cannot be abandoned under Pennsylvania law. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Abandonment of Property.** Abandoned property is that "to which an owner has voluntarily relinquished all right, title, claim and possession with the intention of terminating his ownership, but without vesting it in any other person and with the intention of not reclaiming further possession or resuming ownership, possession or enjoyment."
- **Abandonment of Perfect Title.** The doctrine of abandonment does not apply to perfect titles in real property.
- **Presumption of Possession.** Absent proof to the contrary, possession is presumed to be in the party who has record title.
- **Non-payment of Taxes/Non-use.** Abandonment of title is not presumed from a mere failure to possess the land or from neglect to pay the taxes thereon.
- **Rejected: Intent to Abandon.** Intent is an element in the doctrine of abandonment only if the claim survives a motion for summary judgment, and only in cases involving personal property.

**Reasoning**
- The MacKenzies have not relinquished their rights, title, claim, and possession of their lot; they remain owners in fee simple with a recorded deed and perfect title.
- Pennsylvania law does not allow for the abandonment of real property when owned in fee simple with perfect title.
- The MacKenzies' actions, such as non-use, refusal to pay taxes, and offers to sell, do not constitute abandonment of perfect title.
- The MacKenzies' intent to abandon is irrelevant because the law states that real property cannot be abandoned.
- The deed contains a covenant requiring the MacKenzies, as property owners, to pay annual dues and assessments to the Association.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes a bright-line rule that **perfect title** to real property cannot be abandoned, contrasting with cases like *Ghen v. Rich* where abandonment of personal property (a whale) was possible. It highlights the strong protection afforded to record title in real estate, even against the owner's express intent to abandon. This case is similar to *Eyerman v. Mercantile Trust Co.* in that it limits an owner's ability to dispose of property in a way that conflicts with established legal principles, even if the owner desires it.
2. What if the MacKenzies had tried to convey the property to the county or state, and they had accepted it? Would that be considered abandonment or a transfer of title?

**Takeaway:** Under Pennsylvania law, an owner cannot abandon real property held in fee simple with perfect title, regardless of their intent or actions like non-use and non-payment of taxes.

## Eyerman v. Mercantile Trust Co. (Missouri Court of Appeals, 1975)

**Facts**
- Louise Woodruff Johnston owned a home at #4 Kingsbury Place in St. Louis.
- Johnston died on January 14, 1973.
- Johnston's will directed her executor to "cause our home at 4 Kingsbury Place . . . to be razed and to sell the land upon which it is located . . . and to transfer the proceeds of the sale . . . to the residue of my estate."
- Kingsbury Place is a "private place" established in 1902 by a trust indenture, intended to be maintained as desirable, high-class residential property.
- The trust indenture allows lot owners or trustees to sue to enforce its covenants.
- The subdivision consists of handsome, spacious homes, all used exclusively as private residences, except for one vacant lot.
- The current value of the house and land is $40,000.
- The estate would receive no more than $5,000 for the empty lot after demolition costs of $4,350.
- Demolition would result in a loss of $39,350 to the estate.
- Kingsbury Place has been designated a landmark by the St. Louis Commission on Landmarks and Urban Design.
- Demolition would depreciate adjoining property values by an estimated $10,000.
- The cost to build a comparable house would be $200,000.
- Demolition would create a gap, allowing direct access to Kingsbury Place from an alley, potentially leading to detrimental uses.
- U.S. Census Bureau figures show a decrease of over 14% in St. Louis City housing units during the 1960s.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs (neighboring property owners and Kingsbury Place subdivision trustees) sought an injunction to prevent the demolition.
- The trial court dissolved a temporary restraining order and found against the plaintiffs on all issues.
- Plaintiffs appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a will direct the demolition of a valuable home if doing so would harm the community, neighbors, and the estate, and serve no apparent beneficial purpose?

**Holding**
- Plaintiffs win. A testamentary direction to destroy valuable property is void as against public policy when it causes substantial harm to others and serves no benefit. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Standing.** Persons threatened with wrongful interference of property rights may seek an injunction against a threatened nuisance.
- **Standing (Public Policy).** Individuals have standing to raise public policy issues if they allege a legally protectible interest and will be perceptibly harmed by the challenged action, even if the interests are aesthetic or environmental and shared by the community.
- **Inheritance and Will.** The taking of property by inheritance or will is not an absolute or natural right but one created by the laws of the sovereign power. The state may grant this right upon conditions.
- **Public Policy (General).** Public policy conflicts with the morals of the time and contravenes an established interest of society; acts are against public policy when they have a mischievous tendency injurious to the state's interests.
- **Public Policy (Judicial Determination).** In cases of first impression without guiding statutes, judicial decisions, or constitutional provisions, a judicial determination of public policy must be "so plainly right as to be supported by the general will."
- **Public Policy (Testamentary Conditions).** A testator may not impose conditions in a will that are uncertain, unlawful, or opposed to public policy.
- **Capricious Destruction.** It is against public policy to allow an executor to exercise a power to destroy property if the purpose is merely capricious and confers no other interest in the property.

**Reasoning**
- Plaintiffs have standing because they pleaded and proved facts showing a personal, legally protectible interest.
    - Demolition would depreciate their property values and affect the aesthetic and historical qualities of their neighborhood.
    - The trust indenture empowers them to enforce covenants.
- The demolition would result in an unwarranted loss to the estate, plaintiffs, and the public.
    - The house and land are worth $40,000, but the empty lot would yield only $5,000, less $4,350 for demolition, resulting in a $39,350 loss.
    - Kingsbury Place is architecturally significant and a designated landmark.
    - Demolition would depreciate adjoining property values by $10,000.
    - The space created would permit direct access from an alley, increasing the likelihood of detrimental uses.
    - There is a pressing need for dwelling units in St. Louis, as shown by census data.
- No individual, group, or the community benefits from the destruction; only the "caprice of the dead testatrix is served."
    - The will provides no reason for the eccentric condition.
    - A living person has more freedom with their property, but a will's conditions are subject to judicial review.
- The state has the power to regulate inheritance and wills, as it is a right created by law, not an absolute right.
- Precedent supports invalidating capricious testamentary conditions.
    - *Egerton v. Brownlow* (1853) held that while an owner can leave land uncultivated, they cannot compel a successor to do so if it's against public good.
    - *M'Caig's Trustees* (1915) struck down a will directing the erection of statues with no public benefit, calling it an "absurd whim."
    - *In re Scott's Will* (1903) assumed a direction to destroy money was void.
    - Restatement, Second, Trusts § 124, states it's against public policy to allow a power of destruction if the purpose is merely capricious.
    - *Brown v. Burdett* (1882) invalidated a will directing a house to be boarded up for 20 years.
    - *Colonial Trust Co. v. Brown* (1926) invalidated restrictions on property development that were against the interests of beneficiaries and public welfare.
- The court does not reach the issues of private nuisance or restrictive covenants, though they "may have merit."
- The injunction will not defeat the testatrix's trust purposes, as the proceeds from the sale of the property will still go to the residual estate; only the destructive condition is enjoined.

**Dissent (Clemens)**
- The majority's opinion is based on "wispy, self-proclaimed public policy grounds" that were vaguely pleaded and not in evidence.
- The court should not assume knowledge of the testatrix's motives, which are unknown.
- The beneficiaries have not protested the razing, and the City is not a party.
- The trust indenture does not bar razing, as it acknowledges the existence of vacant lots and empowers trustees to maintain them.
- The nuisance claim is based on conjecture, not certainty, and there's no evidence a second vacant lot would be untended or cause nuisance.
- Public policy should be determined by the Constitution, statutes, and judicial decisions, not by judges' varying opinions or "spasmodic sentiment."
- The "law favors the free and untrammeled use of real property," and an owner has exclusive control subject only to not substantially impairing another's right to enjoy their property.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important because it limits the **dead hand control** of property, showing that a testator's wishes are not absolute, especially when they conflict with public policy. It connects to the idea that property rights are not solely individual but also have a social dimension. It contrasts with cases like *Mountain Brow Lodge No. 82 v. Toscano*, where conditions on land use were upheld if they were not absolute restraints on alienation, but here the condition is outright destruction. It also touches on the concept of waste, similar to how courts might view a life tenant's actions that diminish the value for remaindermen.
2. What if the will had stated a specific, non-capricious reason for demolition, such as a desire to prevent a specific person from living there, or to fund a specific charity with the higher proceeds from a vacant lot sale (if that were the case)? Would the public policy argument still prevail?

**Takeaway:** A testamentary instruction to destroy valuable property is void as against public policy if it causes substantial harm to the community and others without serving any beneficial purpose.

## ProCD Inc. v. Zeidenberg (United States Court of Appeals, Seventh Circuit, 1996)

**Facts**
- ProCD compiled a database of information from over 3,000 telephone directories, which cost over $10 million to create and maintain.
- ProCD sold this database, called SelectPhone, on CD-ROM discs.
- ProCD engaged in price discrimination, selling the consumer version for personal use at a low price (around $150) and charging a higher price to commercial users.
- To implement price discrimination and prevent arbitrage, ProCD included a license with its consumer product.
- This license was printed in the manual, encoded on the CD-ROM, and displayed on the user's screen every time the software ran, limiting use to noncommercial purposes.
- Matthew Zeidenberg bought a consumer package of SelectPhone in 1994.
- Zeidenberg ignored the license and formed Silken Mountain Web Services, Inc., to resell the information from the database on the Internet for a price lower than ProCD's commercial rate.
- Zeidenberg purchased two additional updated SelectPhone packages and made the latest information available online through his corporation.
- ProCD sued Zeidenberg seeking an injunction against further dissemination that exceeded the rights specified in the licenses.

**Procedural History**
- The district court held that the shrinkwrap licenses were not enforceable contracts because their terms were inside the box and not on the outside, and that federal law (Copyright Act § 301(a)) preempted their enforcement even if they were contracts.
- ProCD appealed.

**Issue**
- Are shrinkwrap licenses enforceable contracts under the Uniform Commercial Code, and are they preempted by the Copyright Act?

**Holding**
- ProCD (plaintiff) wins. Shrinkwrap licenses are enforceable contracts under the UCC, and their enforcement is not preempted by the Copyright Act. Reversed and Remanded.

**Rules**
- **Contract Formation (UCC § 2-204(1)).** A contract for the sale of goods may be made in any manner sufficient to show agreement, including conduct by both parties which recognizes the existence of such a contract.
- **Acceptance of Goods (UCC § 2-606(1)(b)).** A buyer accepts goods when, after an opportunity to inspect, they fail to make an effective rejection.
- **Preemption (Copyright Act § 301(a)).** State law rights are preempted if they are equivalent to any of the exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright in works of authorship that are fixed in a tangible medium and come within the subject matter of copyright.
- **Contractual Rights vs. Copyright Rights.** Rights created by contract generally affect only their parties and do not create "exclusive rights" against the world, unlike copyright.

**Reasoning**
- The district court erred in holding that shrinkwrap licenses are not contracts because their terms are inside the box.
    - Transactions where money precedes detailed terms are common (e.g., insurance, airline tickets, concert tickets, consumer goods with warranties).
    - Requiring all terms on the outside of a package would be impractical and could remove more useful information.
    - The UCC allows contract formation by conduct, and a vendor can invite acceptance by conduct, such as using software after having an opportunity to read the license.
    - ProCD offered the license terms, and Zeidenberg accepted by using the software after having the opportunity to read the license and reject the goods.
    - The opportunity to return goods if the terms are unacceptable is important for contract formation in this context.
    - The UCC does not require special prominence for ordinary terms in shrinkwrap licenses.
    - Competition among vendors, not judicial revision of contract terms, protects consumers.
- The district court erred in holding that the Copyright Act § 301(a) preempts the enforcement of shrinkwrap licenses.
    - ProCD's software and data are "fixed in a tangible medium of expression" and "within the subject matter of copyright."
    - However, rights created by contract are not "equivalent to any of the exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright."
    - Copyright creates rights against the world, while contracts generally affect only the parties to the agreement.
    - Enforcing contracts about intellectual property, like trade secrets, does not affect strangers' ability to use information independently.
    - The enforcement of the shrinkwrap license does not withdraw any information from the public domain, as everyone remains free to copy the original telephone books.
    - Contractual terms facilitate price discrimination, which can make information more readily available and reduce prices for consumer buyers.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is crucial for understanding **contract formation** in the digital age and the interplay between state contract law (UCC) and federal intellectual property law (Copyright Act). It contrasts with cases like *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* where property rights in biological materials were debated, showing how contractual agreements can define rights even when traditional property rights are unclear or limited. It also touches on the concept of **price discrimination** and how contracts can be used to achieve it, connecting to economic rationales for property rights.
2. The court emphasizes the "opportunity to return" as a key element of contract formation here. What if the software was downloaded directly without a physical product to return? Would the reasoning still hold, or would the "opportunity to reject" be harder to establish?

**Takeaway:** Shrinkwrap licenses are enforceable contracts under the UCC, and their enforcement is not preempted by federal copyright law, as contractual rights are distinct from the exclusive rights granted by copyright.

## Allen v. Hyatt Regency-Nashville Hotel (Tennessee Supreme Court, 1984)

**Facts**
- Hyatt Regency-Nashville Hotel operated a multi-story parking garage in Nashville, open to the public.
- On February 12, 1981, Edwin Allen, husband of the plaintiff Betty J. Allen, drove her new 1981 automobile into the garage.
- Mr. Allen took a ticket from an automatic dispenser, drove to the fourth floor, parked, locked the car, and kept the ignition key.
- He left the garage and returned several hours later to find the car gone.
- Mr. Allen reported the theft to the exit attendant, who stated the car "didn't come out here."
- He then reported the theft to hotel security personnel.
- A security guard told Mr. Allen that a lady had called earlier that day reporting "someone messing with a car" in the garage.
- The security guard investigated but found nothing unusual.
- The car was never recovered.
- The tickets dispensed stated that charges were for parking space only, the hotel assumed no responsibility for loss, cars were parked at the owner's risk, and parkers should lock their vehicles.
- The tickets were used to measure parking time for proper charge collection, not to identify specific vehicles.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court found a bailment was created.
- The intermediate appellate court affirmed the trial court's finding of a bailment.
- Hyatt Regency-Nashville Hotel appealed.

**Issue**
- Is a commercial parking garage operator liable for the theft of a vehicle when the owner parks and locks the car, keeps the keys, and receives a ticket from an automatic dispenser, thereby creating a bailment?

**Holding**
- Plaintiff (appellee) wins. A bailment for hire was created, and the garage operator is liable for the theft. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Bailment for Hire.** A bailment for hire is created when there is a delivery of personal property by one person to another for a specific purpose, with an express or implied contract that the property will be returned or accounted for.
- **Implied Bailment.** An implied bailment can be created by the conduct of the parties and the surrounding circumstances, particularly when there is sufficient delivery of possession and control to the alleged bailee.
- **Presumption of Negligence (T.C.A. § 24-5-111).** Upon proof of nondelivery of bailed property, a statutory presumption of negligence arises against the bailee.
- Rejected: **No Bailment Without Full Transfer of Possession and Control.** The dissent argues that a bailment requires a full transfer of possession and control to the bailee, excluding the owner and all others, and giving the bailee sole custody and control.

**Reasoning**
- The court found that the facts of this case were consistent with the legal requirements for a bailment for hire.
    - The garage was an enclosed, indoor, attended commercial garage with an attendant controlling the exit and regular security personnel patrolling.
    - The operator assumed control and custody of the vehicles parked by limiting access and requiring a ticket for exit.
    - The attendant's statement that the car "didn't come out here" implied that the attendant was either in error, inattentive, or away from their station, as there was no other exit.
- The court relied on prior Tennessee decisions, particularly *Scruggs v. Dennis*, which found an implied bailment in a similar park-and-lock situation where a ticket was dispensed by a machine and the owner retained keys.
- The court acknowledged the question of sufficient delivery when the owner locks the vehicle and keeps the keys but emphasized the "realities of the situation" where the operator is expected to provide protection.
- The hotel made no effort to rebut the statutory presumption of negligence that arose from the nondelivery of the vehicle.
- The court rejected the idea of placing the risk of loss on the consuming public against commercial parking operators in such circumstances.
- The language on the ticket disclaiming responsibility was not sufficient to exonerate the hotel because it was not shown that the customer read it or had it called to his attention. *(dicta)*

**Dissent (DROWOTA, J.)**
- The dissent argues that no bailment existed because there was no sufficient delivery of possession and control from the owner to the garage operator.
- The dissent emphasizes that the owner parked and locked the car, retained the keys, and the ticket did not identify the vehicle.
- The cashier's role was merely to collect payment, not to identify and return a specific article, and the garage could not move the car or know to whom it belonged.
- The dissent believes the majority's application of bailment law to modern automated parking facilities, particularly in cases like *Scruggs*, stretches the traditional elements of delivery, custody, and control too far.
- The dissent points out that the garage was sometimes entirely open without a cashier, further undermining the idea of the operator having sole custody and control.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the concept of **bailment** in property law, particularly how it applies to modern commercial services like parking garages. It connects to the idea of possession and control as key elements in establishing property interests. The majority's reasoning highlights how courts can adapt traditional legal concepts to new factual scenarios, while the dissent emphasizes strict adherence to traditional definitions. This case can be compared with *Williams v. Ford Motor Credit Co.* regarding the importance of possession and control in different contexts.
2. The case raises the question of how much control is "sufficient" to establish a bailment, especially when the owner retains keys and locks the property. What if the garage had no attendant at all, or if the attendant was only present for a few hours a day?

**Takeaway:** A commercial parking garage can be found to have created an implied bailment for hire, making it liable for vehicle theft, even when the owner parks, locks the car, and retains the keys, if the garage exercises sufficient control over access and exit.

## Cowen v. Pressprich (New York Supreme Court, Appellate Term, 1922)

**Facts**
- Plaintiffs, stockbrokers, agreed to sell and deliver an Oregon Short Line Railroad bond to defendants, also stockbrokers.
- Plaintiffs ordered the bond from a third bond house.
- The third bond house mistakenly sent plaintiffs an Oregon and California Railroad bond.
- Plaintiffs, also by mistake, sent this Oregon and California bond to defendants via a messenger boy, Goldberg, along with a slip describing an Oregon Short Line bond.
- Goldberg dropped the envelope containing the bond and slip through a slot in defendants' delivery window.
- Defendants' partner, Mr. Quackenbush, immediately saw the bond was an Oregon and California bond, not the Oregon Short Line bond they expected.
- Quackenbush opened the window and yelled "Cowen."
- An unidentified boy, not Goldberg, stepped up, mumbled "all right," took the bond, and left.
- The bond was a fully negotiable bearer bond.
- Defendants refused to compensate plaintiffs for the loss of the bond.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs sued defendants for conversion in Municipal Court.
- The Municipal Court found in favor of the plaintiffs.
- Defendants appealed to the New York Supreme Court, Appellate Term.

**Issue**
- Are involuntary bailees absolutely liable for conversion when they attempt to return mistakenly delivered property but deliver it to the wrong person?

**Holding**
- Plaintiffs (respondents) win. When involuntary bailees exercise dominion over mistakenly delivered property by attempting to redeliver it, they become as responsible as voluntary bailees and are absolutely liable for delivery to the wrong person. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Voluntary Bailment Duty.** In voluntary bailments, the bailee has an absolute duty to deliver the bailed article to the right person, and delivery to the wrong person cannot be excused by care, good faith, or innocence.
- **Involuntary Bailee Custody.** An involuntary bailee, as long as their lack of volition continues, is not under a duty to care for or guard the subject of the bailment and cannot be held liable for negligence in custody.
- **Involuntary Bailee Dominion.** If an involuntary bailee exercises any dominion over the bailed thing, they become as responsible as if they were a voluntary bailee.
- **Rejected: Heugh v. L. & N. W. R. R. Co. Doctrine.** The doctrine that an involuntary bailee is not under an absolute duty to deliver to the proper person, but only to exercise reasonable and proper care, is rejected in American cases.

**Reasoning**
- The defendants became involuntary bailees upon the mistaken delivery of the bond.
- While an involuntary bailee is not initially obligated to care for the property, exercising dominion over it changes their responsibility.
- Defendants exercised dominion by undertaking to redeliver the bond.
- Once dominion is exercised, the standard of care becomes that of a voluntary bailee, which includes an absolute duty to deliver to the correct person.
- Defendants could have protected themselves by telephoning the plaintiffs or sending the bond back with their own messenger.
- Instead, they chose to return the bond to an unidentified messenger, acting at their peril.
- The court distinguishes *Krumsky v. Loeser* because that case involved fraud and the defendants' total lack of knowledge of the true owner, neither of which applied here.
- The action was tried as one for conversion, and the judgment can only be sustained on that theory.

**Dissent (Lehman, J.)**
- The defendants were put in possession of the bond by mistake and promptly discovered it.
- They committed no "overt act" of interference with the bond except attempting to divest themselves of possession by delivering it to a person they believed to be the plaintiffs' messenger.
- This act was consistent with the plaintiffs' continued ownership and was an honest attempt to restore possession.
- An implied contract of bailment, with its consequent obligations, arises only where a person does some act inconsistent with not accepting the possession thrust upon them.
- The defendants did not claim title or right to possession; they merely transferred physical possession.
- If the defendants used unreasonable means to return the goods, they might be liable for negligence, but not conversion, as they did not exercise dominion.
- The defendants acted in good faith and under the reasonable belief they were returning the bond to the correct messenger.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the concept of **bailment**, particularly **involuntary bailment**, and the point at which an involuntary bailee's duties increase. It contrasts with cases like *Berg v. Wiley*, where a landlord's self-help repossession was deemed conversion, by focusing on the bailee's actions regarding mistakenly received property rather than a landlord-tenant relationship. The case highlights the strict liability aspect of conversion for misdelivery once dominion is exercised, even if the initial possession was involuntary.
2. The dissent raises an interesting point about the distinction between an "overt act" of dominion that creates an absolute liability for conversion versus an "honest attempt" to return property that might only lead to liability for negligence if the means were unreasonable. This tension could be explored in class.

**Takeaway:** An involuntary bailee who attempts to return mistakenly delivered property becomes absolutely liable for conversion if they deliver it to the wrong person, as this act constitutes exercising dominion over the property.

## Williams v. Estate of Williams (Tennessee Supreme Court, 1993)

**Facts**
- G.A. Williams died on November 17, 1944.
- G.A. Williams' will, executed July 18, 1933, and probated November 24, 1944, stated: "At my death I want Ida Williams, Mallie Williams, and Ethel Williams, three of my daughters to have my home farm... to have and to hold during their lives, and not to be sold during their lifetime. If any of them marry their interest ceases and the ones that remain single have full control of same."
- The will also stated the reason for this devise was that the daughters "have stayed at home and taken care of the home and cared for their mother during her sickness, and I do not want them sold out of a home."
- The will included a clause debarring any contestant from an interest in the estate.
- G.A. Williams was survived by nine children, including the three named daughters.
- Ethel Williams, the plaintiff, was 92 years old when the complaint was filed and is the only survivor of the three named daughters.
- Ethel Williams maintained possession of the farm since G.A. Williams' death, jointly with Ida and Mallie Williams until their deaths.

**Procedural History**
- Ethel Williams filed a complaint seeking construction of the will, alleging she received a life estate or a life estate with a remainder interest by intestate succession.
- The Chancellor initially found a life estate with a remainder interest by intestate succession, but on rehearing, found the devise was a fee simple.
- The Court of Appeals affirmed the trial court, holding each named daughter owned a one-third undivided interest in fee simple.
- Defendants-appellants appealed to the Tennessee Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Does a will devising property "to have and to hold during their lives" and stating "If any of them marry their interest ceases" create a fee simple interest or a determinable life estate?

**Holding**
Defendant (Estate of G.A. Williams, et al.) wins. The will created a determinable life estate for each daughter, not a fee simple. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Testator's Intent.** The function of a suit to construe a will is to ascertain and effectuate the predominant intention of the testator as expressed in the will.
- **Construction of Subsidiary Clauses.** When a controlling or predominant purpose of the testator is expressed, all subsidiary clauses should be construed to subordinate to that purpose.
- **Rules of Construction.** A rule of construction will not be applied if it would defeat the clear intention of the testator.
- **Statutory Presumption of Fee Simple.** Rejected: T.C.A. § 32-3-101 (1984) (a will conveys all real estate unless a contrary intention appears) does not control when a contrary intention is clear from the will.
- **Determinable Life Estate.** A gift to a person "for life or until her marriage, or a gift to A for life or during her widowhood" creates a life estate that is defeasible or determinable upon the marriage or remarriage of the recipient.

**Reasoning**
- The predominant intention of the testator was clear: to provide a residence and support for his three unmarried daughters who had cared for their mother.
    - The phrases "during their lives" and "If any of them marry their interest ceases" explicitly limit the duration of the estate.
    - The statements "not to be sold during their lifetime" and "I do not want them sold out of a home" reinforce the intent to provide a residence and support, not an absolute gift.
    - The testator's reason for the devise (daughters stayed home and cared for their mother) indicates the devise was a substitute for other support, further suggesting a limited interest.
- The will did not create an absolute gift because the interest was not readily alienable and could not be defeated by partition, which aligns with the testator's stated purpose.
- The provision that an interest would terminate upon marriage is a strong indication that the testator did not intend an absolute estate.
- The court distinguished *White v. Brown* because, in this case, a contrary intention clearly appears from the will, making the statutory presumption of a fee simple inapplicable.
- The testator, despite not being a skilled legal draftsman, used common legal phrases ("last will and testament," "sound mind," "to have and to hold") which suggests he understood the implications of his chosen language.
- Upon the death of the testator, each named daughter received a life estate determinable upon marriage, with an executory interest in the other daughters' shares if they died or married.
- The testator's heirs-at-law held a reversion in fee simple, subject to these determinable life estates and executory interests.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case highlights the importance of discerning the **testator's intent** in will construction, even when the language might seem to conflict with common presumptions (like the presumption of a fee simple). It connects to the concept of **defeasible fees** and **future interests**, specifically **determinable life estates** and **executory interests**, which are crucial for understanding how property rights can be limited and transferred over time. The court's discussion of *White v. Brown* and the statutory presumption of a fee simple shows how specific language can overcome general rules of construction.
2. What if one of the daughters had married and then divorced? Would her interest be reinstated? The will states "If any of them marry their interest ceases," implying a permanent cessation, but this is not explicitly addressed.

**Takeaway:** A will's specific language creating a determinable life estate, even if not perfectly drafted, will be upheld over a statutory presumption of a fee simple when the testator's intent is clear.

## Brokaw v. Fairchild (New York Supreme Court, 1929)

**Facts**
- In 1886, Isaac V. Brokaw bought a plot of land in Manhattan and in 1887, built a residence at 1 East Seventy-ninth Street for his own occupancy.
- Isaac V. Brokaw died in 1913.
- Isaac V. Brokaw's will and codicil devised the residence at 1 East Seventy-ninth Street to his son, George Tuttle Brokaw (plaintiff), for life, with the remainder to George's children.
- The will also devised other contiguous parcels to other family members for life, with specific restrictions on those parcels regarding light and air.
- Since 1913, the neighborhood around the residence has changed, with many apartments being built and few new private residences.
- The plaintiff found the residence difficult to rent and expensive to maintain.
- The plaintiff proposed to demolish the existing residence and erect a thirteen-story apartment building, arguing it would be more profitable and beneficial to the inheritance and remaindermen.
- The adult remaindermen (defendants) objected to the demolition, claiming it would constitute waste and violate a testamentary scheme.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiff brought an action under the Civil Practice Act seeking a declaration that he has the right to remove the present structures and erect new ones.
- The court denied motions to dismiss by the defendants.
- The New York Supreme Court ruled in favor of the defendants.

**Issue**
Can a life tenant demolish a valuable residence and replace it with a more profitable apartment building, against the objections of the remaindermen, when the will creating the life estate specifically referred to the property as "my residence"?

**Holding**
Defendant (remaindermen) wins. The plaintiff, as life tenant, does not have the right to demolish the existing residence and erect a new structure, as such an act would constitute waste. Adjudged and declared.

**Rules**
- **Waste.** Any act of a life tenant that causes permanent injury to the inheritance is waste.
- **Life Tenant's Rights.** A life tenant may use and enjoy the estate as they received it, but they do not have the right to exercise an act of dominion or ownership that changes the nature of the property.
- **Changing the Estate.** The law considers whether the tenant's act changes the estate in a way that injures the inheritance, not whether the act might add value.
- **Rejected: Ameliorating Waste.** The court rejects the argument that a tenant can commit waste if the act would be beneficial to the inheritance, especially when the remaindermen object.

**Reasoning**
- The testator's will repeatedly referred to the property as "my residence," indicating an intent to preserve the specific building.
- Demolishing the existing residence and building an apartment house would fundamentally change the inheritance from "my residence" to something entirely different.
- The life tenant's right is to use the property, not to exercise ownership by making substantial alterations that prevent the property from being delivered to the remaindermen in substantially the same form.
- The court is not concerned with the financial wisdom of the remaindermen's objections or their motives.
- The cases cited by the plaintiff (Melms v. Pabst Brewing Co., N. Y., O. & W. Ry. Co. v. Livingston, Doherty v. Allman) are distinguishable because they involved different factual circumstances (e.g., property becoming valueless for its original purpose due to external changes, condemnation proceedings, or situations where an injunction was denied but damages were still possible).
- In this case, the residence is still valuable and located in a desirable area, unlike the "isolated and alone" dwelling in *Melms*.
- The prior proceeding regarding a mortgage for the proposed apartment building established the remaindermen's right to object, which applies here.
- The court found no evidence of a "testamentary scheme" limiting the plaintiff's life estate or connecting the properties in a way that would justify the demolition.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a classic example of the common law doctrine of waste, particularly **ameliorating waste**, where a life tenant makes changes that might increase the property's value but fundamentally alter its character. It connects to *Melms v. Pabst Brewing Co.* by distinguishing its facts and rejecting the idea that a life tenant can make changes that are economically beneficial if they alter the inheritance. It emphasizes the remaindermen's right to receive the property in substantially the same condition as it was when the life estate began, reflecting a strong preference for preserving the original character of the inheritance.
2. This case raises the question of how much control a testator should have over the future use of their property, especially when economic conditions change dramatically. What if the property truly became derelict and unsalvageable without demolition, even if the remaindermen still objected?

**Takeaway:** A life tenant cannot commit waste by demolishing a valuable structure, even if the proposed new structure would be more profitable, if it fundamentally alters the character of the inheritance against the remaindermen's wishes.

## Mountain Brow Lodge No. 82 v. Toscano (Court of Appeal of the State of California, 1967)

**Facts**
- On April 6, 1950, James V. Toscano and Maria Toscano (grantors) conveyed a parcel of real property by gift deed to Mountain Brow Lodge No. 82 (appellant), a nonprofit corporation.
- The deed's habendum clause stated: "Said property is restricted for the use and benefit of the second party, only; and in the event the same fails to be used by the second party or in the event of sale or transfer by the second party of all or any part of said lot, the same is to revert to the first parties herein, their successors, heirs or assigns.”
- The grantors are now deceased.
- Appellant sought to quiet its title to the property against the estates of the deceased grantors.
- The trial court rendered judgment in favor of the respondents (trustees and administrators of the estates).
- The parties stipulated that the only question on appeal is whether the conditions in the deed are void.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court rendered judgment in favor of respondents, refusing to quiet appellant's title.
- Appellant appealed.
- The Court of Appeal affirmed the judgment as modified.

**Issue**
Can a deed validly create a fee simple subject to a condition subsequent that restricts the use of the property to the grantee, even if it effectively limits who can use the land?

**Holding**
Respondent (the estates of the grantors) wins. The condition restricting the use of the property to the grantee for its organizational purposes created a valid fee simple subject to a condition subsequent, and this condition is severable from the void condition against alienation. Affirmed as modified.

**Rules**
- **Restraints on Alienation.** Conditions restraining alienation, when repugnant to the interest created, are void. (California Civil Code section 711)
- **Severability of Conditions.** If a deed contains multiple conditions, an invalid condition does not necessarily affect or nullify a valid, severable condition.
- **Fee Simple Subject to Condition Subsequent.** No formal language is necessary to create a fee simple subject to a condition subsequent as long as the grantor's intent is clear from the words used and surrounding circumstances.
- **Construction of Deeds.** The object in construing a deed is to ascertain the intention of the grantor from the words employed and from surrounding circumstances.
- **Rejected: Restraint on Alienation (Use Restriction).** A condition restricting land use to the grantee's organizational purposes is not an absolute restraint on alienation, even if it limits who can use the land.

**Reasoning**
- The condition prohibiting sale or transfer is an absolute restraint against alienation and is void under Civil Code section 711.
- However, the condition against alienation and the condition relating to land use are in the disjunctive ("or") and are clearly severable.
- The invalid restraint on alienation does not affect the validity of the land use condition.
- The term "use" in the deed, when applied to real property and considering appellant's nature as a nonprofit lodge, fraternal, and similar organization, means the land was conveyed on condition that it would be used for those organizational purposes.
- This interpretation is supported by the fact that the conveyance was a gift deed in consideration of "love and affection" and one grantor was an active member of the lodge.
- Conditions restricting land use have been consistently upheld in California, even if they hamper alienation.
- Restricting use to a specific entity (like a city for city purposes, a school district for school purposes, or a church for church purposes) is a valid use restriction, not an invalid restraint on alienation.
- Other jurisdictions also recognize that conditions limiting use to a specific organization create defeasible estates.
- In doubtful cases, the preferred construction is in favor of an estate subject to a condition subsequent over a fee simple determinable. *(dicta)*
- The rule against perpetuities does not apply to defeasible estates because reversions, possibilities of reverter, and powers of termination are inherently vested. *(dicta)*

**Dissent (Stone, J.)**
- The entire habendum clause is an invalid restraint upon alienation.
- Even if the "sale or transfer" language is removed, the use restriction ("used by only the I.O.O.F. Lodge No. 82") prevents the grantee from conveying the property just as effectively as an explicit prohibition on sale.
- The effect of language should be judged by what it does; if two different terms generate the same legal result, they should be treated alike.
- The policy against restraints on alienation is important, as organizations may cease to exist, leading to fragmented titles among numerous heirs.
- The majority's cited cases involve restrictions on *how* property may be used, not *who* may use it. A restriction on the kind of use does not restrain alienation because the property can still be conveyed, subject to the restriction.
- This restriction accomplishes no reasonable purpose that would justify an indefinite suspension of alienation.
- There is a correlation between a restriction on who may use property and the exclusion of racial groups, which is prohibited by Civil Code section 782. Allowing restrictions like this could open the door to "inverse exclusionary clauses" to violate section 782.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the distinction between a **restraint on alienation** and a **fee simple subject to a condition subsequent** (or fee simple determinable). It shows that a condition limiting the *use* of property to a specific grantee for its organizational purposes is generally considered valid, even if it indirectly limits who can possess or benefit from the land. This contrasts with cases like *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* where the court grappled with the alienability of property interests in human cells. The dissent's argument about the practical effect of the use restriction on alienability is a key point of tension.
2. The dissent raises a compelling point about the practical effect of the use restriction being the same as a direct restraint on alienation. Is the court's distinction between "who uses it" and "how it is used" truly meaningful in terms of marketability? What if the Lodge ceases to exist or moves?

**Takeaway:** A condition in a deed restricting property use to the grantee's specific organizational purposes creates a valid defeasible estate, even if it effectively limits who can use the land, and is severable from an invalid direct restraint on alienation.
