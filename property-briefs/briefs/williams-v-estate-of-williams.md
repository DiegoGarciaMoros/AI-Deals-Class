## Williams v. Estate of Williams (Tennessee Supreme Court, 1993)

**Facts**
- G.A. Williams executed a will on July 18, 1933.
- G.A. Williams died on November 17, 1944.
- The will was probated on November 24, 1944.
- G.A. Williams was survived by nine children, including three daughters named in the will: Ida Williams, Mallie Williams, and Ethel Williams.
- The will stated: "At my death I want Ida Williams, Mallie Williams, and Ethel Williams, three of my daughters to have my home farm where I now live, consisting of one hundred and eighty-eight acres, to have and to hold during their lives, and not to be sold during their lifetime. If any of them marry their interest ceases and the ones that remain single have full control of same. I am making this will because they have stayed at home and taken care of the home and cared for their mother during her sickness, and I do not want them sold out of a home."
- Ethel Williams, the plaintiff, was 92 years old when the complaint was filed and is the only surviving named daughter.
- Ethel Williams maintained possession of the farm since the testator's death, jointly with Ida Williams and Mallie Williams until their deaths.
- None of the three named daughters ever married.

**Procedural History**
- Ethel Williams filed a complaint seeking construction of the will.
- The trial court initially found a life estate with a remainder interest by intestate succession, then on rehearing, found a fee simple interest.
- The Court of Appeals affirmed the trial court's finding of a fee simple interest.
- The defendants (Estate of G.A. Williams, et al.) appealed to the Tennessee Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Did the will of G.A. Williams grant his three daughters a fee simple interest or a determinable life estate in the home farm?

**Holding**
The defendants (Estate of G.A. Williams, et al.) win. The will granted the daughters a determinable life estate, not a fee simple interest. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Will Construction.** The function of a suit to construe a will is to ascertain and effect the intention of the testator.
- **Predominant Intention.** When a controlling or predominant purpose of the testator is expressed, it is the duty of the court to effectuate that purpose and to construe all subsidiary clauses so as to bring them into subordination to such purpose.
- **Testator's Intention.** The intention of the testator must be ascertained from what the testator has written into the will and not from what some interested party supposes that he intended to do.
- **Rules of Construction.** Under no circumstances will a rule of construction be applied that would operate to defeat the clear intention of the testator.
- **Devise for Life or Until Marriage.** A gift to A for life or until her marriage, or during her widowhood, gives a life estate to A, which is defeasible or determinable upon the marriage of A.
- **Rejected: Statutory Presumption of Fee Simple.** A will shall convey all real estate belonging to the testator unless a contrary intention appears by its words in context. (Rejected as controlling because a contrary intention appeared in the will).

**Reasoning**
- The predominant intention of the testator was to provide a residence and support for his three unmarried daughters who had cared for their mother.
    - The will explicitly states the farm is "to have and to hold during their lives" and "not to be sold during their lifetime," and "I do not want them sold out of a home."
    - The provision that "If any of them marry their interest ceases" further indicates a limited interest tied to their unmarried status and need for support.
    - The testator's reason for the devise was that the daughters "stayed at home and taken care of the home and cared for their mother," implying the devise was a substitute for other means of support.
- The testator did not intend to make an absolute gift (fee simple) to the daughters.
    - The limitations on the devise ("during their lives," "if any of them marry their interest ceases") are inconsistent with a fee simple.
    - The will created an interest not readily alienable and not subject to partition, which is characteristic of a limited estate.
- The will's language, though not from a skilled legal draftsman, clearly indicates a limited estate.
    - The testator used terms like "last will and testament" and "to have and to hold," suggesting familiarity with legal concepts.
    - If a fee simple was intended, the testator would have expressed it clearly.
- The daughters received a **determinable life estate**, defeasible upon marriage.
    - Each daughter also held an **executory interest** in the other two daughters' shares, vesting if another life tenant died or married while she remained unmarried.
    - The testator's heirs-at-law held a **reversion in fee simple**, subject to these determinable life estates and executory interests.
- The case is distinguishable from *White v. Brown* because a clear contrary intention appeared in this will, particularly the marriage condition.

None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the importance of a testator's intent in will construction, particularly when dealing with potentially ambiguous language regarding the duration of an estate. It contrasts with *White v. Brown* by finding a clear "contrary intention" that prevents a statutory presumption of a fee simple, emphasizing that specific conditions (like marriage) can define a lesser estate. It connects to the concept of **defeasible fees** and **life estates**, showing how conditions can terminate an interest.

**Takeaway:** A will's specific conditions and the testator's predominant intent can create a determinable life estate, even when general statutory presumptions might otherwise suggest a fee simple.
