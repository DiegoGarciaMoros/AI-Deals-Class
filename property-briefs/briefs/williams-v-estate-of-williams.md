## Williams v. Estate of Williams (Tennessee Supreme Court, 1993)

**Facts**
- G.A. Williams died on November 17, 1944.
- G.A. Williams' will, executed July 18, 1933, and probated November 24, 1944, stated: "At my death I want Ida Williams, Mallie Williams, and Ethel Williams, three of my daughters to have my home farm... to have and to hold during their lives, and not to be sold during their lifetime. If any of them marry their interest ceases and the ones that remain single have full control of same."
- The will also stated the reason for this devise was that the daughters "have stayed at home and taken care of the home and cared for their mother during her sickness, and I do not want them sold out of a home."
- The will included a clause debarring any contestant from an interest in the estate.
- G.A. Williams was survived by nine children, including the three named daughters.
- Ethel Williams, the plaintiff, was 92 years old when the complaint was filed and is the only survivor of the three named daughters.
- Ethel Williams maintained possession of the farm since G.A. Williams' death, jointly with Ida and Mallie Williams until their deaths.

**Procedural History**
- Ethel Williams filed a complaint seeking construction of the will, alleging she received a life estate or a life estate with a remainder interest by intestate succession.
- The Chancellor initially found a life estate with a remainder interest by intestate succession, but on rehearing, found the devise was a fee simple.
- The Court of Appeals affirmed the trial court, holding each named daughter owned a one-third undivided interest in fee simple.
- Defendants-appellants appealed to the Tennessee Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Does a will devising property "to have and to hold during their lives" and stating "If any of them marry their interest ceases" create a fee simple interest or a determinable life estate?

**Holding**
Defendant (Estate of G.A. Williams, et al.) wins. The will created a determinable life estate for each daughter, not a fee simple. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Testator's Intent.** The function of a suit to construe a will is to ascertain and effectuate the predominant intention of the testator as expressed in the will.
- **Construction of Subsidiary Clauses.** When a controlling or predominant purpose of the testator is expressed, all subsidiary clauses should be construed to subordinate to that purpose.
- **Rules of Construction.** A rule of construction will not be applied if it would defeat the clear intention of the testator.
- **Statutory Presumption of Fee Simple.** Rejected: T.C.A. § 32-3-101 (1984) (a will conveys all real estate unless a contrary intention appears) does not control when a contrary intention is clear from the will.
- **Determinable Life Estate.** A gift to a person "for life or until her marriage, or a gift to A for life or during her widowhood" creates a life estate that is defeasible or determinable upon the marriage or remarriage of the recipient.

**Reasoning**
- The predominant intention of the testator was clear: to provide a residence and support for his three unmarried daughters who had cared for their mother.
    - The phrases "during their lives" and "If any of them marry their interest ceases" explicitly limit the duration of the estate.
    - The statements "not to be sold during their lifetime" and "I do not want them sold out of a home" reinforce the intent to provide a residence and support, not an absolute gift.
    - The testator's reason for the devise (daughters stayed home and cared for their mother) indicates the devise was a substitute for other support, further suggesting a limited interest.
- The will did not create an absolute gift because the interest was not readily alienable and could not be defeated by partition, which aligns with the testator's stated purpose.
- The provision that an interest would terminate upon marriage is a strong indication that the testator did not intend an absolute estate.
- The court distinguished *White v. Brown* because, in this case, a contrary intention clearly appears from the will, making the statutory presumption of a fee simple inapplicable.
- The testator, despite not being a skilled legal draftsman, used common legal phrases ("last will and testament," "sound mind," "to have and to hold") which suggests he understood the implications of his chosen language.
- Upon the death of the testator, each named daughter received a life estate determinable upon marriage, with an executory interest in the other daughters' shares if they died or married.
- The testator's heirs-at-law held a reversion in fee simple, subject to these determinable life estates and executory interests.

**NOTES:**
1. This case highlights the importance of discerning the **testator's intent** in will construction, even when the language might seem to conflict with common presumptions (like the presumption of a fee simple). It connects to the concept of **defeasible fees** and **future interests**, specifically **determinable life estates** and **executory interests**, which are crucial for understanding how property rights can be limited and transferred over time. The court's discussion of *White v. Brown* and the statutory presumption of a fee simple shows how specific language can overcome general rules of construction.
2. What if one of the daughters had married and then divorced? Would her interest be reinstated? The will states "If any of them marry their interest ceases," implying a permanent cessation, but this is not explicitly addressed.

**Takeaway:** A will's specific language creating a determinable life estate, even if not perfectly drafted, will be upheld over a statutory presumption of a fee simple when the testator's intent is clear.
