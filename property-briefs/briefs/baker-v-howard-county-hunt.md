## Baker v. Howard County Hunt (Court of Appeals of Maryland, 1936)

**Facts**
- In 1924, Dr. Laurence H. Baker and Rebekah W. Baker acquired a farm in Howard County, Maryland.
- From 1926 to 1929, Dr. Baker conducted experiments on rabbits on the farm and also raised hogs and chickens.
- In 1930, the Howard County Hunt, an unincorporated association, was formed. Philip Bowen, the huntsman, furnished and hunted a pack of hounds for the club.
- In 1931, Dr. Baker noticed fox hounds and riders on his farm but paid little attention as he had few animals.
- By 1933, Dr. Baker's rabbit stock had greatly increased.
- On January 14, 1933, fox hounds overran the Bakers' farm, and Mrs. Baker was bitten while trying to drive them off.
- Following this incident, Mr. T. Stockton Matthews, president of the club, sent a letter to Dr. Baker expressing regret for the annoyance and damage.
- Dr. Baker complained that no real effort was made to keep the hounds off his property, and they continued to trample crops, break hot frames, disturb animals, and annoy Mrs. Baker.
- On one occasion in February 1936, Dr. Baker shot several hounds that were in his chicken yard, frightening his chickens.
- On March 9, 1936, Dr. and Mrs. Baker filed a bill of complaint against the Howard County Hunt and Philip Bowen.

**Procedural History**
- The Bakers filed a bill of complaint in equity seeking an injunction.
- The trial court dismissed the bill.
- The Bakers appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a landowner obtain an injunction against repeated trespasses by fox hounds and hunters that interfere with the peaceful enjoyment of their property, even if the trespasses are not continuous?

**Holding**
- The Bakers (appellants) win. Equity can provide injunctive relief against a series of trespasses that seriously interfere with a landowner's right to peaceful enjoyment of their property. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Trespass by Animals.** While the owner of a dog not believed to be dangerous is not liable for its trespasses of its own volition, they are liable if they take it where they know it will likely damage property, or permit it to stray with that knowledge.
- **Injunction for Repeated Trespasses.** Equity will grant an injunction to prevent repeated trespasses that cause irreparable injury, where full and adequate relief cannot be granted at law, where the trespass goes to the destruction of the property as enjoyed, or to prevent a multiplicity of suits.
- **Adequacy of Legal Remedy.** To preclude equitable relief, the legal remedy must be fully adequate and complete, as practical and efficient as the remedy in equity.
- **Self-Defense of Property.** A landowner may kill a dog in defense of their person or property where circumstances justify a reasonable belief that such a measure is necessary to protect against harm.
- **Rejected: Fox Hunting Rights.** The rights of fox hunters are subordinate to the rights of landowners. There is no principle of law that justifies trespassing over the lands of others for fox hunting.

**Reasoning**
- The evidence showed that the defendants' hounds repeatedly overran the Bakers' farm and caused substantial injury.
- The defendants made no serious effort to prevent the trespasses, despite being warned.
- The resulting damage was of a character for which the Bakers could not obtain adequate relief at law.
    - The injuries were partly intangible and incapable of measurement, such as the interruption of Dr. Baker's rabbit experiments and interference with their plan to use the farm as a bird refuge.
    - The threat of continued trespasses interfered with their reasonable enjoyment of the property.
    - The expense and annoyance of prosecuting numerous actions at law for trifling damages made the legal remedy inadequate.
- The Bakers' act of shooting the hounds was justified under the circumstances, as the hounds were in the chicken yard frightening chickens, and thus did not bar equitable relief under the "clean hands" doctrine.
    - A statute permits killing any dog seen pursuing, worrying, wounding, or killing poultry or livestock.
- The court rejected the argument that the defendants' mere expression of an intention not to repeat a trespass would bar equitable relief, especially when their past conduct showed a contrary intention.
- The common law rule regarding liability for a single straying dog's trespasses does not apply to a pack of 15-20 hounds, which can cause substantial damage.

None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the limits of the **owner sovereignty** principle when faced with repeated invasions of property. It connects to cases like *Jacque v. Steenberg Homes* by emphasizing the importance of the right to exclude, but here, the remedy sought is an injunction rather than just damages, highlighting the inadequacy of legal remedies for certain types of trespass. It also touches on the common law rule regarding animal trespass, which is often distinguished from human trespass.
2. What if the fox hunting club offered to pay a yearly fee to the Bakers for the right to cross their land, but the Bakers still refused? Would the court still grant an injunction, or would it consider a liability rule (damages) more appropriate?

**Takeaway:** Equity will grant an injunction to protect a landowner's right to peaceful enjoyment against repeated trespasses, especially when legal remedies are inadequate.
