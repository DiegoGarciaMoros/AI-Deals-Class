## Gillmor v. Gillmor (Utah Supreme Court, 1984)

**Facts**
- Two brothers, Frank and Edward Lincoln Gillmor, owned several large parcels of land (33,000 acres) and used it for their ranching business.
- Upon Frank Gillmor's death, his one-half interest passed to his sons, Edward Leslie Gillmor (defendant) and C. Frank Gillmor.
- Edward Lincoln Gillmor continued the ranching business.
- Upon Edward Lincoln Gillmor's death, his one-half interest passed to his daughter, Florence Gillmor (plaintiff).
- Florence, C. Frank, and Edward Leslie Gillmor became **tenants in common** of the land.
- In May 1979, Florence sued Edward Leslie for an accounting and damages for his exclusive use of the property since January 1, 1979.
- Florence also filed a separate suit for partition of the common properties.
- The trial was divided into two phases to determine damages for different periods.
- In the first phase, the trial court found that from January 1, 1979, to May 31, 1980, Edward Leslie grazed livestock in a way that constituted exclusive use and excluded Florence.
- The trial court awarded Florence $21,544.91 for one-half the rental value for the first phase. Edward Leslie did not appeal this decision.
- In the second phase, the trial court found that from June 1, 1980, to December 31, 1980, Edward Leslie continued to graze his sheep and cattle or produce feed on the common properties, and this use was to the exclusion of Florence.
- Florence sent Edward Leslie a letter stating her intent to graze her livestock on the properties in proportion to her ownership and requested accommodation.
- Edward Leslie refused to respond and continued to graze the lands to their maximum capacity.
- Edward Leslie admitted that if Florence had attempted to put additional sheep on the land, he would have sought an injunction to prevent damage.
- Edward Leslie made repairs to a range fence and a ditch on the common property sometime after June 1, 1980.

**Procedural History**
- Trial court found Edward Leslie Gillmor's use of the common property constituted exclusive use and exclusion of Florence Gillmor, awarding Florence damages in two phases.
- Edward Leslie Gillmor appealed the second judgment to the Utah Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Can a cotenant recover damages for the exclusive use of common property by another cotenant if the cotenant out of possession made a clear demand to use the land and was refused accommodation?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Florence Gillmor) wins. A cotenant out of possession can establish a claim for relief when they make a clear demand to use land exclusively possessed by another cotenant, and that cotenant refuses to accommodate their right to use the land. Affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded for modification of the judgment.

**Rules**
- **Cotenant Use.** A cotenant has the right to use and occupy the entire property held in cotenancy without liability to other cotenants, as long as they do not interfere with the cotenant's right to likewise occupy, use, and enjoy.
- **Ouster.** A cotenant who ousts another cotenant or acts in such a fashion as to necessarily exclude a fellow cotenant violates the rights of that cotenant.
- **Exclusive Use for Ouster.** Exclusive use means more than one cotenant using the entire property; it requires either an act of exclusion or use of such a nature that it necessarily prevents another cotenant from exercising his rights in the property.
- **Establishing Ouster for Damages.** When a cotenant out of possession makes a clear, unequivocal demand to use land that is in the exclusive possession of another cotenant, and that cotenant refuses to accommodate the other tenant’s right to use the land, the tenant out of possession has established a claim for relief.
- **Contribution for Repairs (General Rule).** A cotenant who makes improvements or repairs to common property without the consent of fellow cotenants generally has no right of contribution and cannot charge them with the value or costs.
- **Contribution for Repairs (Exceptions).** Compensation for improvements is allowed where other cotenants stood by and permitted the cotenant to proceed to their detriment, or when the repairs were essential to preserve or protect the common estate, or when the cotenant acted in good faith with a bona fide belief of sole ownership.
- **Contribution for Repairs (Offset).** Where a cotenant in possession is held liable for the value of use, occupancy, or rents, they are entitled to recover reasonable expenditures made for necessary repairs and maintenance.

**Reasoning**
- The trial court's finding that Edward Leslie exercised exclusive use and possession and excluded Florence was sufficient to establish ouster, even without using the specific term "ouster."
- Edward Leslie's continued grazing to maximum capacity, coupled with his refusal to accommodate Florence's request to graze her own livestock, constituted an effective exclusion.
- If Florence had attempted to graze her livestock, the land would have been overgrazed, and Edward Leslie admitted he would have sought an injunction, demonstrating his intent to prevent her use.
- The court rejected Edward Leslie's argument that the damages were excessive because the first phase award was lower, noting the first phase recognized his contributions to maintaining the property, which was not at issue in the second phase.
- The court found that trail rights appurtenant to the Gillmor land should not be distinguished from the fee land for damage calculation.
- The court upheld the trial judge's decision to ignore Edward Leslie's field notes regarding grazing on non-Gillmor land, as it was the judge's prerogative to determine credibility and accept answers to interrogatories as more reliable.
- The court found that the repairs made by Edward Leslie were a necessary cost of grazing and should be deducted from the damages awarded to Florence.
    - This falls under the exception that a cotenant held liable for use is entitled to recover for necessary repairs.

**NOTES:**
1. This case clarifies the concept of **ouster** in the context of cotenancy, particularly when one cotenant seeks to recover for the exclusive use of common property by another. It distinguishes between mere exclusive use and an actual exclusion, emphasizing the importance of a clear demand and refusal to accommodate. This connects to the broader theme of **owner sovereignty** and the limits on the **right to use** property, even for a cotenant. It also touches on the **right to income** from property.
