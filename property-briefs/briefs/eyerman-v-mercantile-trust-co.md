## Eyerman v. Mercantile Trust Co. (Missouri Court of Appeals, 1975)

**Facts**
- Louise Woodruff Johnston owned a house at #4 Kingsbury Place in St. Louis.
- Johnston died on January 14, 1973.
- Johnston's will directed her executor to raze her home at #4 Kingsbury Place, sell the land, and transfer the proceeds to the residue of her estate.
- Kingsbury Place is a "private place" established in 1902 by a trust indenture, which requires it to be maintained as desirable private residences of the highest class.
- The subdivision consists of handsome, spacious homes, with one existing vacant lot.
- The St. Louis Commission on Landmarks and Urban Design designated Kingsbury Place as a landmark.
- Neighboring property owners and trustees for the Kingsbury Place Subdivision filed a petition seeking an injunction to prevent the demolition.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court dissolved a temporary restraining order and found all issues against the plaintiffs.
- Plaintiffs appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a testamentary direction to destroy a valuable house be enjoined as contrary to public policy when it causes significant loss to the estate, harms the community, and benefits no one?

**Holding**
- Plaintiffs (neighboring property owners and subdivision trustees) win. A testamentary direction to destroy a valuable house is void as against public policy when it causes significant loss to the estate, harms the community, and benefits no one. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Standing for Public Policy Issues.** Individuals have standing to raise public policy issues if they allege a legally protectible interest and will be perceptibly harmed by the challenged action.
- **Testamentary Freedom.** While living, a person may manage, use, or dispose of their property with fewer restraints than a decedent by will.
- **State Power over Inheritance.** The state has inherent sovereign power to determine what becomes of a person's property upon death and can impose conditions on the right to inherit or devise.
- **Public Policy.** Public policy conflicts with the morals of the time and contravenes an established interest of society, or has a mischievous tendency injurious to the interests of the state.
- **Judicial Determination of Public Policy.** In cases of first impression without guiding statutes, judicial decisions, or constitutional provisions, a judicial determination of public policy must be "so plainly right as to be supported by the general will."
- **Capricious Destruction by Will.** It is against public policy to allow an executor to exercise a power to destroy property if the purpose is merely capricious and confers no other interest in the property.
- **Rejected: Absolute Right to Untrammeled Use.** The general principle of an owner's "free and untrammeled" use of real property is narrowed by competing interests of the community and other owners.

**Reasoning**
- Plaintiffs have standing because they pleaded and proved facts showing a personal, legally protectible interest.
    - Demolition would result in an unwarranted loss to the estate ($39,350 loss from a $40,000 asset).
    - Demolition would depreciate adjoining property values by an estimated $10,000.
    - The house is in an area of high architectural significance, designated a landmark.
    - Demolition would create a "missing front tooth" effect, detracting from the urban design and potentially leading to detrimental uses.
    - There is a pressing need for dwelling units in St. Louis, as shown by census figures.
- The testamentary condition for destruction is capricious and serves no good purpose.
    - No reason for the eccentric condition is suggested by the will or record.
    - The destruction harms neighbors, detrimentally affects the community, causes monetary loss to the estate, and benefits no one.
- The state's power over inheritance allows it to impose conditions on testamentary dispositions.
    - While a living person has broad rights, a will's directions are subject to public policy.
    - Courts can intervene when a condition in a will is against the public good.
- Precedent supports invalidating capricious destructive conditions in wills.
    - English cases (Egerton v. Brownlow, M’Caig’s Trustees, Brown v. Burdett) and the Restatement (Second) of Trusts illustrate that directions for wasteful destruction are void.
    - Colonial Trust Co. v. Brown invalidated restrictions on property use that were against public policy and harmful to beneficiaries and public welfare.
- Enjoining the destruction will not defeat the testatrix's trust purposes, as the proceeds from the sale of the property will still pass to the residual estate.

**Dissent (Clemens)**
- The majority's opinion is emotional and assumes psychic knowledge of the testatrix's motives, characterizing her disposition as "capricious," "unwarranted," "senseless," and "eccentric" without evidence.
- The majority's decision confers a "benefit" on beneficiaries who have not litigated or protested.
- The public policy grounds are "wispy," vaguely pleaded, not in evidence, and sketchily briefed.
- The trust indenture does not bar razing, as it acknowledges vacant lots and empowers trustees to maintain them.
- The claim of nuisance is based on conjecture, not certainty, and there is no reason to presume a second vacant lot would be untended or that private police would cease patrolling.
- The court should not create its own public policy; public policy should be found in the Constitution, statutes, and judicial decisions, not the "varying opinions of laymen, lawyers, or judges."
- The law favors freedom in testamentary disposition and the free and untrammeled use of real property.
- Mrs. Johnston had the right to raze her house during her lifetime, and nothing precludes her right to order her executor to do so upon her death.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for illustrating the limits of **owner autonomy** and the **right to destroy** property, especially post-mortem. It connects to **Eyerman v. Mercantile Trust Co.** itself, as the case. It contrasts with cases like *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* where the court was hesitant to restrict a living person's control over their body parts, suggesting a distinction between living and dead owners. It also touches on the concept of **waste**, similar to how courts might intervene in cases of waste by a life tenant (though here it's a testamentary direction).
2. The dissent raises a valid point about the potential for judicial overreach in defining "public policy," especially when it's not clearly established by statute or prior case law. This raises the question of how much discretion courts should have to override a testator's wishes based on evolving societal values.

**Takeaway:** A testamentary direction to destroy valuable property can be voided as against public policy if it is capricious, causes significant economic and community harm, and benefits no one.
