## Eyerman v. Mercantile Trust Co. (Missouri Court of Appeals, 1975)

**Facts**
- Louise Woodruff Johnston owned a home at #4 Kingsbury Place in St. Louis.
- Johnston died on January 14, 1973.
- Johnston's will directed her executor to "cause our home at 4 Kingsbury Place . . . to be razed and to sell the land upon which it is located . . . and to transfer the proceeds of the sale . . . to the residue of my estate."
- Kingsbury Place is a "private place" established in 1902 by a trust indenture, intended to be maintained as desirable, high-class residential property.
- The trust indenture allows lot owners or trustees to sue to enforce its covenants.
- The subdivision consists of handsome, spacious homes, all used exclusively as private residences, except for one vacant lot.
- The current value of the house and land is $40,000.
- The estate would receive no more than $5,000 for the empty lot after demolition costs of $4,350.
- Demolition would result in a loss of $39,350 to the estate.
- Kingsbury Place has been designated a landmark by the St. Louis Commission on Landmarks and Urban Design.
- Demolition would depreciate adjoining property values by an estimated $10,000.
- The cost to build a comparable house would be $200,000.
- Demolition would create a gap, allowing direct access to Kingsbury Place from an alley, potentially leading to detrimental uses.
- U.S. Census Bureau figures show a decrease of over 14% in St. Louis City housing units during the 1960s.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs (neighboring property owners and Kingsbury Place subdivision trustees) sought an injunction to prevent the demolition.
- The trial court dissolved a temporary restraining order and found against the plaintiffs on all issues.
- Plaintiffs appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a will direct the demolition of a valuable home if doing so would harm the community, neighbors, and the estate, and serve no apparent beneficial purpose?

**Holding**
- Plaintiffs win. A testamentary direction to destroy valuable property is void as against public policy when it causes substantial harm to others and serves no benefit. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Standing.** Persons threatened with wrongful interference of property rights may seek an injunction against a threatened nuisance.
- **Standing (Public Policy).** Individuals have standing to raise public policy issues if they allege a legally protectible interest and will be perceptibly harmed by the challenged action, even if the interests are aesthetic or environmental and shared by the community.
- **Inheritance and Will.** The taking of property by inheritance or will is not an absolute or natural right but one created by the laws of the sovereign power. The state may grant this right upon conditions.
- **Public Policy (General).** Public policy conflicts with the morals of the time and contravenes an established interest of society; acts are against public policy when they have a mischievous tendency injurious to the state's interests.
- **Public Policy (Judicial Determination).** In cases of first impression without guiding statutes, judicial decisions, or constitutional provisions, a judicial determination of public policy must be "so plainly right as to be supported by the general will."
- **Public Policy (Testamentary Conditions).** A testator may not impose conditions in a will that are uncertain, unlawful, or opposed to public policy.
- **Capricious Destruction.** It is against public policy to allow an executor to exercise a power to destroy property if the purpose is merely capricious and confers no other interest in the property.

**Reasoning**
- Plaintiffs have standing because they pleaded and proved facts showing a personal, legally protectible interest.
    - Demolition would depreciate their property values and affect the aesthetic and historical qualities of their neighborhood.
    - The trust indenture empowers them to enforce covenants.
- The demolition would result in an unwarranted loss to the estate, plaintiffs, and the public.
    - The house and land are worth $40,000, but the empty lot would yield only $5,000, less $4,350 for demolition, resulting in a $39,350 loss.
    - Kingsbury Place is architecturally significant and a designated landmark.
    - Demolition would depreciate adjoining property values by $10,000.
    - The space created would permit direct access from an alley, increasing the likelihood of detrimental uses.
    - There is a pressing need for dwelling units in St. Louis, as shown by census data.
- No individual, group, or the community benefits from the destruction; only the "caprice of the dead testatrix is served."
    - The will provides no reason for the eccentric condition.
    - A living person has more freedom with their property, but a will's conditions are subject to judicial review.
- The state has the power to regulate inheritance and wills, as it is a right created by law, not an absolute right.
- Precedent supports invalidating capricious testamentary conditions.
    - *Egerton v. Brownlow* (1853) held that while an owner can leave land uncultivated, they cannot compel a successor to do so if it's against public good.
    - *M'Caig's Trustees* (1915) struck down a will directing the erection of statues with no public benefit, calling it an "absurd whim."
    - *In re Scott's Will* (1903) assumed a direction to destroy money was void.
    - Restatement, Second, Trusts § 124, states it's against public policy to allow a power of destruction if the purpose is merely capricious.
    - *Brown v. Burdett* (1882) invalidated a will directing a house to be boarded up for 20 years.
    - *Colonial Trust Co. v. Brown* (1926) invalidated restrictions on property development that were against the interests of beneficiaries and public welfare.
- The court does not reach the issues of private nuisance or restrictive covenants, though they "may have merit."
- The injunction will not defeat the testatrix's trust purposes, as the proceeds from the sale of the property will still go to the residual estate; only the destructive condition is enjoined.

**Dissent (Clemens)**
- The majority's opinion is based on "wispy, self-proclaimed public policy grounds" that were vaguely pleaded and not in evidence.
- The court should not assume knowledge of the testatrix's motives, which are unknown.
- The beneficiaries have not protested the razing, and the City is not a party.
- The trust indenture does not bar razing, as it acknowledges the existence of vacant lots and empowers trustees to maintain them.
- The nuisance claim is based on conjecture, not certainty, and there's no evidence a second vacant lot would be untended or cause nuisance.
- Public policy should be determined by the Constitution, statutes, and judicial decisions, not by judges' varying opinions or "spasmodic sentiment."
- The "law favors the free and untrammeled use of real property," and an owner has exclusive control subject only to not substantially impairing another's right to enjoy their property.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important because it limits the **dead hand control** of property, showing that a testator's wishes are not absolute, especially when they conflict with public policy. It connects to the idea that property rights are not solely individual but also have a social dimension. It contrasts with cases like *Mountain Brow Lodge No. 82 v. Toscano*, where conditions on land use were upheld if they were not absolute restraints on alienation, but here the condition is outright destruction. It also touches on the concept of waste, similar to how courts might view a life tenant's actions that diminish the value for remaindermen.
2. What if the will had stated a specific, non-capricious reason for demolition, such as a desire to prevent a specific person from living there, or to fund a specific charity with the higher proceeds from a vacant lot sale (if that were the case)? Would the public policy argument still prevail?

**Takeaway:** A testamentary instruction to destroy valuable property is void as against public policy if it causes substantial harm to the community and others without serving any beneficial purpose.
