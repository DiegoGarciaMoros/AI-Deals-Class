## Anderson v. Gouldberg (Minnesota Supreme Court, 1892)

**Facts**
- Sigfrid Anderson cut 93 pine logs from Section 22, which was owned by strangers, in the winter of 1889-1890.
- Anderson hauled the logs to a mill on Section 6.
- Hans J. Gouldberg and D. O. Anderson (defendants) took the logs from the mill.
- Defendants claimed the logs were cut on Section 26, belonging to the Ann River Logging Company, and that they took the logs under the Logging Company's authority.

**Procedural History**
- Anderson sued Gouldberg and Anderson in replevin to recover the logs or their value.
- The trial court instructed the jury that Anderson's title would be good against anyone except the real owner or someone with authority from the owner, even if Anderson obtained possession as a trespasser.
- The jury found for Anderson and assessed damages.
- The defendants appealed the denial of their motion for a new trial.

**Issue**
Can a party who obtained possession of property wrongfully maintain an action of replevin against a stranger who takes the property from them?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Anderson) wins. Bare possession of property, even if wrongfully obtained, is sufficient title to maintain replevin against a mere stranger to the property who takes it from the possessor. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Possession as Title.** Possession of property is good title against all the world except those having a better title.
- **Replevin and Lawful Possession.** To maintain replevin, the plaintiff's possession must be lawful as against the person who deprived them of it.
- **Rebutting Presumption of Title.** One who takes property from the possession of another can only rebut the presumption of title arising from possession by showing a superior title in themselves or by connecting themselves with one who has a superior title.
- **Rejected: Wrongful Possession Insufficient.** Rejected: A wrongful possession is not sufficient title to support an action of trover or replevin.

**Reasoning**
- The jury found that the defendants were strangers to the property, meaning they did not cut the logs from their own land.
- The court assumes Anderson obtained possession by trespassing on a third party's land.
- The principle that possession is good title against all but the true owner is well-settled, citing *Armory v. Delamirie*.
- Allowing a mere wrongdoer to take property from a possessor would lead to endless unlawful seizures and reprisals.
- The argument that possession only creates a rebuttable presumption of title is correct, but misapplied by the defendants; they must show a superior title in themselves or connect to one.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes the principle of relative title, where possession, even if wrongful against the true owner, creates a superior right against subsequent wrongdoers. It connects to *Armory v. Delamirie* by reinforcing the idea that a finder or possessor has rights against all but the true owner. It also contrasts with cases like *Johnson v. M'Intosh* where the source of title (discovery vs. native possession) was central.
2. What if the defendants had shown they were acting under the authority of the true owner of Section 22, not the Ann River Logging Company? Would Anderson still have prevailed?

**Takeaway:** Possession is a powerful indicator of ownership and creates a right against all but the true owner or those acting under the true owner's authority.
