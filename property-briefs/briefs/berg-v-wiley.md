## Berg v. Wiley (Minnesota Supreme Court, 1978)

**Facts**
- On November 11, 1970, Wiley (landlord) and a predecessor to Berg (tenant) executed a lease for a restaurant space in Osseo, Minnesota.
- The lease was for a 5-year term starting December 1, 1970, and required the tenant to bear repair costs, make no structural changes without written authorization, and operate the restaurant lawfully.
- The lease also allowed Wiley to retake possession if the tenant failed to meet lease conditions.
- In early 1971, Kathleen Berg took assignment of the lease and opened "A Family Affair Restaurant."
- In January 1973, Berg incorporated the restaurant and assigned her interest to the corporation, continuing to act as the sole shareholder.
- In June and July 1973, Wiley objected to Berg's remodeling without permission and alleged health code violations.
- On June 29, 1973, Wiley's attorney sent a letter to Berg, alleging breaches of the lease for unauthorized structural changes and an unclean kitchen, demanding completion of remodeling items by July 13, 1973, or Wiley would retake possession.
- A June 13, 1973, inspection by the Minnesota Department of Health also ordered changes to comply with health codes by July 15, 1973.
- Berg continued to operate the restaurant during the 2-week deadline.
- At the close of business on Friday, July 13, 1973, Berg dismissed employees, closed the restaurant, and placed a "Closed for Remodeling" sign in the window.
- Earlier on July 13, Berg testified that Wiley attempted to change the locks in her absence but left them unchanged when she returned and asserted her right to possession.
- Later on July 13, Berg observed Wiley peering into the restaurant and then pounding on the back door, leading her to call the sheriff.
- Wiley testified he saw Berg and friends removing paneling, called the police, and an agreement was mediated to preserve the status quo until Monday, July 16, 1973.
- On Monday, July 16, 1973, Wiley, accompanied by a police officer and locksmith, entered the premises in Berg's absence and without her knowledge, and changed the locks.
- Berg found herself locked out later that day.
- The lease was not due to expire until December 1, 1975.
- The premises were re-let to another tenant around August 1, 1973.

**Procedural History**
- Berg brought a damage action against Wiley and others on July 27, 1973.
- All defendants except Wiley and Wiley Enterprises, Inc. were dismissed at the close of Berg's case.
- The trial court found as a matter of law that Wiley locked the tenant out and that the lockout was wrongful.
- The jury found no liability on Berg's claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress and no liability on Wiley's counterclaim for damages to the premises.
- The jury awarded Berg $31,000 for lost profits and $3,540 for loss of chattels due to the wrongful lockout.
- The jury specifically found that Berg neither abandoned nor surrendered the premises.
- The trial court granted Wiley's post-trial motion for indemnification for mechanics lien liability by way of set-off from Berg's judgment.
- Wiley appealed the damages award for wrongful eviction.

**Issue**
Can a landlord use self-help to retake leased premises from a tenant who has not abandoned or surrendered the property, even if the landlord has a right to possession?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Berg) wins. A landlord may not use self-help to dispossess a tenant who has not abandoned or voluntarily surrendered the premises but claims possession adversely to the landlord's claim of breach of a written lease; judicial process is the exclusive lawful means. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Common Law Self-Help (Rejected).** A landlord may rightfully use self-help to retake leased premises from a tenant in possession without liability for wrongful eviction if the landlord is legally entitled to possession and the means of reentry are peaceable.
- **Rejected: Peaceable Reentry (Common Law).** Reentry by a landlord in the tenant's absence by picking locks and locking the tenant out, even without actual violence, is not peaceable under the common law.
- **Exclusive Judicial Remedy.** The only lawful means to dispossess a tenant who has not abandoned or voluntarily surrendered but who claims possession adversely to a landlord’s claim of breach of a written lease is by resort to judicial process.
- **Statutory Remedies.** Minnesota Statutes §§ 566.02 to 566.17 provide an adequate and speedy judicial remedy for landlords to regain possession.

**Reasoning**
- The jury's finding that Berg did not abandon or surrender the premises was supported by sufficient evidence, as she could reasonably have intended to retain possession and close temporarily for remodeling.
- The court's long-standing policy is to discourage landlords from taking the law into their own hands due to the potential for breaches of the peace.
- Minnesota statutory law provides a summary procedure for landlords to recover possession through judicial process (Minn.St. 566.02 to 566.17), which is intended to prevent self-help.
- Wiley's reentry was not peaceable under the common-law rule because Berg was in possession, claiming a right to continue, and Wiley retook possession in her absence by changing locks, despite a history of vigorous dispute and animosity.
- Approving such a lockout merely because no actual violence erupted in the tenant's absence would encourage tenants to be vigilant, setting the stage for public disturbances.
- The court adopts the modern view that self-help is never available to dispossess a tenant who is in possession and has not abandoned or voluntarily surrendered the premises.
    - This rule recognizes the inherent potential for violent breach of peace in such situations.
    - Adequate and speedy judicial means are available for removing a tenant peacefully.
- The rule is fairly applied to Wiley because, even under the older common-law rule, his lockout would be considered non-peaceable.
- Equity dictates that Wiley, who performed the act causing damage, must bear the loss.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important because it represents a significant shift from the traditional common law rule regarding landlord self-help, moving towards an exclusive judicial remedy for eviction. It connects to *Berg v. Wiley* itself, which is the case being briefed, and establishes a modern trend that contrasts with older approaches to property rights and possession. It also relates to the general theme of protecting possession and discouraging self-help, similar to the policy considerations in cases like *Ploof v. Putnam* (necessity as a defense to trespass) or *Williams v. Ford Motor Credit Co.* (limitations on self-help repossession of chattels).
2. The opinion mentions that the issue of whether Berg had actually breached the lease was not judicially determined. This raises the question of whether a landlord's right to possession, even if clear, can ever justify self-help under this new rule.

**Takeaway:** Landlords in Minnesota (and many other states following the modern trend) must always use judicial process to evict a tenant in possession, even if the tenant has breached the lease, to prevent wrongful eviction.
