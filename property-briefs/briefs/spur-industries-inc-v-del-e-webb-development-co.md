## Spur Industries Inc. v. Del E. Webb Development Co. (Arizona Supreme Court, 1972)

**Facts**
- Around 1911, farming began in the area west of Phoenix, Arizona.
- In 1954, the retirement community of Youngtown was started about 1.5 miles north of Olive Avenue.
- In 1956, Spur's predecessors developed cattle feedlots about 0.5 miles south of Olive Avenue.
- By 1959, there were 25 cattle feeding operations within a 7-mile radius of Spur's location.
- In May 1959, Del Webb began planning Sun City, a new urban development, and purchased 20,000 acres of farmland for this purpose.
- In September 1959, Del Webb started constructing a golf course, and Spur's predecessors began leveling ground for more feedlot area.
- In 1960, Spur purchased the feedlot property and expanded its operations.
- In January 1960, Del Webb began selling homes in Sun City, with the first unit completed about 2.5 miles north of Spur's feedlot.
- By May 1960, Del Webb had 450-500 houses completed or under construction.
- Del Webb continued developing Sun City southward, closer to the feedlot.
- By 1963, sales resistance due to feedlot odors became a serious problem for Del Webb in the southwestern portion of its development.
- By December 1967, Del Webb's property extended south to Olive Avenue, and Spur's feedlot was within 500 feet north of Olive Avenue.
- Del Webb filed a complaint alleging that over 1,300 lots were unfit for residential development due to Spur's feedlot operation.
- At the time of the suit, Spur was feeding 20,000 to 30,000 head of cattle.

**Procedural History**
- Del Webb sued Spur Industries, seeking to enjoin the feedlot operation as a public nuisance.
- The trial court found Spur's operation to be a nuisance and permanently enjoined it.
- Spur appealed the injunction.
- Del Webb cross-appealed.

**Issue**
1. Can a lawful cattle feedlot operation be enjoined as a nuisance when a nearby residential area develops around it?
2. If the nuisance can be enjoined, must the developer who created the residential area indemnify the feedlot operator for moving or ceasing operations?

**Holding**
- Del Webb (developer) wins in part, Spur (feedlot operator) wins in part.
- The feedlot operation can be enjoined as a public nuisance, but the developer who brought residents to the nuisance must indemnify the feedlot operator for the cost of moving or shutting down.
- Affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Public Nuisance.** A public nuisance affects the rights enjoyed by citizens as part of the public, impacting a considerable number of people or an entire community.
- **Private Nuisance.** A private nuisance affects a single individual or a small number of persons in the enjoyment of private rights.
- **Statutory Public Nuisance.** Conditions in populous areas that constitute breeding places for disease-carrying insects are specifically declared public nuisances dangerous to public health.
- **Locality Rule.** Whether a lawful occupation constitutes a nuisance depends significantly on the locality and surroundings; what is a nuisance in a populous area may be unobjectionable elsewhere.
- **Standing for Public Nuisance.** A private party can sue to enjoin a public nuisance if they can show special injury.
- **Equity Powers.** Courts of equity may go further to give or withhold relief in furtherance of the public interest than when only private interests are involved.
- **Coming to the Nuisance (Rejected as absolute bar).** A residential landowner may not get relief if they knowingly moved into an area reserved for industrial or agricultural endeavors and were damaged thereby. *However, this doctrine is not an absolute bar when public interest is involved.*
- **Balancing Equities.** The law of nuisance is elastic and requires what is fair and reasonable under all circumstances, especially when considering public interest and economic development.

**Reasoning**
- **Spur's operation is an enjoinable public nuisance:**
    - The feedlot produced massive amounts of manure, causing flies and odor that annoyed residents of Sun City.
    - This affected a considerable number of people, making it a public nuisance.
    - Arizona statute § 36-601(A)(1) declares conditions that breed disease-carrying insects in populous areas to be public nuisances.
    - The locality rule dictates that a business can become a nuisance if it affects a populous neighborhood.
    - Del Webb had standing to sue because it suffered special injury (loss of sales) due to the nuisance.
- **Del Webb must indemnify Spur:**
    - While the "coming to the nuisance" doctrine would normally bar relief for Webb if it were the only injured party, the public interest in protecting Sun City residents from the nuisance is paramount.
    - Spur's business was lawful and established in an agricultural area before Sun City developed.
    - Del Webb knowingly developed residential properties closer to the feedlot, taking advantage of lower land values in a rural area.
    - It would be inequitable to force Spur to bear the entire cost of moving when Del Webb created the situation that made the injunction necessary.
    - The court uses its equitable powers to balance the public's right to be free from nuisance with the fairness to a lawful business forced to relocate due to encroaching development.
    - This remedy is limited to cases where a developer foreseeably brings a population into a previously agricultural/industrial area, necessitating an injunction against a lawful business.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a classic example of a **nuisance** claim and introduces the concept of "coming to the nuisance." It shows how courts balance competing land uses and public welfare. It connects to **Jost v. Dairyland Power Cooperative** and **Campbell v. Seaman** by illustrating how lawful activities can become nuisances due to their impact on neighbors, but it adds a unique equitable remedy not seen in those cases. It also contrasts with **Boomer v. Atlantic Cement Co.** (cited in the opinion), where the court allowed damages instead of an injunction, whereas here an injunction is granted but with compensation.
2. This case raises the question of who should bear the cost of land-use conflicts when one party's lawful activity becomes problematic due to another's development. What if Spur had expanded its operations significantly *after* Sun City began developing nearby? Would the indemnification still be appropriate?

**Takeaway:** A lawful business can be enjoined as a public nuisance even if the plaintiff "came to the nuisance," but equity may require the plaintiff to indemnify the defendant if the plaintiff created the conditions that made the injunction necessary.
