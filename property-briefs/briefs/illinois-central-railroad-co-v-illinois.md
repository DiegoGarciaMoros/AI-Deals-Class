## Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois (Supreme Court of the United States, 1892)

**Facts**
- In 1818, Illinois was admitted to the Union on equal footing with the original states.
- In 1850, Congress granted Illinois a right of way and land to aid in the construction of a railroad from the Illinois and Michigan Canal to Cairo, with branches to Chicago and Dubuque.
- In 1851, the Illinois legislature accepted the Congressional grant and incorporated the Illinois Central Railroad Company (ICRR). ICRR's charter granted it a right of way up to 200 feet wide and the right to use state lands and waters for railroad purposes, but prohibited it from interrupting navigation of streams and from locating its road within any city without the city's consent.
- In 1852, the City of Chicago passed an ordinance granting ICRR permission to lay tracks along the lakefront, specifying the location and requiring ICRR to build a breakwater and other structures to protect the city from the lake. The ordinance also stated that ICRR could not occupy or use the "Lake Park" area except for construction or repair, nor obstruct the view of the lake. ICRR accepted this ordinance and entered into a contract with the city.
- ICRR constructed its tracks and breakwater as permitted, reclaiming land from the lake.
- In 1855 and 1856, Chicago passed additional ordinances granting ICRR permission to curve its tracks and use additional space for its station grounds.
- In 1869, the Illinois legislature passed the "Lake Front Act," which confirmed ICRR's existing rights and granted it in fee "all the right and title of the State of Illinois in and to the submerged lands constituting the bed of Lake Michigan, and lying east of the tracks and breakwater of the Illinois Central Railroad Company, for the distance of one mile." The act stipulated that ICRR could not sell the fee, and that the grant would not authorize obstructions to Chicago harbor or impair public navigation rights. ICRR accepted this act in 1870.
- In 1873, the Illinois legislature repealed the Lake Front Act.
- ICRR constructed several piers between 1872 and 1885.
- In 1883, the State of Illinois filed a lawsuit against ICRR and the City of Chicago to determine the rights to the reclaimed and submerged lands.

**Procedural History**
- The State of Illinois filed a bill in equity in Illinois Circuit Court against ICRR and the City of Chicago.
- ICRR removed the case to the Circuit Court of the United States for the Northern District of Illinois.
- The City of Chicago filed a cross-bill against the State and ICRR.
- The Circuit Court of the United States issued a decree.
- ICRR, the State, and the City each appealed the final decree to the Supreme Court of the United States.

**Issue**
Can the State of Illinois grant a private railroad corporation title to a large portion of the submerged lands under Lake Michigan in Chicago's harbor, thereby relinquishing its control over those lands?

**Holding**
The State (plaintiff) wins. The State cannot abdicate its trust over the submerged lands of Lake Michigan in Chicago's harbor, and the attempted grant to ICRR was invalid. Affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Public Trust Doctrine.** The ownership of and dominion and sovereignty over lands covered by navigable waters belong to the respective states, held in trust for the public to enjoy navigation, commerce, and fishing, free from private obstruction.
- **Navigable Waters.** The doctrine of state ownership and sovereignty over lands under navigable waters applies to the Great Lakes, just as it does to tide waters.
- **State Legislative Power over Public Trust.** The State can grant parcels of submerged lands for the improvement of public interests (e.g., wharves, docks, piers) or when such disposition does not substantially impair the public interest in the remaining lands and waters.
- **Abdication of Public Trust.** A state cannot abdicate its general control over lands under the navigable waters of an entire harbor or bay, or of a sea or lake, as this is inconsistent with its trust to preserve such waters for public use.
- **Revocable Grants.** Any grant by the State that attempts to abdicate its public trust over navigable waters is necessarily revocable, and the State can resume the exercise of that trust at any time.
- **Riparian Rights.** Riparian rights are incident to riparian ownership and attach to land contiguous to navigable water, allowing the owner to access the navigable part of the water and construct wharves or piers for their own or public use, subject to legislative regulation and terminating at the point of navigability.

**Reasoning**
- The State of Illinois holds title to the submerged lands under Lake Michigan in trust for the public, similar to how states hold title to tidelands under common law.
- This public trust requires the State to preserve these waters for public navigation, commerce, and fishing.
- While the State can grant small parcels of submerged lands for improvements that aid navigation and commerce, or when such grants do not substantially impair the public interest, it cannot abdicate its general control over an entire harbor.
- The 1869 Lake Front Act, which granted ICRR over a thousand acres of submerged lands, effectively transferred control of nearly the entire harbor to a private corporation. This is an abdication of the State's public trust.
- Such an extensive grant to a private corporation, especially one created for a different purpose (railroad transportation), is inconsistent with the State's governmental trust and cannot be defended.
- The grant was not for a limited period or for specific improvements that would benefit the public interest without substantial impairment of the remaining public domain.
- The limitations in the 1869 act (no obstructions, no impairment of navigation, regulation of wharfage rates) did not prevent the abdication of control, as ICRR could still indefinitely delay improvements or construct works for its own profit.
- The 1873 repealing act was valid and effective because the 1869 act, to the extent it attempted to abdicate the State's sovereignty and dominion over the submerged lands, was inoperative. There can be no irrepealable contract in a conveyance of property by a grantor in disregard of a public trust.
- ICRR's existing structures (tracks, breakwater) built under the 1852 ordinance are permissible because they were constructed under legal authority and do not interfere with useful freedom in the use of the lake for commerce.
- ICRR's riparian rights, derived from its ownership of certain lakefront lots, allow it to construct piers and docks up to the point of navigability. The lower court must determine if ICRR's piers extend beyond this point.
- The City of Chicago, as riparian owner of public grounds on the lakefront, has the power to construct wharves, docks, and levees, subject to state and federal authority.

**Dissent (Mr. Justice Shiras, Mr. Justice Gray, Mr. Justice Brown)**
- The dissent argues that a State's ownership of lands under navigable waters is as complete as its ownership of other public lands, and it has the full power to convey such lands.
- The validity of a grant should not depend on the size of the parcel, and the grant to ICRR, though large, was not unreasonable given the railroad's need for terminal facilities.
- The extent of such a grant and its effect on public interests are matters of legislative discretion.
- The 1869 act constituted a valid contract, and the State cannot arbitrarily revoke it without infringing the Contract Clause of the U.S. Constitution.
- The limitations in the grant (no obstruction to harbor, no impairment of navigation) are incidents of the grant and do not defeat it.
- If the State wishes to regain control, it must do so through constitutional condemnation and compensation, not by simply repealing the grant.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a foundational case for the **Public Trust Doctrine** in American property law. It establishes that states hold title to navigable waters and the lands beneath them in trust for the public, and this trust cannot be entirely alienated to private parties. This connects to cases like *Ghen v. Rich* and *United States v. Corrow* in discussing public rights and common resources, but here the focus is on the state's role as trustee. It also touches on the concept of **riparian rights**, which we saw in *Evans v. Merriweather* and *Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.*, but here in the context of a large commercial entity and a public trust.
2. The dissent raises the question of whether the majority's ruling on the revocability of the grant, even if it is an abdication of public trust, conflicts with the Contract Clause of the U.S. Constitution. This tension between state sovereignty/public trust and the protection of contracts is a key point for discussion.

**Takeaway:** States hold navigable waters and their submerged lands in an inalienable public trust, meaning they cannot grant away control of an entire harbor to a private entity.
