## Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co. (Colorado Supreme Court, 1882)

**Facts**
- Appellee (Left Hand Ditch Co.) appropriated water from the south fork of the St. Vrain creek and diverted it via a ditch to James creek, then to Left Hand creek, where it was used to irrigate lands.
- Appellants (Coffin et al.) owned lands along the St. Vrain creek below the south fork, which were naturally irrigated by the St. Vrain.
- In 1879, there was not enough water in the St. Vrain to supply both the appellee's ditch and irrigate the appellants' lands.
- Appellants tore out a portion of appellee's dam, interfering with its water diversion.

**Procedural History**
- Appellee sued appellants for damages from trespass and for injunctive relief.
- Appellants raised several defenses, including a prior agreement and riparian rights.
- The trial court sustained demurrers to all defenses except a general denial.
- A jury trial resulted in a verdict and judgment for appellee for damages to the dam.
- Appellants appealed.

**Issue**
- Does the common law doctrine of riparian rights apply in Colorado, or does the doctrine of prior appropriation govern water rights?

**Holding**
- Appellee wins. The doctrine of prior appropriation, not riparian rights, governs water rights in Colorado, and the location of water use does not affect the right. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Prior Appropriation Doctrine.** The first appropriator of water from a natural stream for a beneficial purpose has a prior right to that water, to the extent of the appropriation.
- **Riparian Rights (Rejected).** The common law doctrine giving riparian owners a right to the flow of water in its natural channel, even without beneficial use, is inapplicable in Colorado.
- **Locus of Use.** The right to water acquired by prior appropriation is not dependent on the location where the water is applied to beneficial use.
- **Protection of Water Rights.** The right to water by priority of appropriation is protected even after the land over which the stream flows is patented to a third party, regardless of whether it's mentioned in the patent.

**Reasoning**
- Colorado's dry climate makes artificial irrigation an absolute necessity for agriculture.
    - Water, when appropriated, becomes a distinct usufructuary estate, not merely an incident to the soil.
    - Denying prior appropriation would destroy the value of vast property developed with the understanding that such rights would be protected.
- The doctrine of prior appropriation existed in Colorado from the earliest appropriations, predating specific legislation and the constitution.
    - Congressional acts protecting appropriated water rights recognized pre-existing local customs and laws, rather than creating new rights.
- The common law doctrine of riparian proprietorship is unsuitable for Colorado due to imperative necessity.
- The territorial legislature recognized the doctrine of prior appropriation in statutes, even if not explicitly stated in the earliest ones.
    - Statutes referring to "legal rights" and "priority of right" imply recognition of prior appropriation.
- The right of prior appropriation is not dependent on the location of the beneficial use.
    - It would be inequitable to deprive someone of water simply because they transported it over a watershed to cultivate land in another valley.
    - Topography, distance, expense, and water quantity might make it impossible or impracticable to use water from an adjacent stream.
    - Applying a location-based rule would prevent productive cultivation and waste water on sterile lands.
- Statutory provisions regarding water use "along the line of said stream" or "on the bank, margin or neighborhood" refer to existing uses and detriments at the time of diversion, not future potential uses.
    - "Detriment" only exists if water was previously appropriated or used.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is foundational for understanding the **prior appropriation doctrine** in water law, which contrasts sharply with the **riparian rights** doctrine. It establishes that in arid regions, the first to divert and use water beneficially gains a superior right, regardless of land ownership along the stream. This is a significant departure from common law principles, emphasizing the practical needs of a specific environment over traditional property concepts. It connects to cases like *Evans v. Merriweather* which discusses reasonable use under riparian rights, showing a different approach to water allocation.
2. What if a prior appropriator's use becomes wasteful over time, or a new, more efficient use emerges? Does the "beneficial purpose" requirement evolve, and if so, how is that determined without undermining the certainty of prior rights?

**Takeaway:** In arid regions like Colorado, the necessity of irrigation led to the adoption of the prior appropriation doctrine, granting superior water rights to the first beneficial user, irrespective of land ownership along the stream or the location of water use.
