## Ploof v. Putnam (Vermont Supreme Court, 1908)

**Facts**
- On November 13, 1904, Ploof was sailing his loaded sloop on Lake Champlain with his wife and two minor children.
- A sudden and violent tempest arose, endangering the sloop and its occupants.
- To save them, Ploof moored his sloop to a dock owned by Putnam, which was in charge of Putnam's servant.
- Putnam, through his servant, unmoored Ploof's sloop.
- The sloop was driven ashore by the tempest, destroying the sloop and its contents, and injuring Ploof, his wife, and children.

**Procedural History**
- Ploof sued Putnam for damages in trespass and case.
- Putnam demurred to each count of the declaration.
- The trial court (Chittenden County, Haselton, J.) overruled the demurrers and adjudged each count sufficient.
- Putnam excepted and appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a person moor their vessel to another's dock during a sudden tempest to save lives and property without being liable for trespass, and can the dock owner be held liable for unmooring it?

**Holding**
- Ploof (plaintiff) wins. A person is justified by necessity in mooring to another's dock to save lives and property during a tempest, and the dock owner is liable for damages caused by unmooring the vessel. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Necessity Doctrine.** Necessity can justify actions that would otherwise be trespasses, especially to preserve human life or avoid bodily harm.
- **Trespass Justification.** An entry upon land or interference with personal property may be justified by necessity when movements are started without fault and in the proper exercise of a strict right.
- **Scope of Employment.** An allegation that a defendant acted "by his servant" implies the servant was acting within the scope of employment, even if the act was described as "wilfully and designedly" or "negligently, carelessly and wrongfully."

**Reasoning**
- The court reviews numerous cases establishing that necessity justifies actions that would otherwise be trespasses.
    - Examples include chasing trespassing sheep off land, cattle escaping while being driven to pound, travelers passing onto adjoining land due to highway obstruction, and entering land to save goods from water or fire.
    - The doctrine applies with special force to the preservation of human life, such as running through another's property to escape an assailant or sacrificing personal property to save lives (Mouse's Case).
- The declaration sufficiently alleges necessity because it states that the tempest compelled Ploof to moor to Putnam's dock to save his sloop and the people in it.
    - The details of the situation creating the necessity are matters of proof, not pleading.
    - The plaintiff does not need to explicitly negate the existence of other safe mooring options in the pleading.
- The declaration sufficiently alleges that the servant acted within the scope of employment.
    - The words "wilfully and designedly" or "negligently, carelessly and wrongfully" are applied to the defendant acting through the servant, not to the servant acting independently.
    - This implies the servant was acting under the defendant's authority.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes the **necessity doctrine** as a defense to trespass, emphasizing that property rights are not absolute and can be limited by the need to preserve life and property. It contrasts with cases like *Pile v. Pedrick* or *Edwards v. Allouez Mining Co.* where strict property rights were upheld even when it caused hardship to the trespasser, by showing that certain circumstances (like imminent danger) can override those rights.
2. What if the dock was damaged by Ploof's sloop during the tempest? Would Ploof be liable for those damages, even if his mooring was justified by necessity?

**Takeaway:** The right to use another's property without permission can be justified by necessity, especially when human life is at stake.
