## Skendzel v. Marshall (Supreme Court of Indiana, 1973)

**Facts**
- In December 1958, Mary Burkowski (vendor) entered into a land sale contract with Charles P. and Agnes P. Marshall (vendees) for $36,000.
- The contract required an initial payment of $500, another $500 by December 25, 1958, and $2,500 or more by January 15 of each year thereafter until paid.
- The contract included a prepayment clause allowing prepayments to be applied to future principal payments.
- The contract contained a forfeiture clause stating that if any default continued for 30 days, all prior payments would be forfeited and retained by the vendor as liquidated damages, and the contract would terminate.
- The vendor, Mary Burkowski, died in 1963.
- The plaintiffs (Skendzel, Legowski, Wysocki) are assignees of Burkowski's interests in the contract, receiving their assignment on June 27, 1968.
- The Marshalls made payments totaling $21,000 by February 15, 1965.
- No payments were made after February 15, 1965, leaving $15,000 unpaid on the original contract price.
- One year after receiving their assignment, the plaintiffs filed suit alleging default and seeking to enforce the forfeiture provision.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs (vendor-assignees) sued to enforce a forfeiture clause in a land sale contract.
- The trial court issued a negative judgment against the plaintiffs.
- The Court of Appeals reversed, finding the defendants (vendees) had breached and the plaintiffs had not waived their right to enforce forfeiture.
- Defendants (vendees) petitioned for transfer to the Supreme Court of Indiana.
- The Supreme Court of Indiana granted transfer, reversed the Court of Appeals, and remanded with instructions.

**Issue**
Can a vendor enforce a forfeiture clause in a land sale contract, allowing them to retain all prior payments as liquidated damages and regain possession, when the vendee has paid a substantial portion of the purchase price?

**Holding**
The vendees (Marshalls) win. A vendor cannot enforce a forfeiture clause in a land sale contract when the vendee has paid a substantial amount, as such a forfeiture would be an unconscionable penalty; instead, the vendor must pursue a judicial foreclosure. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Equity abhors forfeitures.** Equity has jurisdiction to grant relief against the enforcement of forfeitures when the loss or injury occasioned by the default can be exactly compensated.
- **Equitable interference with penalties and forfeitures.** Equity will relieve against a penalty or forfeiture if it is merely used to secure payment of a debt or performance of an act, and compensation can be adequately made for the breach.
- **Liquidated damages vs. penalty.** "Reasonable" liquidated damage provisions are permitted, but if damages are unreasonable or disproportionate to the actual loss, they are characterized as penal.
- **Land sale contract as a secured transaction.** A conditional land sale contract is in the nature of a secured transaction, creating a vendor's lien on the property to secure the unpaid balance, analogous to a mortgage.
- **Foreclosure of vendor's lien.** A vendor's lien under a land sale contract should be enforced through judicial foreclosure proceedings, similar to a mortgage, including redemption rights.
- **Rejection of strict foreclosure.** American jurisdictions have largely rejected strict foreclosure in favor of foreclosure by judicial sale, which is more equitable.
- **Appropriate circumstances for forfeiture.** Forfeiture may be appropriate for an abandoning or absconding vendee, or where the vendee has paid a minimal amount and the vendor is bearing the costs of upkeep.

**Reasoning**
- The forfeiture of $21,000 (well over half the original contract price) is clearly excessive and inconsistent with principles of fairness and equity.
- The vendees acquired a substantial equitable interest in the property, and forfeiture would result in substantial injustice.
- Equity disfavors forfeitures, especially when the amount forfeited is disproportionate to the actual damages suffered by the vendor.
- The court views a conditional land contract as a sale with a security interest, where the vendor's retention of legal title is functionally equivalent to reserving a lien or mortgage.
- To treat a land contract differently from a mortgage would be to prioritize form over substance.
- Judicial foreclosure, which includes a period of redemption, is a more equitable remedy than forfeiture, allowing the vendee to protect their equity.
- Trial Rule 69(C) and the mortgage foreclosure statute apply to "all liens upon real estate," and the vendor's interest in a land contract constitutes such a lien.
- The trial court correctly refused to enforce the forfeiture but erred by denying all relief; it should have ordered a judicial foreclosure.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence (Prentice, J.)**
- The opinion might be seen as indifferent to contract vendors' rights, which is not the intent.
- Vendees seeking to avoid forfeiture should clearly show the inequity of enforcement.
- If the vendee has little or no equity, or has abandoned the premises, forfeiture should be allowed.
- If forfeiture is deemed unjust, the court should grant the vendor maximum equitable relief, treating the transaction as a note and mortgage.
- This would include provisions for increased interest during default, acceleration clauses, attorney's fees, and waiver of valuation and appraisement laws, which are typically found in mortgages but often omitted from land contracts.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is crucial for understanding how courts use equitable principles to limit the enforcement of contractual terms, particularly forfeiture clauses in land sale contracts. It connects to the concept of **equity** and the court's role in preventing unconscionable outcomes, similar to how courts might intervene in cases of **waste** or **restraints on alienation**. It also highlights the functional equivalence of certain property interests, comparing a land contract to a mortgage, which is a common theme in property law (e.g., the "sticks in the bundle" metaphor).
2. What if the vendee had only paid $1,000 on a $100,000 contract and then defaulted? Would the court still require foreclosure, or would forfeiture be deemed appropriate under the "minimal amount" exception mentioned in the reasoning?

**Takeaway:** Courts will generally treat a land sale contract as a mortgage, requiring judicial foreclosure rather than forfeiture, to protect a vendee's substantial equitable interest and prevent unconscionable results.
