## Charles River Bridge v. Warren Bridge (Supreme Court of the United States, 1837)

**Facts**
- In 1650, the Massachusetts legislature granted Harvard College the right to operate a ferry between Charlestown and Boston.
- Harvard College operated this ferry through lessees and received profits until 1785.
- In 1785, the Massachusetts legislature incorporated the Proprietors of the Charles River Bridge to build a bridge where the ferry was located.
- The Charles River Bridge company was granted tolls for 40 years (later extended to 70 years) and was required to pay Harvard College 200 pounds annually.
- The Charles River Bridge was built and operated, with the company paying Harvard College and maintaining the bridge.
- In 1828, the Massachusetts legislature incorporated the Proprietors of the Warren Bridge to build another bridge over the Charles River, very close to the Charles River Bridge.
- The Warren Bridge was allowed to take tolls until its construction costs were reimbursed, after which it would become free.
- The Warren Bridge was built and eventually became free, diverting traffic and tolls from the Charles River Bridge.

**Procedural History**
- The Proprietors of the Charles River Bridge filed a bill in the Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts against the Proprietors of the Warren Bridge, seeking an injunction to prevent the erection of the bridge and for general relief.
- The Charles River Bridge company argued that the act authorizing the Warren Bridge impaired the obligation of a contract, violating the U.S. Constitution.
- The Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts dismissed the bill.
- The Charles River Bridge company appealed to the Supreme Court of the United States by writ of error.
- The Supreme Court of the United States affirmed the judgment of the Massachusetts court.

**Issue**
Can a state legislature authorize the construction of a new public work that competes with and diminishes the value of an existing chartered public work, without violating the Contract Clause of the U.S. Constitution, when the original charter does not explicitly grant an exclusive privilege?

**Holding**
Defendant (Warren Bridge) wins. A state legislature can authorize the construction of a new public work that competes with an existing chartered public work, even if it diminishes the value of the existing work, if the original charter does not explicitly grant an exclusive privilege. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Strict Construction of Public Grants.** Public grants, especially those to private corporations, are to be construed strictly against the grantee and in favor of the public. Nothing passes by implication in such grants.
- **Contract Clause.** A state law may be retrospective and divest vested rights without violating the U.S. Constitution, unless it also impairs the obligation of a contract.
- **State Sovereignty.** The government's power to promote the happiness and prosperity of the community, including providing necessary channels of communication, should not be presumed to be surrendered or diminished by implication.
- Rejected: **Implied Exclusive Privilege.** An exclusive privilege or a covenant not to authorize competition will not be implied in a public grant unless explicitly stated.

**Reasoning**
- The Court must interpret the charter of the Charles River Bridge strictly, as it is a public grant to a private corporation concerning public interest.
- Nothing in the Charles River Bridge charter explicitly granted an exclusive privilege over the Charles River or prohibited the state from authorizing another bridge.
- To imply such an exclusive right would diminish the state's power to provide for the public good and would be contrary to the public interest in a growing and enterprising country.
- The object of government is to promote community happiness and prosperity, which often requires new infrastructure and improvements.
- If implied contracts were recognized in such cases, it would jeopardize investments in new technologies (like railroads) and hinder progress.
- The ferry right of Harvard College was extinguished by the building of the Charles River Bridge, with compensation provided, and the bridge charter is a separate grant.
- The argument that the state cannot diminish the value of a granted franchise without impairing a contract is rejected; the power of the state, like the taxing power, is not presumed to be abandoned.
- The Court's role is to interpret the contract as written, not to create implied terms that were not the deliberate purpose of the state.

**Dissent (Story, J.)**
- The grant of a franchise, like a ferry or bridge, inherently includes an exclusive right to prevent ruinous competition within a reasonable distance, as established by common law.
- The state's grant to the Charles River Bridge company was a contract, and the common law understanding of such grants at the time implied an exclusive right to protect the franchise's value.
- The act incorporating the Warren Bridge effectively destroyed the value of the Charles River Bridge franchise, thus impairing the obligation of the contract.
- The state should not be allowed to take private property (the franchise) for public use without compensation, even if it is for public convenience.
- The strict construction rule should not be applied to defeat the clear intent and beneficial interest conveyed by a public grant, especially when there was a valuable consideration.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is crucial for understanding the **Contract Clause** and the **strict construction of public grants**. It contrasts with cases like *Fletcher v. Peck* and *Dartmouth College v. Woodward*, where the Court protected contracts against state impairment, by emphasizing the public interest and limiting implied rights in corporate charters. It also touches on the concept of **eminent domain** and the need for compensation, though the majority found no contract impairment here.
2. The tension between protecting private property rights (especially those derived from state charters) and allowing states to promote public welfare and economic development is a key theme. How might the outcome have differed if the Charles River Bridge charter had explicitly included an exclusivity clause?

**Takeaway:** Public grants are to be construed strictly against the grantee, and exclusive privileges will not be implied, prioritizing the state's power to promote public welfare over the potential for private monopolies.
