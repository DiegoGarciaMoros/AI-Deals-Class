## Mountain Brow Lodge No. 82 v. Toscano (Court of Appeal of the State of California, 1967)

**Facts**
- On April 6, 1950, James V. Toscano and Maria Toscano (grantors) conveyed a parcel of real property by gift deed to Mountain Brow Lodge No. 82 (appellant), a nonprofit corporation.
- The deed's habendum clause stated: "Said property is restricted for the use and benefit of the second party, only; and in the event the same fails to be used by the second party or in the event of sale or transfer by the second party of all or any part of said lot, the same is to revert to the first parties herein, their successors, heirs or assigns.”
- The grantors are now deceased.
- Appellant sought to quiet its title to the property against the estates of the deceased grantors.
- The trial court rendered judgment in favor of the respondents (trustees and administrators of the estates).
- The parties stipulated that the only question on appeal is whether the conditions in the deed are void.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court rendered judgment in favor of respondents, refusing to quiet appellant's title.
- Appellant appealed.
- The Court of Appeal affirmed the judgment as modified.

**Issue**
Can a deed validly create a fee simple subject to a condition subsequent that restricts the use of the property to the grantee, even if it effectively limits who can use the land?

**Holding**
Respondent (the estates of the grantors) wins. The condition restricting the use of the property to the grantee for its organizational purposes created a valid fee simple subject to a condition subsequent, and this condition is severable from the void condition against alienation. Affirmed as modified.

**Rules**
- **Restraints on Alienation.** Conditions restraining alienation, when repugnant to the interest created, are void. (California Civil Code section 711)
- **Severability of Conditions.** If a deed contains multiple conditions, an invalid condition does not necessarily affect or nullify a valid, severable condition.
- **Fee Simple Subject to Condition Subsequent.** No formal language is necessary to create a fee simple subject to a condition subsequent as long as the grantor's intent is clear from the words used and surrounding circumstances.
- **Construction of Deeds.** The object in construing a deed is to ascertain the intention of the grantor from the words employed and from surrounding circumstances.
- **Rejected: Restraint on Alienation (Use Restriction).** A condition restricting land use to the grantee's organizational purposes is not an absolute restraint on alienation, even if it limits who can use the land.

**Reasoning**
- The condition prohibiting sale or transfer is an absolute restraint against alienation and is void under Civil Code section 711.
- However, the condition against alienation and the condition relating to land use are in the disjunctive ("or") and are clearly severable.
- The invalid restraint on alienation does not affect the validity of the land use condition.
- The term "use" in the deed, when applied to real property and considering appellant's nature as a nonprofit lodge, fraternal, and similar organization, means the land was conveyed on condition that it would be used for those organizational purposes.
- This interpretation is supported by the fact that the conveyance was a gift deed in consideration of "love and affection" and one grantor was an active member of the lodge.
- Conditions restricting land use have been consistently upheld in California, even if they hamper alienation.
- Restricting use to a specific entity (like a city for city purposes, a school district for school purposes, or a church for church purposes) is a valid use restriction, not an invalid restraint on alienation.
- Other jurisdictions also recognize that conditions limiting use to a specific organization create defeasible estates.
- In doubtful cases, the preferred construction is in favor of an estate subject to a condition subsequent over a fee simple determinable. *(dicta)*
- The rule against perpetuities does not apply to defeasible estates because reversions, possibilities of reverter, and powers of termination are inherently vested. *(dicta)*

**Dissent (Stone, J.)**
- The entire habendum clause is an invalid restraint upon alienation.
- Even if the "sale or transfer" language is removed, the use restriction ("used by only the I.O.O.F. Lodge No. 82") prevents the grantee from conveying the property just as effectively as an explicit prohibition on sale.
- The effect of language should be judged by what it does; if two different terms generate the same legal result, they should be treated alike.
- The policy against restraints on alienation is important, as organizations may cease to exist, leading to fragmented titles among numerous heirs.
- The majority's cited cases involve restrictions on *how* property may be used, not *who* may use it. A restriction on the kind of use does not restrain alienation because the property can still be conveyed, subject to the restriction.
- This restriction accomplishes no reasonable purpose that would justify an indefinite suspension of alienation.
- There is a correlation between a restriction on who may use property and the exclusion of racial groups, which is prohibited by Civil Code section 782. Allowing restrictions like this could open the door to "inverse exclusionary clauses" to violate section 782.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the distinction between a **restraint on alienation** and a **fee simple subject to a condition subsequent** (or fee simple determinable). It shows that a condition limiting the *use* of property to a specific grantee for its organizational purposes is generally considered valid, even if it indirectly limits who can possess or benefit from the land. This contrasts with cases like *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* where the court grappled with the alienability of property interests in human cells. The dissent's argument about the practical effect of the use restriction on alienability is a key point of tension.
2. The dissent raises a compelling point about the practical effect of the use restriction being the same as a direct restraint on alienation. Is the court's distinction between "who uses it" and "how it is used" truly meaningful in terms of marketability? What if the Lodge ceases to exist or moves?

**Takeaway:** A condition in a deed restricting property use to the grantee's specific organizational purposes creates a valid defeasible estate, even if it effectively limits who can use the land, and is severable from an invalid direct restraint on alienation.
