## Allen v. Hyatt Regency-Nashville Hotel (Tennessee Supreme Court, 1984)

**Facts**
- Hyatt Regency-Nashville Hotel operated a multi-story parking garage in Nashville, open to the public.
- On February 12, 1981, Edwin Allen, husband of the plaintiff Betty J. Allen, drove her new 1981 automobile into the garage.
- Mr. Allen took a ticket from an automatic dispenser, drove to the fourth floor, parked, locked the car, and kept the ignition key.
- He left the garage and returned several hours later to find the car gone.
- Mr. Allen reported the theft to the exit attendant, who stated the car "didn't come out here."
- He then reported the theft to hotel security personnel.
- A security guard told Mr. Allen that a lady had called earlier that day reporting "someone messing with a car" in the garage.
- The security guard investigated but found nothing unusual.
- The car was never recovered.
- The tickets dispensed stated that charges were for parking space only, the hotel assumed no responsibility for loss, cars were parked at the owner's risk, and parkers should lock their vehicles.
- The tickets were used to measure parking time for proper charge collection, not to identify specific vehicles.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court found a bailment was created.
- The intermediate appellate court affirmed the trial court's finding of a bailment.
- Hyatt Regency-Nashville Hotel appealed.

**Issue**
- Is a commercial parking garage operator liable for the theft of a vehicle when the owner parks and locks the car, keeps the keys, and receives a ticket from an automatic dispenser, thereby creating a bailment?

**Holding**
- Plaintiff (appellee) wins. A bailment for hire was created, and the garage operator is liable for the theft. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Bailment for Hire.** A bailment for hire is created when there is a delivery of personal property by one person to another for a specific purpose, with an express or implied contract that the property will be returned or accounted for.
- **Implied Bailment.** An implied bailment can be created by the conduct of the parties and the surrounding circumstances, particularly when there is sufficient delivery of possession and control to the alleged bailee.
- **Presumption of Negligence (T.C.A. § 24-5-111).** Upon proof of nondelivery of bailed property, a statutory presumption of negligence arises against the bailee.
- Rejected: **No Bailment Without Full Transfer of Possession and Control.** The dissent argues that a bailment requires a full transfer of possession and control to the bailee, excluding the owner and all others, and giving the bailee sole custody and control.

**Reasoning**
- The court found that the facts of this case were consistent with the legal requirements for a bailment for hire.
    - The garage was an enclosed, indoor, attended commercial garage with an attendant controlling the exit and regular security personnel patrolling.
    - The operator assumed control and custody of the vehicles parked by limiting access and requiring a ticket for exit.
    - The attendant's statement that the car "didn't come out here" implied that the attendant was either in error, inattentive, or away from their station, as there was no other exit.
- The court relied on prior Tennessee decisions, particularly *Scruggs v. Dennis*, which found an implied bailment in a similar park-and-lock situation where a ticket was dispensed by a machine and the owner retained keys.
- The court acknowledged the question of sufficient delivery when the owner locks the vehicle and keeps the keys but emphasized the "realities of the situation" where the operator is expected to provide protection.
- The hotel made no effort to rebut the statutory presumption of negligence that arose from the nondelivery of the vehicle.
- The court rejected the idea of placing the risk of loss on the consuming public against commercial parking operators in such circumstances.
- The language on the ticket disclaiming responsibility was not sufficient to exonerate the hotel because it was not shown that the customer read it or had it called to his attention. *(dicta)*

**Dissent (DROWOTA, J.)**
- The dissent argues that no bailment existed because there was no sufficient delivery of possession and control from the owner to the garage operator.
- The dissent emphasizes that the owner parked and locked the car, retained the keys, and the ticket did not identify the vehicle.
- The cashier's role was merely to collect payment, not to identify and return a specific article, and the garage could not move the car or know to whom it belonged.
- The dissent believes the majority's application of bailment law to modern automated parking facilities, particularly in cases like *Scruggs*, stretches the traditional elements of delivery, custody, and control too far.
- The dissent points out that the garage was sometimes entirely open without a cashier, further undermining the idea of the operator having sole custody and control.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the concept of **bailment** in property law, particularly how it applies to modern commercial services like parking garages. It connects to the idea of possession and control as key elements in establishing property interests. The majority's reasoning highlights how courts can adapt traditional legal concepts to new factual scenarios, while the dissent emphasizes strict adherence to traditional definitions. This case can be compared with *Williams v. Ford Motor Credit Co.* regarding the importance of possession and control in different contexts.
2. The case raises the question of how much control is "sufficient" to establish a bailment, especially when the owner retains keys and locks the property. What if the garage had no attendant at all, or if the attendant was only present for a few hours a day?

**Takeaway:** A commercial parking garage can be found to have created an implied bailment for hire, making it liable for vehicle theft, even when the owner parks, locks the car, and retains the keys, if the garage exercises sufficient control over access and exit.
