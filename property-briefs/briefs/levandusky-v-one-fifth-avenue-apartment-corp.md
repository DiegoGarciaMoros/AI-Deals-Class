## Levandusky v. One Fifth Avenue Apartment Corp. (New York Court of Appeals, 1990)

**Facts**
- In 1987, Ronald Levandusky (respondent) decided to enlarge his kitchen and "jog" a steam riser in his apartment at One Fifth Avenue.
- Levandusky, then president of the cooperative's board, claimed the cooperative's architect, Elliot Glass, orally approved the alteration. Glass denied specific approval.
- Levandusky's proprietary lease required prior written consent for alterations to water, gas, or steam risers.
- Levandusky's architect prepared plans, approved by Glass, and submitted them to the board. The plans did not show a change to the steam riser.
- The board approved Levandusky's plans on March 14, 1988.
- On March 15, 1988, Levandusky executed an "Alteration Agreement" incorporating "Renovation Guidelines" he largely drafted, which required advance written approval for changes to the heating system.
- In late spring 1988, the building's managing agent learned Levandusky intended to move the steam riser and informed the board.
- Both Levandusky and the board consulted engineer John Flynn, who advised that relocating risers was technically feasible but risky and should be avoided.
- At a June 13, 1988 meeting, which Levandusky attended, the board resolved to "reaffirm the policy — no relocation of risers."
- At a June 23, 1988 meeting, the board denied Levandusky a variance to move his riser and conditioned approval of his renovation plans on an acceptable redesign.
- Levandusky hired a contractor who severed and jogged the kitchen steam riser.
- In August 1988, the board issued a "stop work" order.
- Levandusky installed air-conditioning units that allegedly did not conform to Landmarks Preservation Commission requirements.

**Procedural History**
- Levandusky commenced an Article 78 proceeding to set aside the stop work order.
- The cooperative cross-petitioned to compel Levandusky to restore the riser and remove the air conditioners.
- Supreme Court initially granted Levandusky's petition, annulled the stop work order, and dismissed the counterclaims, finding the board's decision arbitrary and capricious.
- On reargument, Supreme Court withdrew its decision, dismissed Levandusky's petition, and ordered him to restore the riser, applying the **business judgment rule**. It adhered to its ruling on the air conditioners.
- The Appellate Division modified the judgment, affirming the dismissal of the air conditioner claim but reversing on the stop work order, agreeing with Supreme Court's initial decision that the board's action was arbitrary and capricious. Two justices dissented, arguing the board's action was within its business judgment.
- The cooperative appealed to the Court of Appeals.

**Issue**
What standard of review should courts apply when a cooperative corporation's board of directors seeks to enforce building policy against a tenant-shareholder?

**Holding**
The appellant (One Fifth Avenue Apartment Corp.) wins. The **business judgment rule** furnishes the correct standard of review for decisions made by cooperative boards. The order of the Appellate Division is modified.

**Rules**
- **Business Judgment Rule.** Courts will not inquire into actions of corporate directors taken in good faith and in the exercise of honest judgment in the lawful and legitimate furtherance of corporate purposes, so long as they have not breached their fiduciary obligation.
- **Cooperative Board Authority.** A cooperative board's decision will be upheld if it acts for the purposes of the cooperative, within the scope of its authority, and in good faith.
- Rejected: **Reasonableness Standard.** This standard requires the board to demonstrate its decision was reasonable and permits the court to evaluate the merits or wisdom of the board's decision.

**Reasoning**
- Cooperative and condominium associations are "quasi-governments" where owners consent to be governed by a board.
- Boards have broad powers, including approving structural alterations, which can restrict property rights.
- However, agreement to submit to board authority is voluntary, and community control offers economic and social benefits.
- The standard of review must balance protecting individual residents from abuse with not undermining the community's collective interests.
- The **business judgment rule** best balances these interests because:
    - A cooperative corporation is a corporation, subject to the Business Corporation Law.
    - Courts are ill-equipped to evaluate business judgments; directors have unique capabilities and experience.
    - Board members possess experience with their building's peculiar needs not shared by courts.
    - Allowing full judicial review of every board decision threatens the stability of the common living arrangement and hampers the board's effectiveness.
    - The rule protects board decisions from indiscriminate attack while permitting review of improper decisions (e.g., no legitimate relationship to welfare, singles out individuals, taken without notice, beyond scope of authority).
- Levandusky failed to show the board breached its duty.
    - He did not comply with the Alteration Agreement or Renovation Guidelines requiring explicit written notice for heating system changes.
    - The board promptly consulted its engineer and notified Levandusky of its policy against moving pipes.
    - His claims of personal animosity and disparate treatment are conclusory and unrefuted by evidence from the board.
    - The board's decision to adhere to a uniform policy regarding the piping system, based on the engineer's advice, falls within the rule.

**Concurrence (Titone)**
- Agrees with the majority's result but disagrees with applying the **business judgment rule**.
- Argues that the shareholder's challenge was an Article 78 proceeding, which should be governed by CPLR 7803's "arbitrary and capricious" standard.
- The **business judgment rule** is suited for challenges to management's business decisions (e.g., investment choices, waste), focusing on honesty and integrity, not for administration of bylaws governing shareholder rights.
- Courts are well-suited to review the rationality or arbitrariness of board decisions regarding renovations, unlike complex business judgments.
- The "arbitrary and capricious" standard, properly applied, does not inquire into the "reasonableness" or wisdom of the decision but rather whether a rational basis exists.
- Focusing on board members' ulterior personal motives (as the majority's application of the business judgment rule might allow) is impractical and undesirable, leading to more litigation and judicial entanglement in petty grievances.
- The board's concern about the risk of unforeseen problems with the old pipe system, articulated by the engineer, provides a rational basis for its decision, which would satisfy the "arbitrary and capricious" standard.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the limits of individual property rights within a communal living arrangement, specifically in the context of cooperative apartments. It connects to the broader theme of **owner sovereignty and its limits** by showing how an individual owner's right to use their property is constrained by the collective governance structure they voluntarily joined. It contrasts with cases like *Jacque v. Steenberg Homes*, where the owner's right to exclude was paramount, by demonstrating a context where individual autonomy is significantly curtailed for the common good. It also relates to *Nahrstedt v. Lakeside Village Condominium Association* in its discussion of the deference given to community association rules, though *Nahrstedt* applied a "reasonableness" standard to recorded use restrictions.
2. A tension exists between the majority's desire to protect boards from "undue court involvement" and the concurrence's concern that the **business judgment rule** might actually lead to more litigation by inviting inquiry into "malevolent conduct" or "personal motives."

**Takeaway:** Courts will generally defer to the decisions of cooperative boards under the **business judgment rule**, upholding their actions if taken in good faith, within their authority, and for the cooperative's purposes, placing a high burden on the challenging tenant-shareholder.
