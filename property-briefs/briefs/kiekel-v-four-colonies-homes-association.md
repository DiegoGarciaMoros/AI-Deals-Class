## Kiekel v. Four Colonies Homes Association (Kansas Court of Appeals, 2007)

**Facts**
- In 1971, the Four Colonies subdivision was created as a planned unit development, subject to a Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions (Declaration) and Bylaws.
- The Declaration could be amended by 75% of lot owners, while the Bylaws could be amended by a majority vote of owners present at a meeting.
- The Declaration included various restrictive covenants but did not expressly prohibit or permit renting. It did refer to "lessees and tenants."
- Since 1971, lot owners in Four Colonies have rented their properties, with 100-115 lots rented as of 2005.
- In 1997, Four Colonies proposed a bylaw amendment to restrict renting but withdrew it, determining it conflicted with the Declaration.
- James A. Kiekel and Margaret G. Kiekel (Kiekels) owned eight rental properties in the subdivision since 1988.
- Four Colonies received complaints about tenants' conduct and property maintenance, including from the Kiekels' tenants.
- In October 2004, Four Colonies proposed a new bylaw amendment to impose various rental restrictions, including prohibitions on future rentals, approval of lease agreements, and authority for the Association to terminate leases and evict tenants.
- At a special meeting, 51.34% of the 372 attending lot owners voted to approve the 2004 bylaw amendment.
- Four Colonies sent the Kiekels a letter requesting tenant information based on the new bylaw.
- The Kiekels filed a petition for declaratory judgment, arguing the 2004 bylaw amendment was void.
- Four Colonies filed a counterclaim for injunctive relief, seeking to prevent the Kiekels from renting their properties, alleging violations of the Declaration's commercial use and noxious activity restrictions.

**Procedural History**
- The district court denied the Kiekels' petition for declaratory judgment, finding the bylaw enforceable and not in conflict with the Declaration.
- The district court denied Four Colonies' counterclaim for injunctive relief.
- The Kiekels appealed the denial of their petition for declaratory judgment.
- Four Colonies cross-appealed the denial of its request for injunctive relief.

**Issue**
Can a homeowners association impose new property use restrictions, such as rental limitations, through a bylaw amendment when its Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions, which defines fundamental property rights, requires a super-majority vote for amendment and does not explicitly grant such authority to bylaws?

**Holding**
The Kiekels (appellants) win. A homeowners association cannot impose new property use restrictions through a bylaw amendment when the Declaration, which governs fundamental property rights, requires a super-majority for amendment and does not delegate such authority to the bylaws. Reversed in part and Affirmed in part.

**Rules**
- **Interpretation of Restrictive Covenants.** Covenants and agreements restricting the free use of property are strictly construed against limitations upon such use, and doubts will be resolved in favor of the unrestricted use of property.
- **Declaration vs. Bylaws.** A declaration is the enabling document or "constitution" of a community association, containing the plan of development and essentials of ownership, while bylaws set forth procedures for internal government and operation.
- **Amendment Authority.** Fundamental provisions dealing with ownership and property rights are typically in the declaration, and any attempt to restrict property use as authorized in the declaration is void and unenforceable unless the declaration is properly amended.
- **Rejected: Bylaws can expand, interpret, or define Declaration.** The court rejected the argument that a bylaw provision stating bylaws "expand, interpret or specifically define certain portions of the Articles of Incorporation... and the Declaration" granted authority to impose new property use restrictions.

**Reasoning**
- The Declaration is the "enabling document" or "constitution" of the association, defining fundamental ownership rights.
- The Bylaws typically contain governance and operational provisions, functioning like corporate bylaws.
- The Declaration's amendment procedure requires a 75% super-majority vote, while the Bylaws require only a simple majority of those present, indicating that amending the Declaration is intended to be more difficult due to its impact on property rights.
- The Declaration did not expressly prohibit or permit renting, but it did refer to "lessees and tenants," implying that renting was contemplated.
- The 2004 bylaw amendment imposed extensive property use restrictions concerning renting, effectively eliminating the right to rent for many owners.
- Such extensive restrictions on property use can only be accomplished through an amendment to the Declaration, not the Bylaws.
- The Declaration did not delegate authority to the Board of Directors to impose property use restrictions through bylaws, even though it allowed delegation of common area enjoyment rights to tenants "in accordance with the Bylaws." This specific delegation did not extend to general rental restrictions.
- The Kiekels had notice that their property rights could be restricted by an amendment to the Declaration, but not by a bylaw amendment.
- Four Colonies' prior conduct (withdrawing a similar 1997 bylaw amendment because it conflicted with the Declaration) supports the conclusion that such restrictions require a Declaration amendment.
- Regarding injunctive relief, the Kiekels' renting did not violate the Declaration's commercial use restriction because earning rental income is not the same as conducting a commercial business on the property, as long as lessees use it as a residence.
- The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying injunctive relief based on the noxious activity restriction, as Four Colonies failed to prove the Kiekels' tenants were uniquely problematic or that the Kiekels' maintenance issues were worse than others.
- Four Colonies had an adequate legal remedy for maintenance issues through its right to restoration and the ability to sue for unpaid assessments.

**NOTES:**
1. This case highlights the distinction between a Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions and Bylaws in a homeowners association context. It reinforces the principle that fundamental property rights, especially those restricting use, are primarily governed by the Declaration and require strict adherence to its amendment procedures. This connects to cases like Nahrstedt v. Lakeside Village Condominium Association, which also deals with the enforceability and interpretation of restrictive covenants in common interest communities, emphasizing the importance of the founding documents.
