## Producers Lumber & Supply Co. v. Olney Building Co. (Texas Courts of Civil Appeals, 1960)

**Facts**
- Prior to November 1, 1956, Elliott Construction Co., Inc. owned Lot 8.
- On November 1, 1956, H. P. Orts, as Assistant Secretary and Agent and Attorney in Fact for Elliott Construction Co., Inc., executed a warranty deed conveying Lot 8 to Producers Lumber & Supply Co. (appellant) for $1,428. This deed was properly recorded.
- Producers Lumber & Supply Co. purchased Lot 8 with the intention that its general manager, George R. Montgomery, and his wife would build a home there.
- Montgomery and his wife had the lot graded and planted trees and grass on it.
- On or about February 27, 1958, Orts and Elliott decided to construct nine dwellings, including one on Lot 8.
- Orts asked A. L. Burden, secretary-treasurer of Olney Building Co. (appellee), if Lot 8 had been sold. Burden, after consulting a map, told Orts it had not been sold.
- Orts inspected Lot 8 and noticed the planted trees, thinking it was "nice of someone to plant trees on this lot."
- Construction of the nine houses began shortly thereafter.
- On April 1, 1958, Olney Building Co. ordered a Title Binder for all nine lots from Stewart Title Company for interim financing.
- On April 14, 1958, Orts learned from the Title Company that Lot 8 had been sold to Producers Lumber & Supply Co.
- Orts notified Montgomery of the situation; this was Producers Lumber & Supply Co.'s first notice of construction on its lot.
- The dwelling on Lot 8 was almost completed when the discovery was made.
- Orts began negotiations with Montgomery to reach a settlement. Orts told Montgomery that Montgomery had him "at his mercy."
- Various offers and counter-offers were made, but no settlement was reached.
- On April 22, 1958, Orts broke off negotiations and sent his construction superintendent with a crew and heavy equipment to Lot 8.
- The dwelling on Lot 8 was demolished, leaving only debris.
- The parties stipulated that the dwelling had a value of $5,000 and enhanced the value of Lot 8 by $5,000.

**Procedural History**
- Producers Lumber & Supply Co. sued Olney Building Co. for damages.
- The jury found that Orts acted in good faith in erecting the building, that the cost to restore the lot was $600, that Orts acted maliciously in removing the building, and awarded $300 in exemplary damages.
- The trial court rendered judgment for Producers Lumber & Supply Co. for $600, disregarding the jury's findings on malice and exemplary damages.
- Producers Lumber & Supply Co. appealed, contending the judgment should have been $5,900.

**Issue**
Can a builder who mistakenly constructs a dwelling on another's land in good faith, but then maliciously demolishes it, recover for the value of the improvements or avoid liability for the demolition?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Producers Lumber & Supply Co.) wins. A builder who, after mistakenly constructing a dwelling on another's land in good faith, resorts to self-help and maliciously demolishes the dwelling, commits waste and cannot claim equitable relief for the improvements. The judgment is amended to include the stipulated value of the dwelling and exemplary damages. Affirmed as amended.

**Rules**
- **Fixtures.** Where a person erects a building upon the land of another without his knowledge and consent, the building becomes a fixture and belongs to the owner of the land, and the builder is without remedy.
- **Good Faith Improver.** It is only where a person places permanent improvements upon land belonging to another in a good faith belief that he is the owner of the land that he has any remedy at all.
- **Equitable Relief for Good Faith Improver.** A court of equity may grant relief to a good faith improver, such as permitting removal of the building if it can be done without great injury, or allowing recovery for the enhanced value of the land, or ordering sale of the land and improvements.
- **Duty to Investigate Records.** A person contemplating the improvement of land is under a duty to investigate the records before commencing work, as recorded deeds impart notice of outstanding claims.
- **Waste.** An improver is not authorized to go upon the land of another without his knowledge and consent and demolish improvements placed thereon; if he does so, he commits waste and must pay the landowner for such waste.
- **Unclean Hands Doctrine.** A party who resorts to self-help and takes the law into their own hands, causing great destruction of property, cannot come into a court of equity with "unclean hands" to seek equitable remedies like reimbursement for enhanced value.

**Reasoning**
- The jury's findings that Orts acted maliciously in removing the building and that Producers was entitled to exemplary damages were well supported by the evidence and should not have been disregarded.
- Orts' testimony detailed the extensive and destructive methods used to demolish the dwelling, including bulldozers, cranes, drop hammers, air hammers, and cutting torches, and his admission that $2,768 "went down the drain" as if he "had set a match to it."
- Even if the jury's finding of good faith in building the dwelling was supported by the evidence (despite the recorded deed conveying the lot to Producers), Olney Building Co. cannot prevail on a plea of improvement in good faith due to the malicious destruction.
- While the law provides remedies for good faith improvers, these remedies are equitable and require the improver to seek court intervention.
- Orts' actions in demolishing the dwelling without Producers' knowledge or consent constituted waste, making Olney Building Co. liable for the stipulated value of the dwelling ($5,000).
- By resorting to self-help and destroying the property, Orts (and thus Olney Building Co.) came to court with "unclean hands," precluding any equitable claim for reimbursement for the enhanced value of the lot.
- Producers' failure to settle on Orts' terms did not constitute unclean hands, as Producers was not seeking an equitable remedy but suing for damages for willful destruction of its property.

**Dissent (Barrow, Justice)**
- The dissent disagrees with allowing Producers to recover $5,000 for the value of the house, arguing that Producers had no investment in it.
- The jury's finding of good faith in building the house was supported by the evidence, and the recorded deed was only one evidentiary fact.
- Texas law allows a good faith improver to obtain relief, including removing improvements, and does not lose equitable rights by doing so.
- Olney Building Co. was not seeking reimbursement but interposing its rights as a defense to a damage suit.
- The "demolished" property belonged to Olney, not Producers, as Producers would have had to pay for the enhancement in value.
- Producers attempted to drive an "unconscionable bargain" by refusing reasonable offers and demanding a low price for the completed house, effectively having Olney "at its mercy."
- The "unclean hands" doctrine should not apply to Olney because it was exercising a right to remove its property, and it was Producers who acted inequitably.
- Awarding Producers $5,000 for a house it did not build and had no investment in constitutes an additional punitive damage and an "inequitable decision" that makes Producers richer to the damage of Olney.

**NOTES:**
1. This case highlights the tension between the common law rule that improvements become part of the land (fixtures) and equitable principles designed to prevent unjust enrichment for good faith improvers. It connects to **Pile v. Pedrick** and **Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands** by showing how courts grapple with mistaken improvements on land, but here, the improver's self-help and malicious destruction drastically alter the outcome. Unlike those cases where the improver sought to keep the improvement or be compensated, here the improver destroyed it, leading to liability for waste. The "unclean hands" doctrine is a key equitable principle applied here, similar to how equitable considerations might influence remedies in cases like **eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C.**
2. The dissent raises an interesting point about whether the "unclean hands" doctrine should apply when the improver is merely exercising a right to remove their property, even if done destructively, especially when the landowner is perceived as acting inequitably in negotiations. This could lead to a discussion about the limits of self-help even when a party might have a colorable claim to the property.

**Takeaway:** A good faith improver who resorts to self-help and maliciously destroys improvements on another's land forfeits equitable remedies and is liable for waste.
