## Cowen v. Pressprich (New York Supreme Court, Appellate Term, 1922)

**Facts**
- Plaintiffs, stockbrokers, agreed to sell and deliver an Oregon Short Line Railroad bond to defendants, also stockbrokers.
- Plaintiffs ordered the bond from a third bond house.
- The third bond house mistakenly sent plaintiffs an Oregon and California Railroad bond.
- Plaintiffs, also by mistake, sent this Oregon and California bond to defendants via a messenger boy, Goldberg, along with a slip describing an Oregon Short Line bond.
- Goldberg dropped the envelope containing the bond and slip through a slot in defendants' delivery window.
- Defendants' partner, Mr. Quackenbush, immediately saw the bond was an Oregon and California bond, not the Oregon Short Line bond they expected.
- Quackenbush opened the window and yelled "Cowen."
- An unidentified boy, not Goldberg, stepped up, mumbled "all right," took the bond, and left.
- The bond was a fully negotiable bearer bond.
- Defendants refused to compensate plaintiffs for the loss of the bond.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs sued defendants for conversion in Municipal Court.
- The Municipal Court found in favor of the plaintiffs.
- Defendants appealed to the New York Supreme Court, Appellate Term.

**Issue**
- Are involuntary bailees absolutely liable for conversion when they attempt to return mistakenly delivered property but deliver it to the wrong person?

**Holding**
- Plaintiffs (respondents) win. When involuntary bailees exercise dominion over mistakenly delivered property by attempting to redeliver it, they become as responsible as voluntary bailees and are absolutely liable for delivery to the wrong person. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Voluntary Bailment Duty.** In voluntary bailments, the bailee has an absolute duty to deliver the bailed article to the right person, and delivery to the wrong person cannot be excused by care, good faith, or innocence.
- **Involuntary Bailee Custody.** An involuntary bailee, as long as their lack of volition continues, is not under a duty to care for or guard the subject of the bailment and cannot be held liable for negligence in custody.
- **Involuntary Bailee Dominion.** If an involuntary bailee exercises any dominion over the bailed thing, they become as responsible as if they were a voluntary bailee.
- **Rejected: Heugh v. L. & N. W. R. R. Co. Doctrine.** The doctrine that an involuntary bailee is not under an absolute duty to deliver to the proper person, but only to exercise reasonable and proper care, is rejected in American cases.

**Reasoning**
- The defendants became involuntary bailees upon the mistaken delivery of the bond.
- While an involuntary bailee is not initially obligated to care for the property, exercising dominion over it changes their responsibility.
- Defendants exercised dominion by undertaking to redeliver the bond.
- Once dominion is exercised, the standard of care becomes that of a voluntary bailee, which includes an absolute duty to deliver to the correct person.
- Defendants could have protected themselves by telephoning the plaintiffs or sending the bond back with their own messenger.
- Instead, they chose to return the bond to an unidentified messenger, acting at their peril.
- The court distinguishes *Krumsky v. Loeser* because that case involved fraud and the defendants' total lack of knowledge of the true owner, neither of which applied here.
- The action was tried as one for conversion, and the judgment can only be sustained on that theory.

**Dissent (Lehman, J.)**
- The defendants were put in possession of the bond by mistake and promptly discovered it.
- They committed no "overt act" of interference with the bond except attempting to divest themselves of possession by delivering it to a person they believed to be the plaintiffs' messenger.
- This act was consistent with the plaintiffs' continued ownership and was an honest attempt to restore possession.
- An implied contract of bailment, with its consequent obligations, arises only where a person does some act inconsistent with not accepting the possession thrust upon them.
- The defendants did not claim title or right to possession; they merely transferred physical possession.
- If the defendants used unreasonable means to return the goods, they might be liable for negligence, but not conversion, as they did not exercise dominion.
- The defendants acted in good faith and under the reasonable belief they were returning the bond to the correct messenger.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the concept of **bailment**, particularly **involuntary bailment**, and the point at which an involuntary bailee's duties increase. It contrasts with cases like *Berg v. Wiley*, where a landlord's self-help repossession was deemed conversion, by focusing on the bailee's actions regarding mistakenly received property rather than a landlord-tenant relationship. The case highlights the strict liability aspect of conversion for misdelivery once dominion is exercised, even if the initial possession was involuntary.
2. The dissent raises an interesting point about the distinction between an "overt act" of dominion that creates an absolute liability for conversion versus an "honest attempt" to return property that might only lead to liability for negligence if the means were unreasonable. This tension could be explored in class.

**Takeaway:** An involuntary bailee who attempts to return mistakenly delivered property becomes absolutely liable for conversion if they deliver it to the wrong person, as this act constitutes exercising dominion over the property.
