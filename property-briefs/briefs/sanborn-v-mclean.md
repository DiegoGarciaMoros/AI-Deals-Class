## Sanborn v. McLean (Michigan Supreme Court, 1925)

**Facts**
- In 1891, the Green Lawn subdivision was platted with 91 lots on Collingwood Avenue, designed for residence purposes.
- On December 28, 1892, Robert J. and Joseph R. McLaughlin, common owners of the lots, deeded lots 37-41 and 58-62 with restrictions allowing only residences costing at least $2,500 and set back 20 feet from the street.
- On July 24, 1893, the McLaughlins conveyed lots 17-21, 78-82, and lot 98 with the same restrictions.
- On September 7, 1893, the McLaughlins conveyed lot 86 (the defendants' lot) without express restrictions in the deed.
- Subsequent deeds to other lots were executed by the McLaughlins, some with restrictions and some without.
- For over 30 years, all lots on Collingwood Avenue were used strictly for residences, with expensive dwellings built on them.
- In 1910 or 1911, Mr. McLean purchased lot 86 on contract, which had a partly built dwelling, and he completed and occupied it.
- Defendants John A. McLean and Christina McLean (owners of lot 86) began to erect a gasoline filling station at the rear of their lot.
- Plaintiffs, owners of adjoining and nearby land in the subdivision, sought to enjoin the construction of the gasoline station.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs filed a bill to enjoin the violation of building restrictions.
- The trial court issued a decree enjoining the defendants and ordering the work done on the building to be torn down.
- Defendants appealed.

**Issue**
Can a reciprocal negative easement be enforced against a property owner whose deed does not contain express restrictions, if the owner had constructive notice of a general residential plan for the subdivision?

**Holding**
Plaintiffs win. A reciprocal negative easement can be enforced against a property owner whose deed does not contain express restrictions, if the owner had constructive notice of a general residential plan for the subdivision. Modified and affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Reciprocal Negative Easement.** If the owner of two or more lots, so situated as to bear the relation, sells one with restrictions of benefit to the land retained, the servitude becomes mutual, and, during the period of restraint, the owner of the lot or lots retained may do nothing forbidden to the owner of the lot sold; they being subject to a reciprocal negative easement.
- **Running with the Land.** A reciprocal negative easement runs with the land sold, is not personal to owners, but is operative upon the use of the land by any owner having actual or constructive notice thereof, passing its benefits and carrying its obligations to all purchasers of land subject to its affirmative or negative mandates.
- **Origin of Reciprocal Negative Easements.** Reciprocal negative easements are never retroactive, but arise, if at all, out of a benefit accorded land retained by restrictions imposed upon neighboring land sold by a common owner, and may not arise and fasten upon one lot by reason of other lot owners conforming to a general plan.
- **Notice.** A purchaser of land is bound by a reciprocal negative easement if they have actual or constructive notice of it.
- **Constructive Notice (Recording Acts).** Under recording acts, if an abstract of title shows that a lot, while in the hands of a common owner, was burdened with a reciprocal negative easement, the current owners and their predecessors are bound by constructive notice thereof.
- **Constructive Notice (Character of Neighborhood).** Where the purchaser of a lot in a residential district could not avoid noticing the strictly uniform residence character given the lots by the expensive dwellings thereon, clearly indicating a general plan, he was put to inquiry beyond merely asking his grantor whether there were restrictions.

**Reasoning**
- The subdivision was planned strictly for residence purposes, and the McLaughlins, as common owners, imposed restrictions on many lots sold to carry out this residential scheme.
- When the common owners sold lots with restrictions beneficial to the retained land, a reciprocal negative easement attached to the retained lots, including lot 86.
- This easement was created before the sale of lot 86 to defendants' predecessors in title, even though the deed for lot 86 did not explicitly mention restrictions.
- The defendants had constructive notice of this easement through two means:
    - **Recording Acts:** The abstract of title showed the common owners and the record of deeds for other lots in the plat restricted to carry out the general plan. This provided constructive notice under the recording acts.
    - **Character of the Neighborhood:** Mr. McLean, when he purchased, could not avoid noticing the uniform residential character of the street with expensive dwellings, which clearly indicated a general plan. This put him on inquiry, and a reasonable inquiry would have revealed the reciprocal negative easement.
- The fact that some lots were sold without express restrictions does not defeat the general plan, as purchasers of those lots consistently observed the restrictions.
- The court modified the trial court's decree to allow the constructed portion of the building to remain if it can be utilized for a purpose within the restrictions, rather than requiring its destruction.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a foundational example of how **equitable servitudes** can be implied even without express language in a deed, based on a common scheme and notice. It connects to the concept of **covenants running with the land** but applies in equity where the technical requirements for a real covenant might not be met. It highlights the importance of notice (both record and inquiry) in property law, similar to how **Howard v. Kunto** deals with the importance of possession and tacking for adverse possession.
2. What if the neighborhood had been mixed-use for a long time before the defendants purchased their lot? Would the "character of the neighborhood" still provide constructive notice of a residential-only restriction?

**Takeaway:** A reciprocal negative easement can be implied and enforced against a property owner if there was a common scheme of development and the owner had actual or constructive notice of the restrictions, even if their deed does not explicitly state them.
