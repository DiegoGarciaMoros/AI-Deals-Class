## Medico-Dental Building Co. v. Horton & Converse (Supreme Court of California, 1942)

**Facts**
- In 1925, Horton & Converse (defendant) began occupying space in the Medico-Dental Building as a drug store under a lease.
- On July 1, 1934, defendant entered into a new lease for the same space for 16 years and 4 months with the original lessor.
- This lease stipulated that the premises would be used "as a drug store and for no other business or purpose" and that the lessor agreed "not to lease or sublease any part or portion of the Medico-Dental Building to any other person, firm or corporation for the purpose of maintaining a drug store or selling drugs or ampoules."
- Plaintiff, Medico-Dental Building Company, became the successor in interest to the original lessor.
- On December 30, 1937, plaintiff leased the entire ninth floor of the building to Dr. Boonshaft, a physician, for three years, starting April 15, 1938.
- Dr. Boonshaft's lease stated the premises were "to be used solely as offices for the practice of medicine and dentistry" and that he would not "maintain therein or thereon, nor permit to be maintained therein or thereon, a drug store or drug dispensary, nor will lessee compound or dispense, or permit to be compounded or dispensed, drugs or ampoules except in connection with the regular course of treatment of lessee’s own patients." It also prohibited signs with "Pharmacy," "Drug Store," or "Dispensary."
- Dr. Boonshaft took possession on April 15, 1938, and operated the Boonshaft Medical Group, which provided medical treatment to registered groups and lodge members for a monthly fee, including some drugs, with additional charges for others.
- Dr. Boonshaft maintained a drug room where drugs were sold and prescriptions filled for patients of the Medical Group.
- Dr. Boonshaft obtained a pharmacy license on May 10, 1938.
- Until June 25, 1938, Dr. Boonshaft bought drugs from defendant's store in the building.
- On June 25, 1938, Dr. Boonshaft began buying drugs wholesale from defendant's wholesale department at another location due to sales tax objections.
- In May 1938, a drug store, registered as a pharmacy, was opened on the ninth floor, selling drugs and compounding prescriptions for a charge.
- On July 8, 1938, a sales tax permit was issued to Dr. Boonshaft.
- In the last week of July 1938, defendant learned of Dr. Boonshaft's drug store operation.
- On August 3, 1938, defendant notified plaintiff in writing that Dr. Boonshaft's practice breached its lease and demanded plaintiff stop the drug sales and prescription compounding.
- Between August 3 and August 31, 1938, Dr. Boonshaft's drug store continued to operate.
- Plaintiff's agents conferred about defendant's objection but did not communicate the discussions to defendant.
- On August 8, 1938, at a meeting in defendant's office, plaintiff advised defendant it would "take the matter up further and see what it could do about it and advise defendant."
- On August 19, 1938, plaintiff's attorney informed defendant that "no arrangements could be made with Dr. Boonshaft and plaintiff could not do anything with him."
- Defendant's president responded that it would vacate the premises to avoid waiving its exclusive right. Plaintiff's attorney replied, "Well, use your own judgment about that."
- Plaintiff took no further action.
- The next day, defendant closed its store, put a sign directing customers to other locations, and piled empty packing boxes in its leased premises.
- On August 24, 1938, defendant sent plaintiff a written notice of rescission.
- Defendant vacated the store on August 31, 1938.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiff sued defendant for unpaid rent, electricity charges, and renovation costs.
- The trial court found in favor of the defendant, except for the electricity charge, concluding that plaintiff breached the restrictive covenant in the lease.
- Plaintiff appealed.

**Issue**
Can a tenant rescind a lease and stop paying rent if the landlord breaches a restrictive covenant that was a material part of the lease agreement?

**Holding**
Defendant (lessee) wins. A tenant can rescind a lease and stop paying rent if the landlord breaches a restrictive covenant that goes to the whole consideration of the lease, as such covenants are dependent. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Lease as Contract.** Leases have a dual character as both a conveyance and a contract, and contractual features should be interpreted according to general contract rules to give effect to the parties' intentions.
- **Dependent Covenants.** Covenants in leases are dependent if they run to the entire consideration of the contract, meaning the breach of one covenant defeats the entire object of the other party in entering the lease.
- **Independent Covenants.** Rejected: The rule that all covenants in leases are independent.
- **Material Breach.** A breach of a contractual right that is so dominant or pervasive as to frustrate the purpose of the undertaking justifies rescission.
- **Waiver.** Waiver may be shown by conduct that is inconsistent with the intent to enforce a right, inducing a reasonable belief that the right has been relinquished.

**Reasoning**
- The restrictive covenant was a dependent covenant because it went to the whole consideration of the lease.
    - The lease itself indicated the parties' intention for interdependence, particularly with the restrictive covenant and the rent payment appearing in a rider and the lessee being limited to a drug store use.
    - The exclusive right to operate a drug store was vital to defendant's business, which catered to doctors and dentists in the building.
    - Plaintiff knew the building tenants were the "chief source" of defendant's business.
    - The court rejected the plaintiff's argument that all covenants in leases are independent, distinguishing prior cases.
- Plaintiff breached the restrictive covenant by leasing to Dr. Boonshaft for the purpose of maintaining a drug store and by acquiescing in Dr. Boonshaft's conduct.
    - The trial court reasonably inferred from Dr. Boonshaft's lease provisions and subsequent conduct that plaintiff intended to give him the right to operate a drug store for his patients.
    - The provision in Dr. Boonshaft's lease prohibiting "Pharmacy," "Drug Store," or "Dispensary" signs suggested that a drug store, even if restricted, was contemplated.
    - Plaintiff's property manager admitted that Dr. Boonshaft was violating his lease.
    - The evidence showed Dr. Boonshaft was operating a drug store, with a registered pharmacist, selling drugs, and compounding prescriptions.
    - The fact that Dr. Boonshaft could legally operate a drug store as a physician with a pharmacist did not satisfy plaintiff's contractual obligation to protect defendant from competition.
    - Technical subterfuges are disregarded; the substance of the situation determines the issue.
- The breach was substantial and went to the whole of the consideration.
    - The exclusive nature of defendant's pharmacy, its limited patronage source, and its long tenancy established the materiality of the breach.
    - The lease would not have been executed without the restrictive covenant.
    - The potential loss of business from Dr. Boonshaft's drug store was a significant financial impact, not easily measurable in damages.
    - Defendant was not required to prove the exact pecuniary detriment.
- Defendant did not waive the breach.
    - Defendant promptly protested the breach and allowed plaintiff time to resolve the issue.
    - Plaintiff's final communication that it "could not do anything" with Dr. Boonshaft forced defendant to choose between waiving the breach or vacating.
    - The trial court found that defendant did not assist Dr. Boonshaft or have prior knowledge of the competitive activity.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the **contractual nature of leases** and the concept of **dependent covenants**. It contrasts with the traditional common law view that lease covenants are independent, as seen in cases like *Berg v. Wiley*, where a landlord's breach of a covenant might not excuse a tenant's obligation to pay rent. Here, the court emphasizes the parties' intent and the materiality of the covenant to the entire consideration, allowing the tenant to rescind.
