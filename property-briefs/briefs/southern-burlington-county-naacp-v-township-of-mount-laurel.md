## Southern Burlington County NAACP v. Township of Mount Laurel (Supreme Court of New Jersey, 1975)

**Facts**
- In 1950, Mount Laurel Township was a rural agricultural area with a population of 2817.
- After 1950, the township experienced significant population growth, reaching 5249 by 1960 and 11,221 by 1970.
- The township adopted a general zoning ordinance in 1964, which was similar to earlier, less restrictive enactments.
- This ordinance zoned 29.2% of the land for industry, 1.2% for retail business, and the remainder for residential use.
- The residential zones (R-1, R-1D, R-2, R-3) permitted only single-family, detached dwellings.
- The ordinance required minimum lot sizes of 9,375 sq ft (R-1), 11,000 sq ft (R-2), and 20,000 sq ft (R-3), and minimum dwelling floor areas of 1,100 sq ft (one-story) or 1,300 sq ft (one-and-a-half stories or higher).
- The R-1D zone, a "cluster" zone created in 1968, reduced minimum lot size to 10,000 sq ft but maintained similar dwelling floor area requirements and an overall density limit.
- The township approved four Planned Unit Development (PUD) projects, which included multi-family housing but were designed for middle and upper-income residents, with restrictions on the number of bedrooms and school-age children.
- In 1972, the township created an R-4 zone for Planned Adult Retirement Communities (PARC), with extensive development requirements making it unsuitable for low and moderate-income retirees and restrictions on age and number of residents.
- The township's 1969 Master Plan Report recognized the need for positive action regarding substandard housing in the Springville section (R-3 zone), but official policy was to wait for dilapidation and then forbid occupancy.
- A 1968 private non-profit effort to build subsidized multi-family housing in Springville was thwarted by municipal requirements for single-family detached dwellings on 20,000 sq ft lots.
- The township's land use regulations were intended to result in economic discrimination and exclusion of substantial segments of the area population to keep down local property taxes, particularly school costs.
- Plaintiffs, including minority group poor, challenged the township's land use regulations.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court found Mount Laurel's zoning ordinance invalid and ordered the township to study housing needs and present a plan for affirmative public action.
- The township appealed to the Appellate Division.
- Plaintiffs cross-appealed, arguing the plan should consider regional housing needs beyond those connected to the township.
- The Supreme Court of New Jersey certified the appeals on its own motion.

**Issue**
Can a developing municipality use land use regulations to make it physically and economically impossible to provide low and moderate-income housing, thereby excluding people based on their income and resources?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (NAACP) wins. A developing municipality cannot use land use regulations to prevent the opportunity for low and moderate-income housing. Modified and Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Police Power and General Welfare.** All police power enactments, including zoning regulations, must conform to state constitutional requirements of substantive due process and equal protection, promoting public health, safety, morals, or the general welfare.
- **Regional General Welfare.** When land use regulation has a substantial external impact, the general welfare of the state's citizens beyond the borders of the particular municipality cannot be disregarded and must be recognized and served.
- **Housing as Basic Need.** Proper provision for adequate housing for all categories of people is an absolute essential in promoting the general welfare required in all local land use regulation.
- **Presumptive Obligation for Developing Municipalities.** A developing municipality has a presumptive obligation to plan and provide, by its land use regulations, the reasonable opportunity for an appropriate variety and choice of housing, including low and moderate-cost housing, to meet the needs, desires, and resources of all categories of people who may desire to live within its boundaries.
- **Negative Obligation.** A developing municipality may not adopt regulations or policies which thwart or preclude the opportunity for low and moderate-income housing.
- **Affirmative Obligation.** A developing municipality must affirmatively afford the opportunity for low and moderate-income housing, at least to the extent of the municipality's fair share of the present and prospective regional need.
- **Burden of Proof (Exclusionary Zoning).** When a developing municipality's land use regulations have not made realistically possible a variety and choice of housing, including adequate provision for low and moderate-income housing, or have expressly prescribed requirements or restrictions that preclude or substantially hinder it, a facial showing of violation of substantive due process or equal protection under the state constitution is made, and the burden shifts to the municipality to establish a valid basis for its action or non-action.
- **Fiscal Reasons for Exclusion (Rejected).** No municipality may exclude or limit categories of housing for the purpose of keeping down local taxes.
- **Ecological/Environmental Reasons for Exclusion (Limited).** While land use regulations should consider ecological or environmental factors, these cannot be used as a pretext to limit housing to single-family dwellings on large lots if the area is amenable to utility installations.

**Reasoning**
- Mount Laurel's zoning ordinance permits only single-family detached dwellings, prohibiting multi-family housing, townhouses, and mobile homes, which are essential for low and moderate-income families.
- The PUDs allowed multi-family housing but were designed for affluent residents, with restrictions on bedroom numbers and school-age children, clearly intended to limit families with children and reduce educational costs.
- The minimum lot area, lot frontage, and building size requirements in Mount Laurel's residential zones are so restrictive that they preclude single-family housing for moderate-income families, significantly increasing housing costs.
- The township zoned almost 30% of its area for industrial uses, far exceeding the realistic potential for industrial development, thereby removing an unreasonable amount of land from possible residential development, seemingly for local fiscal reasons.
- The court finds that Mount Laurel's zoning ordinance is presumptively contrary to the general welfare and outside the intended scope of the zoning power because it makes it impossible to provide low and moderate-income housing.
- The court rejects the municipality's primary justification of fiscal benefits, stating that relief from the tax system must come from other branches of government, not by restricting housing types through zoning.
- The court also rejects environmental concerns as a primary justification for large-lot zoning in this case, noting that the flat land is amenable to utility installations.
- The court emphasizes that the "general welfare" in land use regulation extends beyond municipal boundaries to the regional need for housing, especially for low and moderate-income families.
- The court modifies the trial court's remedy, invalidating the ordinance only in the specified particulars and giving the township 90 days to amend it, rather than nullifying the entire ordinance.
- The court clarifies that its role is not to build housing but to ensure the municipality provides the opportunity through appropriate land use regulations.

**Dissent (None.)**

**Concurrence (Mountain, J.)**
- Agrees with the conclusions but would base the decision on an interpretation of the statutory term "general welfare" in N.J.S.A. 40:55-32, rather than on state constitutional grounds.

**Concurrence (Pashman, J.)**
- Joins the majority but believes the court should go farther and faster, extending the principles to all municipalities in the state (developing, developed, and rural) and establishing a policy of active judicial enforcement of both negative and affirmative obligations.
- Highlights the widespread nature of exclusionary zoning, its motivations (fiscal concerns and prejudice), and its various forms (minimum house/lot size, prohibition of multi-family housing, bedroom restrictions, prohibition of mobile homes, overzoning for nonresidential uses).
- Emphasizes the acute national housing shortage, the burden on employees due to job migration to suburbs, and the risk of prolonged judicial inaction allowing exclusionary practices to become permanent.
- Details the affirmative obligations of developing municipalities, including considering regional housing needs in all planning activities, cooperating in regional planning efforts, and encouraging private and public efforts to provide low and moderate-income housing.
- Notes limitations on affirmative obligations, such as not requiring a municipality to assume more than its fair share of regional needs and allowing control over the pace and sequence of development, provided regulations are reasonable and least exclusionary.
- Argues that developed suburban municipalities also have negative and affirmative obligations, especially when land becomes available for development or redevelopment.
- States that even rural municipalities should not establish inherently exclusionary land use controls that bear no substantial relationship to legitimate zoning purposes.
- Proposes a four-step judicial process for enforcement: identify the relevant region, determine regional housing needs, allocate needs among municipalities, and shape a suitable remedial order.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is foundational for understanding the **Mount Laurel doctrine** and the concept of **exclusionary zoning**. It significantly expands the interpretation of the "general welfare" clause in zoning to include regional housing needs, especially for low and moderate-income individuals. It connects to *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.* by acknowledging the evolving nature of zoning law and the need for judicial intervention when conditions change, as warned in *Pierro v. Baxendale*. It also contrasts with cases like *Lionshead Lake, Inc. v. Township of Wayne* and *Fischer v. Township of Bedminster* which previously sanctioned broad restrictive municipal decisions.
2. A tension exists between the court's reluctance to act as a "super planning board" and the need for judicial oversight to ensure compliance. How far should courts go in dictating specific zoning changes or housing types to municipalities?

**Takeaway:** Developing municipalities have a constitutional obligation under state law to provide a realistic opportunity for a fair share of regional low and moderate-income housing, and cannot use zoning to exclude such housing for fiscal reasons.
