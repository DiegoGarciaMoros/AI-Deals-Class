## Hecht v. Superior Court (Court of Appeal of the State of California, 1993)

**Facts**
- William E. Kane (decedent) deposited 15 vials of his sperm at California Cryobank, Inc. in October 1991.
- On September 24, 1991, Kane signed a "Specimen Storage Agreement" with the sperm bank, instructing it to continue storing or release specimens to the executor of his estate upon his death. He also authorized release of his specimens to Deborah Ellen Hecht (petitioner), his girlfriend.
- On September 27, 1991, Kane executed a will naming Hecht as executor and bequeathing all rights to his stored sperm to her, stating his wish that she use it to have his child.
- On October 21, 1991, Kane wrote a letter to his children, stating his intention for Hecht to have a child by him after his death using his frozen sperm samples.
- Kane committed suicide on October 30, 1991.
- Robert L. Greene was appointed special administrator of Kane's estate on November 18, 1991.
- Kane's adult children, William E. Kane, Jr. and Katharine E. Kane (real parties in interest), filed will contests on December 3, 1991.
- A "First Settlement Agreement" was orally placed on the record in December 1991 to resolve estate matters, but it did not explicitly mention the sperm. Hecht later alleged this agreement was breached and unenforceable.
- In October 1992, the administrator filed a petition for approval of a "Second Settlement Agreement" which would assign any estate interest in the sperm to Hecht, but this agreement was not finalized due to an objection from a creditor.
- On November 5, 1992, the administrator filed a "Petition for Alternative Relief," seeking instructions from the court regarding the disposition of the sperm, including destruction, distribution to the children, or distribution to Hecht.
- The children argued for destruction of the sperm on public policy grounds, citing concerns about the welfare of potential children and disruption to existing families.
- Hecht argued the sperm was a gift to her, or if an estate asset, should be distributed to her per the will and settlement agreements, and that destruction would violate her procreative rights.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court ordered the sperm destroyed.
- Hecht filed a petition for peremptory writ of mandate/prohibition to vacate the order.

**Issue**
Can a probate court order the destruction of a decedent's cryogenically preserved sperm when the decedent's will bequeathed it to his girlfriend for her use in artificial insemination?

**Holding**
Petitioner (Hecht) wins. The trial court abused its discretion in ordering the destruction of the decedent's sperm. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Probate Court Jurisdiction.** The power of the probate court extends only to the property of the decedent.
- **Property Interest in Sperm.** A decedent has an interest in his stored sperm, in the nature of ownership, to the extent of decision-making authority over its use for reproduction, which constitutes "property" under Probate Code section 62.
- **Public Policy on Artificial Insemination of Unmarried Women.** California public policy does not prohibit artificial insemination of unmarried women.
- **Public Policy on Postmortem Artificial Insemination.** California public policy does not prohibit postmortem artificial insemination.
- **Legislative Role in Reproductive Technology.** It is not the role of the judiciary to inhibit the use of reproductive technology when the Legislature has not done so.

**Reasoning**
- The probate court had jurisdiction over the sperm because the decedent had an ownership interest in it, making it "property" of his estate under Probate Code section 62.
    - The court rejects the argument that *Moore v. Regents of University of California* means the decedent had no ownership interest, as that would remove the sperm from probate jurisdiction.
    - Human biological materials, including sperm, are *sui generis* and not simply abandoned to general personal property law, but they do carry an interest in the nature of ownership for decision-making.
- The trial court's order to destroy the sperm was an abuse of discretion because it was not based on the will, settlement agreements, or any valid public policy.
    - The will clearly expressed the decedent's intent for Hecht to receive the sperm.
    - The court did not adjudicate the validity of the will or settlement agreements, nor did it hold any evidentiary hearing on claims of unsound mind or undue influence.
    - The court rejects the argument that public policy forbids artificial insemination of an unmarried woman.
        - Civil Code section 7005, subdivision (b) was amended to remove the word "married," indicating legislative intent to allow unmarried women to use artificial insemination.
        - There is no state policy preventing the formation of single-parent families.
    - The court rejects the argument that public policy forbids postmortem artificial insemination.
        - There is no California statute or public policy that justifies infringing on gamete-providers' decisional authority regarding postmortem use of sperm.
        - Concerns about psychological or financial burdens on existing family members or society are speculative and not supported by legal authority.
        - The Uniform Status of Children of Assisted Conception Act suggests that posthumously conceived children generally do not inherit from the deceased parent unless explicitly provided for in a will, which addresses some concerns.
- The court emphasizes that it is not adjudicating the validity of the will, sperm bank contract, or settlement agreements, nor is it ordering distribution of the sperm to Hecht, as those issues remain to be resolved.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the evolving concept of "property" in the context of human biological materials, particularly reproductive cells. It distinguishes itself from *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* by finding a sufficient "ownership interest" in sperm for probate jurisdiction, whereas *Moore* found no conversion claim for excised cells used in research. It highlights that while not traditional property, gametes are treated with "special respect" due to their potential for human life. The case also touches on the limits of judicial intervention in reproductive choices when the legislature has not acted.
2. This case leaves open the question of what would happen if the decedent's intent regarding the sperm was unclear or contested, or if there were no will. What if the sperm bank contract had explicitly stated that sperm would be destroyed upon death without further instructions?

**Takeaway:** Cryogenically preserved sperm is considered "property" for probate purposes, and courts generally will not interfere with a decedent's expressed intent for its postmortem use for procreation unless there is a clear, established public policy against it.
