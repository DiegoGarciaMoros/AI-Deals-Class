## Hecht v. Superior Court (Court of Appeal of the State of California, 1993)

**Facts**
- In October 1991, William E. Kane (decedent) deposited 15 vials of his sperm at California Cryobank, Inc.
- On September 24, 1991, decedent signed a "Specimen Storage Agreement" with the sperm bank, instructing the sperm bank to continue to store or release the specimens to the executor of his estate upon his death.
- The agreement also included an "Authorization to Release Specimens" stating, "I, William Everett Kane, . . . authorize the [sperm bank] to release my semen specimens (vials) to Deborah Ellen Hecht."
- On September 27, 1991, decedent executed a will, naming Deborah E. Hecht (petitioner) as executor and bequeathing to her "all right, title, and interest that I may have in any specimens of my sperm stored with any sperm bank or similar facility for storage."
- The will also included a "Statement of Wishes" expressing his intention for Hecht to use his sperm to have a child after his death.
- On October 21, 1991, decedent wrote a letter to his children, William E. Kane, Jr., and Katharine E. Kane (real parties in interest), stating his intention for Hecht to have a child by him after his death using his frozen sperm samples.
- On October 30, 1991, decedent committed suicide.
- On November 18, 1991, Robert L. Greene was appointed special administrator of decedent's estate.
- On December 3, 1991, decedent's children filed will contests.
- On December 3, 1991, a "tentative agreement" (First Settlement Agreement) was placed on the record to resolve estate matters, but it did not explicitly mention the sperm.
- In October 1992, the administrator filed a petition for approval of a "Second Settlement Agreement" which would assign the estate's interest in the sperm to Hecht, subject to court approval.
- On November 5, 1992, the administrator filed a "Petition for Alternative Relief" seeking instructions to destroy the sperm or for its distribution.
- Decedent's children requested the sperm be destroyed on public policy grounds.
- Hecht argued the sperm was a gift to her and should be distributed to her.
- On December 9, 1992, the trial court ordered the sperm destroyed.
- On January 4, 1993, the trial court entered an order directing the administrator to destroy the sperm.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court ordered the destruction of the decedent's sperm.
- Hecht filed a petition for peremptory writ of mandate/prohibition to vacate the trial court's order.
- The Court of Appeal issued an order to show cause and stayed the execution of the trial court's order.

**Issue**
Did the trial court abuse its discretion by ordering the destruction of a deceased man's sperm, which he had bequeathed to his girlfriend for posthumous artificial insemination, based on public policy arguments against artificial insemination of unmarried women or posthumous conception?

**Holding**
Petitioner (Hecht) wins. The trial court abused its discretion in ordering the destruction of the decedent's sperm. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Probate Court Jurisdiction.** The power of the probate court extends only to the property of the decedent.
- **Right of Inheritance.** The right of inheritance and testamentary disposition is entirely within the control of the state Legislature.
- **Probate Court Distribution.** Except as expressly provided by law, the probate court has no jurisdiction to distribute a decedent’s estate except to heirs, devisees, or legatees.
- **Fiduciary Duty of Executor.** An executor or administrator occupies a fiduciary relationship to all parties having an interest in the estate and has a duty to protect their legal rights.
- **Probate Code Section 62.** "Property" includes "anything that may be the subject of ownership and includes both real and personal property and any interest therein."
- **Nature of Rights in Human Biological Materials.** Human biological materials are objects *sui generis*, regulated by specialized statutes to achieve policy goals rather than abandoned to the general law of personal property.
- **Decision-making Authority over Gametes.** Gamete providers have primary decision-making authority regarding preembryos in the absence of specific legislation.
- **California Civil Code Section 7005(b).** This section, part of the Uniform Parentage Act, applies to all women, married or not, allowing them to obtain semen for artificial insemination without fear of the donor claiming paternity, provided the semen is provided to a licensed physician.
- **Public Policy on Single-Parent Families.** The state does not have a policy of preventing the formation of single-parent families.
- **Public Policy on Posthumous Conception.** There is no state interest sufficient to justify infringing on gamete-providers’ decisional authority regarding posthumous conception.
- **Uniform Status of Children of Assisted Conception Act (Section 4(b)).** An individual who dies before implantation of an embryo, or before a child is conceived other than through sexual intercourse, using the individual’s egg or sperm, is not a parent of the resulting child, primarily to avoid intestate succession problems.
- **Probate Code Section 6407.** Relatives of the decedent conceived before the decedent’s death, but born thereafter, inherit as if they had been born in the lifetime of the decedent.
- **Probate Code Section 6408.** For purposes of intestate succession, this section provides rules for determining parent-child relationships and inheritance rights.
- **Judicial Role in Reproductive Technology.** It is not the role of the judiciary to inhibit the use of reproductive technology when the Legislature has not seen fit to do so, as such efforts raise serious questions regarding fundamental rights of procreation and privacy.

**Reasoning**
- The decedent's interest in his frozen sperm vials, while not strictly personal property, occupies an "interim category" entitling them to special respect due to their potential for human life.
- The decedent had an interest in his sperm, in the nature of ownership, to the extent that he had decision-making authority over its use for reproduction, which constitutes "property" under Probate Code section 62.
- The trial court's order to destroy the sperm could not be upheld by applying the will, as the will evidenced the decedent's intent for Hecht to receive the sperm for posthumous artificial insemination.
- The trial court's order could not be supported by the settlement agreements, as neither agreement mandated destruction of the sperm, and the enforceability of the Second Settlement Agreement was still at issue.
- Public policy does not prohibit the artificial insemination of an unmarried woman in California, as indicated by the Legislature's exclusion of the word "married" from Civil Code section 7005(b).
- There is no established state policy against posthumous conception or against the formation of single-parent families.
- The court found no authority for the trial court to make a value judgment on whether it is better for a potential child not to be born, assuming both gamete providers wish to conceive.
- The court rejected arguments that posthumous conception would create psychological or financial burdens on the decedent's existing children or society, noting that existing statutes (Probate Code sections 6407 and 6408) and potential settlement agreements address inheritance and support issues.
- The court emphasized that the judiciary should not inhibit reproductive technology when the Legislature has not done so, given the fundamental nature of procreation and privacy rights.
- The court did not adjudicate the validity of the will, sperm bank contract, or settlement agreements, or issues of decedent's competence or undue influence, as these were not before it.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is significant for establishing that cryogenically preserved sperm can be considered "property" within the meaning of probate law, even if it is *sui generis* and not subject to all general personal property rules. It connects to **Moore v. Regents of the University of California** by distinguishing the property interest in excised cells (where Moore had no ownership claim) from the decision-making authority over gametes intended for reproduction. The court here emphasizes the "potential for human life" as a key differentiator. It also touches on the tension between individual procreative rights and societal concerns, similar to discussions in cases like **Hecht v. Superior Court** itself regarding the disposition of genetic material.
2. The court explicitly states it is not adjudicating the validity of the will or the enforceability of the settlement agreements. This leaves open the question of whether Hecht will ultimately receive the sperm, even if the destruction order is vacated. What if the will is found invalid, or the settlement agreement that gives her the sperm is not enforced?

**Takeaway:** Human gametes intended for reproduction, even after the donor's death, constitute a unique form of "property" subject to the donor's decision-making authority, and public policy does not prohibit posthumous artificial insemination.
