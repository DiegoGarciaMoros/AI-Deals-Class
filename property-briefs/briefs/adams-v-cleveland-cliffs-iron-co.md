## Adams v. Cleveland-Cliffs Iron Co. (Michigan Court of Appeals, 1999)

**Facts**
- The Empire Mine, operated by Cleveland-Cliffs Iron Company and Empire Mining Partnership, engaged in blasting, extracting, and processing iron ore 24 hours a day, year-round.
- Plaintiffs, 55 individuals living near the mine in Palmer, complained of dust, noise, and vibrations emanating from the mine.
- Plaintiffs alleged the blasting caused tremors, and dust accumulated inside and outside their homes, leading to masonry cracks, window replacement, plumbing and sewer leaks, carpet and drape replacement, and increased cleaning and repainting.
- Many plaintiffs testified that noise and vibrations caused shock, nervousness, and sleeplessness.
- Several plaintiffs claimed these conditions diminished their home values, making some unmarketable.
- Emissions from the mine consistently remained within applicable air-quality standards, but the amount of particulate matter accumulating over Palmer was four times greater than in surrounding communities.
- Plaintiffs sued for damages under theories of trespass and nuisance.
- The trial court instructed the jury that an unauthorized intrusion onto land, including emissions, dust, vibration, or noise, could constitute a trespass.
- The jury found for 52 plaintiffs on the trespass claim, awarding $599,199, but could not reach a verdict on the nuisance claim.
- The trial court denied the defendants' motions for a new trial or judgment notwithstanding the verdict.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs sued defendants in Marquette Circuit Court for trespass and nuisance.
- The jury found for 52 plaintiffs on the trespass claim and awarded damages; it was hung on the nuisance claim.
- The trial court denied defendants' post-trial motions.
- Defendants appealed to the Michigan Court of Appeals.

**Issue**
Can a landowner recover for trespass in Michigan for intrusions of airborne particulate, noise, or vibrations?

**Holding**
Defendant (Cleveland-Cliffs Iron Co.) wins. Recovery for trespass to land in Michigan requires proof of an unauthorized direct or immediate intrusion of a physical, tangible object. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Trespass.** An invasion of a landholder’s interest in the exclusive possession of the land.
- **Nuisance.** An interference with a landholder’s use and enjoyment of the land.
- **Traditional Trespass Elements.** Recovery for trespass requires an unauthorized direct or immediate intrusion of a physical, tangible object onto land over which a plaintiff has a right of exclusive control.
- **Trespass Damages.** Once a trespass is proved, a plaintiff is entitled to at least nominal damages, even without proof of other injury.
- **Nuisance Damages.** Recovery for nuisance traditionally requires proof of actual and substantial injury and balancing the disturbance against the social utility of its cause.
- **Rejected: Modern Trespass View.** The court rejects the view that trespass can include indirect, intangible invasions that interfere with exclusive possessory interests, often requiring actual and substantial damages and balancing social utility.
- **Tangible Object Requirement.** Noise, vibrations, and dust are generally considered intangible and thus not actionable in trespass. Dust particles do not normally occupy land in a meaningful sense.
- **Direct Intrusion Requirement.** A direct or immediate invasion for trespass is one accomplished by any means the offender knew or reasonably should have known would result in the physical invasion of the plaintiff’s land.

**Reasoning**
- The court aims to preserve the separate identities of trespass and nuisance.
- **Trespass** protects the right to exclusive possession, while **nuisance** protects the right to use and enjoyment.
- The traditional view of trespass required a direct or immediate invasion by a physical, tangible object.
- Intangible irritants like dust, smoke, noise, and vibrations were historically addressed under nuisance law.
- The "modern view of trespass," which allows recovery for intangible intrusions and requires actual damages, effectively blurs the line between trespass and nuisance.
- This blurring undermines the traditional principle of presumed damages in trespass, which is essential to protecting the right to exclude.
- Requiring actual and substantial damages and balancing them against social utility is appropriate for nuisance, not trespass.
- The court finds no Michigan precedent that unambiguously eliminates the traditional distinction between trespass and nuisance.
- The court rejects the argument that *Adkins v. Thomas Solvent Co.* implicitly adopted the modern view of trespass, noting that *Adkins* was a nuisance case and its reference to modern trespass was dicta to illustrate a point about nuisance.
- Noise and vibrations are clearly not tangible objects and cannot give rise to a trespass action.
- Dust, while physically composed of particles, does not normally present itself as a significant physical intrusion and is generally considered intangible for trespass purposes.
- The trial court's instruction, allowing trespass for intangible irritants and requiring proof of damages, erroneously conflated trespass with nuisance.
- The proper remedy for noise, vibrations, or ambient dust that interferes with use and enjoyment is an action for nuisance, requiring proof of significant harm from unreasonable interference.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is crucial for understanding the distinction between **trespass** and **nuisance**, particularly regarding intangible invasions. It contrasts with cases like *Jost v. Dairyland Power Cooperative* and *Campbell v. Seaman*, which dealt with similar types of invasions but often under nuisance theories or with different jurisdictional approaches to the trespass/nuisance boundary. It reinforces the idea that the "bundle of sticks" includes distinct rights of exclusion (trespass) and use/enjoyment (nuisance).
