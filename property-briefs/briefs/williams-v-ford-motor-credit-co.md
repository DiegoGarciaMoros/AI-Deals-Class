## Williams v. Ford Motor Credit Co. (United States Court of Appeals, Eighth Circuit, 1982)

**Facts**
- In July 1975, David Williams purchased a Ford Mustang, and the title was in the names of both David and Cathy Williams.
- The car was financed through a Ford dealer, who assigned the paper to Ford Motor Credit Company (FMCC).
- In 1977, Cathy and David Williams divorced, and the divorce court granted Cathy title to the car and required David to continue payments to FMCC for 18 months.
- David defaulted on payments and signed a voluntary repossession authorization for FMCC.
- Cathy Williams was informed of the delinquency.
- FMCC directed S & S Recovery, Inc. (S & S) to repossess the automobile.
- On December 1, 1977, at approximately 4:30 a.m., Cathy Williams was awakened by a noise and saw a wrecker truck hooking up to her car.
- She went outside, told the men the car was hers, and asked what they were doing.
- One man, Don Sappington of S & S, informed her he was repossessing the vehicle for FMCC.
- Williams explained she was trying to bring payments up to date and that the car contained personal items.
- Sappington retrieved the personal items from the car and handed them to her.
- Williams made no further complaint, and Sappington drove off with the car.
- Williams testified that Sappington was polite and did not threaten her or cause her to fear physical harm.
- The car was parked in an unenclosed driveway.
- Williams called the police and reported her car stolen.

**Procedural History**
- Cathy Williams sued FMCC for conversion.
- A jury awarded Williams $5,000 in damages.
- FMCC moved for judgment notwithstanding the verdict.
- The district court initially ordered a nonsuit without prejudice.
- The Eighth Circuit reversed and remanded, directing the district court to rule on FMCC's motion.
- The district court entered judgment notwithstanding the verdict for FMCC.
- Williams appealed this judgment.

**Issue**
Did the repossession of Cathy Williams' car constitute a breach of the peace under Arkansas law, thereby making it an unlawful conversion?

**Holding**
Defendant (FMCC) wins. The repossession was a legal repossession under Arkansas law because there was no breach of the peace. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Self-help repossession.** Unless otherwise agreed, a secured party has on default the right to take possession of the collateral.
- **Breach of the peace.** In taking possession, a secured party may proceed without judicial process if this can be done without breach of the peace.
- **Breach of the peace (Arkansas pre-Code cases).** A finding of conversion only occurred where force, or threats of force, or risk of invoking violence, accompanied the repossession.
- **Rejected:** A breach of the peace occurs if the debtor does not give permission but also does not object. *(The court implies this rule was rejected by earlier cases where no objection meant no breach.)*

**Reasoning**
- The Uniform Commercial Code (UCC) allows self-help repossession without judicial process if it can be done "without breach of the peace."
- Arkansas pre-Code cases, which inform the interpretation of "breach of the peace," required force, threats of force, or a risk of invoking violence for a conversion finding.
- Williams admitted that the repossessors were polite, complied with her requests, and did not perform any oppressive or threatening acts.
- There was no evidence that Williams would have been forced to resort to physical violence to stop the repossession.
- Williams did not object to the taking after Sappington retrieved her personal items.
- The repossession was accomplished without any incident that might tend to provoke violence.
- While the night-time repossession was not commendable, it was legal under Arkansas law given the lack of objection or confrontation that risked violence.

**Dissent (Heaney, Circuit Judge)**
- The jury's finding of a breach of the peace by creating a "risk of invoking violence" was reasonable and should not have been overturned.
- Williams did everything she could to stop the repossession short of physical force, and the confrontation did not result in violence only because she was powerless.
- The commotion awakened neighbors, indicating a disturbance.
- The policy of the UCC restriction on self-help is to discourage extrajudicial acts fraught with the likelihood of violence.
- When an invasion is detected and a confrontation ensues, the repossessor should be under a duty to retreat and turn to judicial process.
- The majority's rule invites tragic consequences by allowing repossession to proceed after confrontation unless violence actually results.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the limits of self-help remedies and the interpretation of "breach of the peace" in the context of secured transactions, connecting to the broader theme of property rights and their enforcement. It contrasts with cases like *Berg v. Wiley*, where a landlord's self-help repossession was deemed a breach of the peace due to the tenant's clear objection and the landlord's use of force (changing locks). Here, the court finds no breach because the debtor did not actively object or resist, distinguishing it from situations where confrontation escalates.
2. The dissent raises an important tension: should the law require a repossessor to retreat upon any confrontation, or only when violence is imminent? The majority's ruling seems to require a higher threshold of actual or threatened violence.

**Takeaway:** A self-help repossession does not constitute a breach of the peace if the debtor does not actively object or resist, and no force or threats of force are used by the repossessor.
