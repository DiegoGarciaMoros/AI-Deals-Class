## Williams v. Ford Motor Credit Co. (United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, 1982)

**Facts**
- In July 1975, David Williams purchased a Ford Mustang, and the title was in the names of both David and Cathy Williams.
- The car was financed through a Ford dealer, who assigned the paper to Ford Motor Credit Company (FMCC).
- In 1977, Cathy and David Williams divorced, and Cathy was granted title to the car, with David ordered to continue payments to FMCC.
- David defaulted on the payments and signed a voluntary repossession authorization for FMCC.
- Cathy Williams was informed of the delinquency and stated she was trying to get David to make payments.
- On December 1, 1977, at approximately 4:30 a.m., Cathy Williams was awakened by a noise and saw a wrecker truck hooking up to her car.
- She went outside, hollered at the men, and the truck stopped.
- She told them the car was hers and asked what they were doing.
- One man, Don Sappington, identified himself as repossessing the vehicle for FMCC.
- Williams explained she was trying to bring payments up to date and stated the car contained personal items.
- Sappington retrieved the personal items from the car and handed them to her.
- Williams made no further complaint, and Sappington drove off with the car.
- Williams testified that Sappington was polite and did not threaten her or cause her to fear physical harm.
- The car was parked in an unenclosed driveway.
- Williams called the police and reported her car stolen.

**Procedural History**
- Cathy Williams sued FMCC for conversion.
- A jury awarded Williams $5,000 in damages.
- FMCC moved for judgment notwithstanding the verdict (JNOV).
- The district court initially ordered a nonsuit without prejudice for Williams to refile in state court.
- On FMCC's appeal, the Eighth Circuit reversed and remanded with directions to rule on the JNOV motion.
- The district court entered JNOV for FMCC.
- Williams appealed the JNOV.

**Issue**
Can a secured party repossess a vehicle without judicial process when the debtor is present and objects, but does not physically resist, without breaching the peace?

**Holding**
Defendant (FMCC) wins. A repossession does not breach the peace if the debtor does not object to the taking or if the confrontation does not involve force, threats of force, or risk of invoking violence. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **UCC § 9-503 (now § 9-609).** Unless otherwise agreed, a secured party has on default the right to take possession of the collateral, and in taking possession, a secured party may proceed without judicial process if this can be done without breach of the peace.
- **Breach of the Peace (Arkansas Pre-Code Cases).** A finding of conversion based on repossession occurred only where force, or threats of force, or risk of invoking violence, accompanied the repossession.
- **Rejected: Breach of the Peace (Dissent).** A breach of the peace occurs when a night raid is detected and a confrontation ensues, requiring the repossessor to retreat and turn to judicial process.

**Reasoning**
- The Arkansas Supreme Court's interpretation of "breach of the peace" in pre-Code cases, which are still relevant, requires force, threats of force, or risk of invoking violence.
- In *Manhattan Credit Co. v. Brewer*, a breach of peace occurred because the debtor and her husband confronted the agent and clearly objected.
- In *Rutledge v. Universal C.I.T. Credit Corp.*, no breach of peace occurred when the debtor did not give permission but also did not object.
- Williams did not object to the taking of the car after Sappington retrieved her personal items.
- Williams herself testified that the repossessors were polite and did not make any threats or cause her to fear physical harm.
- The repossession was accomplished without any incident that might tend to provoke violence.
- While the night-time taking is not commendable, the facts, even viewed most favorably to Williams, show a legal repossession under Arkansas law.

**Dissent (HEANEY, Circuit Judge)**
- The only issue is whether the repossession created a "risk of invoking violence."
- The jury found that it did, and this was a reasonable determination that should not be overturned.
- Williams did everything she could to stop them short of physical force, and the confrontation did not result in violence only because she did not take such steps and was powerless.
- The policy of UCC § 9-503 is to discourage extrajudicial acts fraught with the likelihood of violence.
- A confrontation in the dead of night, detected by the debtor, creates a risk of violence.
- When an invasion is detected and a confrontation ensues, the repossessor should be under a duty to retreat and turn to judicial process.
- The majority's rule encourages violence by allowing repossession to proceed after confrontation unless violence actually results.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the limits of **self-help repossession** under the UCC and the definition of **breach of the peace**. It can be compared to *Berg v. Wiley*, where a landlord's self-help eviction was found to be a breach of the peace due to the tenant's presence and objection, even without physical violence. Here, the court emphasizes the lack of explicit objection or threat of violence from the debtor.
2. The dissent raises a critical point about the "risk of invoking violence" versus actual violence. The majority seems to require a higher threshold for "breach of the peace" than the dissent, focusing on the debtor's actions (or inactions) rather than the inherent tension of a confrontation during a repossession. This leaves open the question of how much objection or resistance is required from a debtor to constitute a "breach of the peace" and force the creditor to resort to judicial process.

**Takeaway:** A secured creditor's self-help repossession is generally permissible under the UCC if the debtor does not physically resist or explicitly object to the taking, even if the repossession occurs at an inconvenient time and causes distress.
