## Neponsit Property Owners' Association v. Emigrant Industrial Savings Bank (New York Court of Appeals, 1938)

**Facts**
- In January 1911, Neponsit Realty Company (grantor) filed a map for a residential development in Queens County.
- In 1917, Neponsit Realty Company conveyed a lot to Robert Oldner Deyer and his wife (predecessors in title to defendant).
- The deed contained a covenant requiring an annual charge, not exceeding $4 per lot, for the maintenance of roads, paths, parks, beach, sewers, and other public purposes.
- The covenant stated that the charge would become a lien on the land and could be collected by a Property Owners' Association, which might be organized later.
- The covenant also stated it would "run with the land" until January 31, 1940.
- Every subsequent deed in the chain of title, including the referee's deed to the defendant, conveyed the property subject to the covenants and restrictions of former deeds of record.
- The Neponsit Property Owners' Association, Inc. (plaintiff) was organized to receive and expend these sums for the benefit of property owners.
- The defendant, Emigrant Industrial Savings Bank, purchased the land at a judicial sale.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiff (Neponsit Property Owners' Association) brought an action to foreclose a lien on the defendant's land, based on the covenant.
- Defendant moved for judgment on the pleadings, dismissing the complaint.
- Plaintiff moved to dismiss defendant's counterclaim and strike out affirmative defenses.
- The Special Term granted plaintiff's motion and denied defendant's motion.
- The Appellate Division unanimously affirmed the Special Term's order and granted leave to appeal to the Court of Appeals upon certified questions.

**Issue**
Can a property owners' association enforce an affirmative covenant to pay money for the maintenance of common areas against a subsequent purchaser, even if the association does not own property benefiting from the covenant and there is no strict privity of estate?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Neponsit Property Owners' Association) wins. The covenant is enforceable against the defendant because it "touches and concerns" the land in substance, and the corporate plaintiff, acting as a representative of the property owners, satisfies the requirement of privity of estate in substance. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Requirements for a Real Covenant to Run with the Land.** A covenant will run with the land and be enforceable against a subsequent purchaser if: (1) grantor and grantee intended it to run with the land; (2) it "touches" or "concerns" the land; and (3) there is "privity of estate" between the promisee and promisor.
- **"Touching and Concerning" the Land.** This test is vague and requires the court to determine, in its best judgment, whether the covenant affects the legal relations (advantages and burdens) of the parties as owners of particular parcels of land, rather than merely as members of the community.
- **Affirmative Covenants.** While traditionally, a covenant to do an affirmative act (as opposed to a negative restriction) does not run with the land, exceptions exist, especially where the covenant to pay money is for something done in connection with the promisor's land and substantially alters the legal rights flowing from ownership.
- **Privity of Estate.** This requirement, in the context of covenants running with the land, can be satisfied in substance, even if not in technical form, when a corporate entity acts as a representative or agent for the property owners for whose benefit the covenant was created.
- **Rejected: Strict Privity of Estate for Corporate Associations.** The court rejects a blind adherence to the ancient formula of privity of estate when a corporate property owners' association is formed as a medium for the enjoyment of common rights by property owners.

**Reasoning**
- The covenant clearly shows the grantor's intent for it to run with the land and be enforceable by a property owners' association.
- The covenant "touches and concerns" the land because it requires payment for the maintenance of common areas (roads, parks, etc.) that benefit the defendant's property and other properties in the development.
    - The payment is for something done in connection with the land, not disconnected from its use.
    - It imposes a burden on the land that increases the value of the land by ensuring the maintenance of appurtenant easements and rights of common enjoyment.
    - To distinguish this covenant based on it being an affirmative act to pay money, rather than a restriction on use, would be to prioritize form over substance.
    - The court looks to the "substantial effect" of the covenant on the legal rights of the parties as landowners, rather than technical distinctions.
- While the plaintiff association does not own property in the tract, it was formed as a convenient instrument for the property owners to advance their common interests.
    - Ignoring the corporate form, the association acts as an agent or representative of the property owners.
    - To deny enforcement based on a lack of technical privity of estate would be "blind adherence to an ancient formula" that does not fit the modern context of property owners' associations.
    - In substance, there is privity of estate between the plaintiff (representing the property owners) and the defendant.
- The defense that the lien is unenforceable under Real Property Law sections 242 and 259 is insufficient.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is crucial for understanding the evolution of **covenants running with the land**, particularly the "touch and concern" and "privity of estate" requirements. It demonstrates a move away from rigid, formalistic interpretations towards a more flexible, substantive approach, especially in the context of modern residential developments and property owners' associations. It connects to cases like *Tulk v. Moxhay* by showing how courts adapt equitable principles to enforce restrictions, even if not strictly meeting common law requirements. It also contrasts with older, more rigid applications of the "affirmative covenant" rule, as seen in *Miller v. Clary*, by finding an exception based on the covenant's practical effect.
2. The court explicitly states that the "touch and concern" test is "too vague to be of much assistance." This raises the question of how much discretion courts have in applying this test and whether a more predictable standard is desirable.

**Takeaway:** Courts may interpret traditional requirements for covenants running with the land flexibly to uphold the substance and intent of agreements that benefit a community, especially in modern residential developments.
