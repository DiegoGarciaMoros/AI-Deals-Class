## Jost v. Dairyland Power Cooperative (Wisconsin Supreme Court, 1969)

**Facts**
- Dairyland Power Cooperative operated a power plant that emitted sulfur fumes into the atmosphere.
- The Josts and other neighboring farmers complained about the sulfur fumes for several years.
- The sulfur fumes caused damage to the farmers' alfalfa crops and other vegetation.
- The farmers sued Dairyland Power Cooperative for damages.

**Procedural History**
- Trial court found Dairyland Power Cooperative liable for nuisance and awarded damages for crop loss and diminution in market value for one plaintiff.
- The trial judge changed the jury's "no" answer to the "substantial damage" question to "yes."
- Dairyland Power Cooperative appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a defendant in a nuisance action for damages avoid liability by showing due care or that the social utility of its conduct outweighs the harm to the plaintiffs?

**Holding**
- Plaintiff farmers win. A defendant in a nuisance action for damages cannot avoid liability by showing due care or that the social utility of its conduct outweighs the harm to the plaintiffs. Affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded for a new trial on the issue of diminution of market value for all plaintiffs.

**Rules**
- **Nuisance.** A nuisance is a field of tort liability that refers to the interests invaded and the harm inflicted, rather than a particular type of tortious conduct.
- **Negligence and Nuisance.** Negligence is merely one type of conduct that may give rise to a nuisance.
- **Due Care in Nuisance Actions.** Freedom from negligence is no defense if the consequences of continued conduct nevertheless cause substantial injury to a claimant.
- **Balancing Utility vs. Harm in Nuisance Damages.** In a suit for damages for nuisance, the doctrine of comparative injury (balancing the utility of the offending conduct against the gravity of the injury inflicted) is not applied in Wisconsin.
- **Substantial Injury.** Substantial injury is defined as tangible injury or a discomfort perceptible to the senses of ordinary people.
- **Permanent Nuisance Damages.** When a nuisance is continuing and permanent, damages for diminution in market value are appropriate, and future recovery for specific items of damage on a year-by-year basis is generally not permitted unless the level of nuisance increases.

**Reasoning**
- The case was properly tried on a theory of nuisance, not negligence, as made clear by plaintiffs' counsel from the outset.
- The trial judge correctly changed the jury's answer regarding substantial damage because the evidence showed tangible injury to property (alfalfa crops and vegetation) that was more than nominal.
- The court properly excluded evidence of Dairyland's due care because **freedom from negligence is not a defense to a nuisance action** where substantial injury occurs.
- The court properly excluded evidence of the social and economic utility of the power plant because **Wisconsin law does not permit balancing the utility of the offending conduct against the gravity of the injury in damage suits for nuisance**.
    - To allow a socially useful enterprise to injure others without compensation would be a taking of property without due process.
    - The rule from *Pennoyer v. Allen* (1883) remains applicable: the interruption of enjoyment and destruction of comfort furnishes the ground of action, and it is no defense that the business was conducted reasonably.
- The court erred in finding no diminution in market value for all plaintiffs, as the continuing nature of the nuisance and the undisputed damage to vegetation indicated a permanent injury.
    - Damages for a permanent nuisance should be reflected in a diminution of market value.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the distinction between nuisance and negligence, and particularly for the rule that **due care is not a defense to a nuisance claim for damages**. It connects to *Jost v. Dairyland Power Cooperative* by reinforcing the idea that property owners have a right to be free from substantial interference, even if the interfering activity is otherwise lawful and conducted with care. It also contrasts with cases where balancing of equities might be considered, such as in injunction requests (though the court here explicitly states that issue is not before them).
2. What if the power plant was the only source of electricity for a large region, and shutting it down would cause widespread hardship? Would the court's reasoning still hold for an injunction request, or would the "comparative injury" doctrine apply then?

**Takeaway:** In Wisconsin, a defendant cannot escape liability for damages caused by a nuisance by arguing they used due care or that their activity has greater social utility than the harm it causes.
