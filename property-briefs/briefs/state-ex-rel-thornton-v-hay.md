## State ex rel. Thornton v. Hay (Oregon Supreme Court, 1969)

**Facts**
- William and Georgianna Hay owned a tourist facility at Cannon Beach, Oregon, which included dry-sand area between the sixteen-foot elevation contour line and the ordinary high-tide line of the Pacific Ocean.
- The public had used the dry-sand area for recreational purposes, such as picnics, gathering wood, and building fires, since the beginning of Oregon's political history.
- State and local officers policed the dry sand, and municipal sanitary crews maintained the area.
- The dry-sand area is unstable, unsafe during winter storms, and generally unfit for permanent structures.
- In 1967, public debate and legislative activity occurred regarding public and private interests in the dry-sand area.
- The Hays sought to construct fences or other improvements in the dry-sand area of their property.

**Procedural History**
- The State of Oregon, ex rel. Thornton, brought an action against the Hays to enjoin them from constructing fences or improvements.
- The trial court found in favor of the state, ruling that the public had acquired an easement for recreational purposes in the dry-sand area.
- The Hays appealed.
- The Oregon Supreme Court affirmed the trial court's decree.

**Issue**
Can the state prevent landowners from enclosing the dry-sand area of their ocean-front property when the public has historically used it for recreation?

**Holding**
The State wins. The public has a right to use the dry-sand area of ocean-front property for recreational purposes based on the doctrine of custom. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Public Easement by Custom.** A public right to use private land can be established by long-standing custom if the custom is ancient, exercised without interruption, peaceable and free from dispute, reasonable, certain, obligatory, and not repugnant to other customs or law.
- **Rejected: Implied Dedication.** Dedication, whether express or implied, rests upon an intent to dedicate.
- **Rejected: Prescriptive Easement (for this case).** While the public can acquire prescriptive easements in private land in Oregon, the doctrine of custom provides a better legal basis for affirming the decree in this case because it applies to a larger region and the unique nature of the land.

**Reasoning**
- The dry-sand area has been enjoyed by the general public as a recreational adjunct to the wet-sand area since the beginning of Oregon's political history.
- The public's use of the dry-sand area meets all seven requisites of a custom as outlined by Blackstone:
    - **Ancient:** The public has freely exercised the right to use the dry-sand area up and down the Oregon coast for recreational purposes for as long as there has been an institutionalized system of land tenure in Oregon.
    - **Without interruption:** The public's use and enjoyment of the dry-sand area has never been interrupted by private landowners.
    - **Peaceable and free from dispute:** This is satisfied by the evidence relating to the lack of interruption.
    - **Reasonable:** The public has always used the land in a manner appropriate to the land and community usages.
    - **Certain:** The visible boundaries of the dry-sand area and the character of the land limit its use to recreational purposes connected with the foreshore.
    - **Obligatory:** The dry-sand area has been used as of right, uniformly with similarly situated lands, and the public's use has never been questioned by upland owners as long as they remained on the dry sand.
    - **Not repugnant:** The custom violates no law and is not inconsistent with other customs.
- The custom is so notorious that notice of it must be presumed for persons buying land along the shore.
- The rule based on custom confirms a public right and takes nothing from landowners that they had a legitimate reason to regard as exclusively theirs.
- *(dicta)* The court declined to rule on the constitutionality of ORS 390.640 as a zoning regulation if the public had not acquired an easement.
- *(dicta)* The court left open the effect of consent on the doctrine of prescription.

**Concurrence (Denecke, J.)**
- Disagrees with basing the decision on the English doctrine of "customary rights" because the facts cannot be fitted into that ancient doctrine.
- Believes "custom" is an important ingredient, but only as an analogy.
- Bases the public's right on: (1) long usage by the public of the dry sands area; (2) a universal and long-held belief by the public in their right to such use; (3) long and universal acquiescence by upland owners; and (4) the extreme desirability to the public of the right to use the dry sands.
- Argues this is a new concept for dry sands but not for other public usages, citing cases about public rights to boat on lakes and fish in navigable rivers, which are based on the "jus publicum" doctrine.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes that public rights can arise from long-standing custom, even over privately owned land, which is a significant limitation on the traditional bundle of sticks associated with fee simple ownership. It connects to the idea of **public rights** and **custom** as sources of property law, similar to how **Ghen v. Rich** considered custom in establishing property rights in whales. It also touches on the tension between private property rights and public access, a theme seen in cases like **Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois** regarding the public trust doctrine.
2. The concurrence's argument for a broader "public interest" doctrine, rather than strict adherence to the ancient doctrine of custom, raises the question of how far courts should go in recognizing public rights based on evolving societal needs and expectations, especially when it impacts established private property interests.

**Takeaway:** Long-standing public custom can create an easement for public recreational use over privately owned dry-sand beach areas, limiting the landowner's right to exclude.
