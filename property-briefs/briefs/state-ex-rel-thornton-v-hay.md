## State ex rel. Thornton v. Hay (Oregon Supreme Court, 1969)

**Facts**
- William and Georgianna Hay (defendants) owned a tourist facility at Cannon Beach, Oregon.
- Their property included a "dry-sand area" between the sixteen-foot elevation contour line and the ordinary high-tide line of the Pacific Ocean.
- The public had used this dry-sand area for recreational purposes, such as picnics, gathering wood, and building fires, since the beginning of Oregon's political history.
- State and local officers policed the dry sand, and municipal sanitary crews maintained it.
- The dry-sand area is unstable, unsafe during winter storms, and generally unfit for permanent structures.
- In 1967, the Oregon legislature passed ORS 390.610, declaring a public policy to preserve public use of the seashore and ocean beaches.
- The Hays sought to construct fences or other improvements in the dry-sand area of their property.
- The State of Oregon, through Robert Y. Thornton (plaintiff), sought to enjoin the Hays from constructing these improvements.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court found in favor of the state, ruling that the public had acquired an easement for recreational purposes in the dry-sand area.
- The Hays appealed.
- The Oregon Supreme Court affirmed the trial court's decree.

**Issue**
Can the state prevent landowners from enclosing the dry-sand area of their ocean-front property if the public has historically used the area for recreation?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (State) wins. The public has a right to use the dry-sand area of ocean beaches for recreational purposes based on the **doctrine of custom**. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Public Easement by Custom.** A public right to use land can be established by long-standing custom if the custom is ancient, exercised without interruption, peaceable and free from dispute, reasonable, certain, obligatory, and not repugnant to other laws or customs.
- Rejected: **Implied Dedication.** This doctrine requires an intent to dedicate, which was not present here.
- Rejected: **Prescriptive Easement for the Public.** While the public can acquire prescriptive easements in Oregon, the court found a "better legal basis" in custom for this case, which applies more broadly than tract-by-tract prescription.

**Reasoning**
- The dry-sand area has been used by the general public for recreational purposes since the beginning of Oregon's political history, satisfying the "ancient" requirement of custom.
- The public's use of the dry-sand area has been continuous and uninterrupted by private landowners.
- The use has been peaceable and free from dispute, with municipal officers intervening when inappropriate uses occurred.
- The use is reasonable, appropriate to the land, and consistent with community usages.
- The boundaries of the dry-sand area are visible and certain, and the character of the land limits its use to foreshore-connected recreational activities.
- The custom is obligatory, as the public's use has never been questioned by upland owners as long as they remained on the dry sand.
- The custom is not repugnant to other laws or customs.
- The public's long-standing use of the dry-sand area was so notorious that landowners must be presumed to have notice of it.
- The unique nature of the land (unstable, unsafe for construction) and the uniform treatment of ocean-front lands across the state make custom a more appropriate doctrine than prescription.
- The court did not need to consider the constitutionality of ORS 390.640 as a zoning regulation because the public easement was established. *(dicta)*

**Concurrence (Denecke, J.)**
- Disagrees with basing the decision on the English doctrine of "customary rights" because the facts don't fit its ancient outlines.
- Believes "custom" as an analogy, combined with long public usage, universal belief in the right to use, long acquiescence by owners, and the extreme desirability of the right, establishes the public's right.
- Cites cases where public rights in navigable waters and tidelands (jus publicum) persist despite private ownership, even without explicit reservation in title.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for introducing the **doctrine of custom** as a means for the public to acquire rights in private land, particularly in the context of environmental protection and public access to natural resources. It contrasts with **prescriptive easements** (which are typically for specific individuals or a defined group) and **implied dedication** (which requires owner intent). It connects to cases like *Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois* by demonstrating how public rights can limit private property interests, even those held by record title.
2. The court explicitly leaves open the effect of "consent" on prescription, suggesting that if landowners tacitly allow public use, it might not be considered "adverse" for prescription purposes, but it is consistent with custom. This raises a question about the interplay between these doctrines.

**Takeaway:** The public can acquire recreational rights in private land through long-standing custom, even if the landowner holds record title.
