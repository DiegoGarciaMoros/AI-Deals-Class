## Holbrook v. Taylor (Supreme Court of Kentucky, 1976)

**Facts**
- In 1942, the Holbrooks (appellants) purchased property.
- In 1944, the Holbrooks gave permission for a haul road to be cut on their property to move coal from a new mine, and they were paid a royalty for its use until the mine closed in 1949.
- In 1957, the Holbrooks built a tenant house on their property, and they and their tenant used the roadway.
- In 1961, the tenant house burned down and was not replaced.
- In 1964, the Taylors (appellees) bought a three-acre building site adjoining the Holbrooks' property.
- In 1965, the Taylors built their residence on their property.
- During the construction of the Taylors' home, they used the roadway for ingress and egress for workmen, hauling machinery and materials, and making general improvements, with the Holbrooks' consent.
- The Taylors widened the roadway, put in a culvert, and graveled part of it with "red dog" at a cost of approximately $100.
- After the $25,000 residence was completed, the Taylors continued to use the roadway regularly.
- In the fall of 1970, a dispute arose between the parties regarding the use of the roadway.
- The Holbrooks erected a steel cable across the roadway and put up "no-trespassing" signs.

**Procedural History**
- The Taylors filed suit to require the removal of the obstruction and to declare their right to use the roadway.
- The trial court found that a right to use the roadway by prescription had not been established, but that it had been established by estoppel.
- The Holbrooks appealed.

**Issue**
Can a license to use a roadway over another's land become irrevocable by estoppel if the licensee makes substantial expenditures and improvements on the faith of that license?

**Holding**
The appellees (Taylors) win. A license to use a roadway becomes irrevocable by estoppel when the licensee has exercised the privilege and made substantial expenditures and improvements on the faith of the license, with the licensor's knowledge and acquiescence. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Easement Creation.** An easement may be created by express written grant, by implication, by prescription, or by estoppel.
- **Easement by Prescription.** An easement, such as a right of way, is created when the owner of a tenement to which the right is claimed, or those under whom he claims title, have openly, peaceably, continuously, and under a claim of right adverse to the owner of the soil, and with his knowledge and acquiescence, used a way over the lands of another for as much as 15 years.
- **Irrevocable License by Estoppel.** Where a license is not a bare right of entry, but includes the right to erect structures and acquire an interest in the land in the nature of an easement by the construction of improvements thereon, the licensor may not revoke the license and restore his premises to their former condition after the licensee has exercised the privilege given by the license and erected the improvements at considerable expense.
- **Rejected: Revocable License.** Many courts hold that a licensee is conclusively presumed as a matter of law to know that a license is revocable at the pleasure of the licensor, and if he expends money in connection with his entry upon the land of the latter, he does so at his peril. (This rule is explicitly rejected in Kentucky for licenses involving improvements).

**Reasoning**
- The trial court was justified in finding no prescriptive easement because the use of the road prior to 1965 was by permission and there was no evidence of adverse, continuous, or uninterrupted use.
- The use of the roadway by the Taylors for construction, general improvements, and maintenance, and their construction of a $25,000 residence, all with the Holbrooks' actual consent or tacit approval, demonstrates that the license to use the roadway cannot be revoked.
- The court cites prior cases establishing that a license becomes irrevocable when the licensee makes substantial expenditures or improvements on the faith of the license.
    - In *Lashley Telephone Co. v. Durbin*, the court held that a licensor cannot revoke a license after the licensee has erected structures and made improvements at considerable expense.
    - In *Gibbs v. Anderson*, the court reversed a denial of a claim for a roadway by estoppel where the landowner agreed to the passway and watched the claimant expend money on its construction.
    - In *McCoy v. Hoffman*, the court affirmed a right to use a road by estoppel, noting that where a licensee makes substantial expenditures on the faith of a license, it becomes irrevocable.
    - In *Akers v. Moore*, the court found a right to use a passway by estoppel where landowners acquiesced in changes and allowed money to be spent on improvements, stating it would be unconscionable to permit revocation.
- There is no other reasonable location for a roadway to provide access to the Taylors' property.
- The evidence supports the trial court's finding that the right to use the roadway was established by estoppel.

**Dissent (STEPHENSON, J.)**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the doctrine of **irrevocable license by estoppel**, which acts as an exception to the general rule that licenses are revocable at will. It connects to the broader theme of how property rights can be created or altered by reliance and equity, even without a formal grant. It contrasts with cases like *Wood v. Leadbitter*, which strictly adheres to the revocability of licenses.
