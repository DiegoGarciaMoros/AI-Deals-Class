## Harbison v. City of Buffalo (New York Court of Appeals, 1958)

**Facts**
- In 1924, Andrew Harbison, Sr. purchased property at 35 Cumberland Avenue in Buffalo, NY.
- Shortly after, he built a 30x40 foot frame building and started a cooperage business, reconditioning barrels.
- At that time, the area was unpaved, had a city dump, and a glue factory nearby.
- By 1926, the area was zoned for residential use, though Harbison's business was an existing nonconforming use.
- From 1936 to 1956, Harbison obtained annual licenses to operate his business, which the city classified as a "junk dealer."
- In 1950, Harbison installed a special sewage system for $2,000 and a boiler for $700 at the city's insistence.
- On July 30, 1953, Buffalo amended its ordinances to require certain nonconforming uses in residential districts, including "junk yards," to cease or change to a conforming use within three years.
- On November 27, 1956, the city's director of licenses notified Harbison to discontinue his junk yard operation.
- The city refused Harbison's subsequent applications for a wholesale junk license and a drum reconditioning license, citing the residential zoning and the prohibition of outside storage of used materials.

**Procedural History**
- Petitioners (Harbison) brought an article 78 proceeding in the nature of mandamus, seeking an order directing the city to issue a license.
- The lower courts sustained the petitioners.
- The City of Buffalo appealed.

**Issue**
Can a city ordinance constitutionally require the termination of a pre-existing nonconforming use after a reasonable amortization period?

**Holding**
The City of Buffalo wins. A city ordinance requiring the termination of a pre-existing nonconforming use after a reasonable amortization period is a valid exercise of police power, provided the period is reasonable considering the specific facts. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Vested Rights in Nonconforming Uses.** Owners of property with existing nonconforming uses or structures have a constitutional right to continue such use, but this right is not perpetual.
- **Police Power and Public Benefit.** Where the benefit to the public is greater than the detriment to the property owner, the prohibition of continuation of prior nonconforming uses may be sustained.
- **Amortization Period.** A zoning ordinance may provide for the termination of a nonconforming use after a period sufficient to allow the property owner to amortize their investment and make other plans.
- **Reasonableness Test for Amortization.** The reasonableness of an amortization period must be determined by balancing the social harm and private injury, considering factors such as the nature of the business, improvements on the land, character of the neighborhood, and detriment to the property owner.
- Rejected: **Perpetual Right to Nonconforming Use.** A property owner has a perpetual right to continue a prior nonconforming use, regardless of changes in the neighborhood or the existence of an amortization period.

**Reasoning**
- The court acknowledges the general rule that nonconforming uses are constitutionally protected and can continue.
- However, this right has never been held to continue in perpetuity.
- The policy of zoning embraces the ultimate elimination of nonconforming uses, and courts favor reasonable restrictions on them.
- Many other states and text writers support the constitutionality of ordinances that require termination after a reasonable amortization period.
- Such regulations are akin to those restricting the enlargement, extension, rebuilding, or alteration of nonconforming structures.
- The court distinguishes *Town of Somers v. Camarco*, where a one-year notice period for a sand and gravel business was found unconstitutional due to substantial investment and an unreasonably short termination period.
- To hold that a nonconforming use vests an owner with a perpetual right would be an unwarranted restriction on the legislature in dealing with urban development.
- The court cannot determine the reasonableness of the three-year period without more facts, as the lower courts did not consider this question.
- Material issues of fact remain regarding the value and condition of improvements, relocation costs, and potential for continued business operation without outside storage.

**Dissent (Van Voobhis, J.)**
- The decision marks the beginning of the end of constitutional protection for pre-existing nonconforming uses.
- The traditional rule is that nonconforming uses are constitutionally protected and will be permitted to continue.
- The city's action is confiscatory, especially given Harbison's recent investment at the city's insistence.
- Zoning relates to future development, not retroactive reconstruction; confiscation of property for public use requires just compensation, similar to slum clearance.
- The "amortization" theory is a "catch phrase" without real meaning, as the periods vary widely and do not relate to the useful life of structures.
- This theory encourages owners to let nonconforming uses decay, leading to slums.
- The decision creates confusion, instability, and an avalanche of litigation by departing from established precedent.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is significant for introducing and validating the concept of "amortization" as a tool for municipalities to eliminate nonconforming uses, which was a departure from the traditional "vested rights" approach. It connects to *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.* by showing how zoning regulations, initially upheld for prospective application, can be extended to affect existing property rights. It also contrasts with cases like *Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon* by suggesting that some diminution of property value due to regulation is permissible without compensation, provided there's a reasonable period for the owner to recoup their investment.
2. The dissent raises a crucial point about the potential for "amortization" to be applied arbitrarily or to encourage neglect of property. A hypo could be: What if the city had required a 10-year amortization period for Harbison's business, and he had made significant improvements in year 9? Would that still be considered reasonable?

**Takeaway:** While nonconforming uses are generally protected, municipalities can require their termination after a reasonable amortization period, balancing public benefit against private injury.
