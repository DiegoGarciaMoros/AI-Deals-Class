## Shelley v. Kraemer (Supreme Court of the United States, 1948)

**Facts**
- In 1911, 30 out of 39 property owners on Labadie Avenue in St. Louis signed a restrictive covenant, recorded, stating that for 50 years, the property could not be occupied by anyone not of the Caucasian race.
- At the time the agreement was signed, five parcels in the restricted district were owned by Negroes, one since 1882.
- In 1945, petitioners Shelley, who are Negroes, purchased a parcel in the restricted area from Fitzgerald via a warranty deed.
- The Shelleys had no actual knowledge of the restrictive agreement at the time of purchase.
- In 1945, respondents Kraemer, owners of other property subject to the covenant, sued to prevent the Shelleys from taking possession and to divest their title.
- In a separate case in Detroit, in 1934, Ferguson and his wife executed a similar restrictive covenant for their property, stating it could not be used or occupied by anyone except those of the Caucasian race, effective if 80% of properties in the block were similarly restricted.
- Similar agreements were executed for 80% of the lots in that block.
- In 1944, petitioners, who were Negroes, acquired title to the Detroit property and occupied it.
- In 1945, respondents, owners of property subject to the Detroit covenant, sued to force petitioners to move and enjoined them from future occupancy.

**Procedural History**
- In the Missouri case, the trial court denied relief, finding the covenant invalid because not all property owners had signed it.
- The Supreme Court of Missouri reversed, holding the agreement effective and enforceable, and directed the trial court to grant the requested relief. Petitioners appealed.
- In the Michigan case, the trial court ordered petitioners to move and enjoined future occupancy.
- The Supreme Court of Michigan affirmed, rejecting petitioners' Fourteenth Amendment claims. Petitioners appealed.
- The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari for both cases.

**Issue**
Can state courts enforce private restrictive covenants that prevent people of a designated race from owning or occupying real property without violating the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause?

**Holding**
Petitioners (Shelley and others) win. State court enforcement of racially restrictive covenants constitutes state action that denies equal protection of the laws guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **State Action Doctrine.** The Fourteenth Amendment applies only to state action, not to purely private conduct.
- **Judicial Action as State Action.** The action of state courts and judicial officers in their official capacities is considered action of the State within the meaning of the Fourteenth Amendment.
- **Equal Protection Clause.** The Fourteenth Amendment guarantees equal protection of the laws, which includes the right to acquire, enjoy, own, and dispose of property free from state-sponsored racial discrimination.
- **Rejected: Private Agreements.** Restrictive agreements, standing alone, are not violative of the Fourteenth Amendment if their purposes are effectuated by voluntary adherence.

**Reasoning**
- The Fourteenth Amendment's prohibitions extend to all action of the State, whether by its legislative, executive, or judicial authorities.
- Judicial enforcement of a common-law rule, even if procedurally fair, can violate the Fourteenth Amendment if it results in the denial of constitutional rights.
- In these cases, the state courts actively intervened to enforce the restrictive covenants, using the full coercive power of government.
- Without judicial enforcement, petitioners would have been free to occupy the properties.
- The state courts' actions denied petitioners the enjoyment of property rights solely because of their race or color.
- The argument that denying access to courts for covenant enforcement would deny equal protection to covenant parties is without merit, as the Constitution does not grant a right to state action that denies equal protection to others.
- The power of the State to create and enforce property interests must be exercised within the boundaries of the Fourteenth Amendment.
- The primary concern of the Fourteenth Amendment's framers was to establish equality in the enjoyment of basic civil and political rights and to preserve those rights from discriminatory state action based on race or color.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is crucial for understanding the **state action doctrine** and its application to private agreements in property law. It connects to the broader theme of limits on owner sovereignty, showing that even private property rights can be constrained when their enforcement implicates constitutional protections. It builds on cases like *Buchanan v. Warley*, which struck down racially discriminatory zoning ordinances, by extending the prohibition against racial discrimination to judicial enforcement of private covenants.
2. The case leaves open the question of whether a state court's enforcement of a restrictive covenant that excludes white persons would also be unconstitutional, though the Court suggests "indiscriminate imposition of inequalities" is not equal protection.

**Takeaway:** State court enforcement of private racially restrictive covenants constitutes unconstitutional state action under the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause.
