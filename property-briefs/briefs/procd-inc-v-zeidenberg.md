## ProCD Inc. v. Zeidenberg (United States Court of Appeals, Seventh Circuit, 1996)

**Facts**
- ProCD compiled a database of information from over 3,000 telephone directories, which cost over $10 million to create and maintain.
- ProCD sold this database, called SelectPhone, on CD-ROM discs.
- ProCD engaged in price discrimination, selling the consumer version for personal use at a low price (around $150) and charging a higher price to commercial users.
- To implement price discrimination and prevent arbitrage, ProCD included a license with its consumer product.
- This license was printed in the manual, encoded on the CD-ROM, and displayed on the user's screen every time the software ran, limiting use to noncommercial purposes.
- Matthew Zeidenberg bought a consumer package of SelectPhone in 1994.
- Zeidenberg ignored the license and formed Silken Mountain Web Services, Inc., to resell the information from the database on the Internet for a price lower than ProCD's commercial rate.
- Zeidenberg purchased two additional updated SelectPhone packages and made the latest information available online through his corporation.
- ProCD sued Zeidenberg seeking an injunction against further dissemination that exceeded the rights specified in the licenses.

**Procedural History**
- The district court held that the shrinkwrap licenses were not enforceable contracts because their terms were inside the box and not on the outside, and that federal law (Copyright Act § 301(a)) preempted their enforcement even if they were contracts.
- ProCD appealed.

**Issue**
- Are shrinkwrap licenses enforceable contracts under the Uniform Commercial Code, and are they preempted by the Copyright Act?

**Holding**
- ProCD (plaintiff) wins. Shrinkwrap licenses are enforceable contracts under the UCC, and their enforcement is not preempted by the Copyright Act. Reversed and Remanded.

**Rules**
- **Contract Formation (UCC § 2-204(1)).** A contract for the sale of goods may be made in any manner sufficient to show agreement, including conduct by both parties which recognizes the existence of such a contract.
- **Acceptance of Goods (UCC § 2-606(1)(b)).** A buyer accepts goods when, after an opportunity to inspect, they fail to make an effective rejection.
- **Preemption (Copyright Act § 301(a)).** State law rights are preempted if they are equivalent to any of the exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright in works of authorship that are fixed in a tangible medium and come within the subject matter of copyright.
- **Contractual Rights vs. Copyright Rights.** Rights created by contract generally affect only their parties and do not create "exclusive rights" against the world, unlike copyright.

**Reasoning**
- The district court erred in holding that shrinkwrap licenses are not contracts because their terms are inside the box.
    - Transactions where money precedes detailed terms are common (e.g., insurance, airline tickets, concert tickets, consumer goods with warranties).
    - Requiring all terms on the outside of a package would be impractical and could remove more useful information.
    - The UCC allows contract formation by conduct, and a vendor can invite acceptance by conduct, such as using software after having an opportunity to read the license.
    - ProCD offered the license terms, and Zeidenberg accepted by using the software after having the opportunity to read the license and reject the goods.
    - The opportunity to return goods if the terms are unacceptable is important for contract formation in this context.
    - The UCC does not require special prominence for ordinary terms in shrinkwrap licenses.
    - Competition among vendors, not judicial revision of contract terms, protects consumers.
- The district court erred in holding that the Copyright Act § 301(a) preempts the enforcement of shrinkwrap licenses.
    - ProCD's software and data are "fixed in a tangible medium of expression" and "within the subject matter of copyright."
    - However, rights created by contract are not "equivalent to any of the exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright."
    - Copyright creates rights against the world, while contracts generally affect only the parties to the agreement.
    - Enforcing contracts about intellectual property, like trade secrets, does not affect strangers' ability to use information independently.
    - The enforcement of the shrinkwrap license does not withdraw any information from the public domain, as everyone remains free to copy the original telephone books.
    - Contractual terms facilitate price discrimination, which can make information more readily available and reduce prices for consumer buyers.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is crucial for understanding **contract formation** in the digital age and the interplay between state contract law (UCC) and federal intellectual property law (Copyright Act). It contrasts with cases like *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* where property rights in biological materials were debated, showing how contractual agreements can define rights even when traditional property rights are unclear or limited. It also touches on the concept of **price discrimination** and how contracts can be used to achieve it, connecting to economic rationales for property rights.
2. The court emphasizes the "opportunity to return" as a key element of contract formation here. What if the software was downloaded directly without a physical product to return? Would the reasoning still hold, or would the "opportunity to reject" be harder to establish?

**Takeaway:** Shrinkwrap licenses are enforceable contracts under the UCC, and their enforcement is not preempted by federal copyright law, as contractual rights are distinct from the exclusive rights granted by copyright.
