## Jaber v. Miller (Arkansas Supreme Court, 1951)

**Facts**
- In 1945, Jaber leased a business building from its owner for a five-year term, from March 1, 1946, to March 1, 1951, with a monthly rent of $200. The lease provided for termination if the premises were destroyed by fire.
- Jaber operated a rug shop in the building until 1949.
- In 1949, Jaber sold his merchandise and transferred his lease to Norber & Son via an instrument titled "Contract and Assignment." This instrument stated Jaber "hereby transfers and assigns" the lease for the remainder of the term. Norber & Son paid Jaber $700 cash and executed five promissory notes for $700 each. Norber & Son also agreed to pay the owner $200 monthly rent, and Jaber reserved a right to retake possession if Norber & Son failed to pay rent or the notes. The instrument did not address destruction by fire.
- Later, Miller obtained a transfer of the lease from Norber & Son.
- Miller, unable to pay the $700 notes, arranged with Jaber to divide the payments into monthly installments of $175. Miller and the Norbers executed fourteen new promissory notes for $175 each, which Jaber accepted in place of the unpaid original notes.
- On December 3, 1949, the building was destroyed by fire.
- Miller then contended that Jaber's transfer to Norber & Son was a sublease, and thus the notes represented rent, which terminated with the destruction of the building.

**Procedural History**
- Miller sued Jaber to cancel the fourteen promissory notes.
- The chancellor concluded the notes were rental payments and should be canceled.
- Jaber appealed.

**Issue**
- Should the court determine whether a transfer of a leasehold is an assignment or a sublease based on the parties' intent, or based on whether the entire term was transferred?

**Holding**
- Appellant (Jaber) wins. The transfer from Jaber to Norber & Son was an assignment, and the notes were deferred payments for the assignment, not rent. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Common Law Assignment/Sublease Distinction.** If a lessee transfers their estate for the entire remainder of the term, it is an assignment, regardless of form or intent. If the lessee transfers their estate for less than the entire term, even for a day less, it is a sublease, regardless of form or intent.
- **Rejected: Common Law Assignment/Sublease Distinction.** The court rejects the common law rule that distinguishes between an assignment and a sublease based solely on whether the entire term is transferred.
- **Modern Assignment/Sublease Distinction.** The intention of the parties governs in determining whether an instrument is an assignment or a sublease.
- **Right of Re-entry (Massachusetts Rule).** *(Rejected by this court)* If a sublessor reserves a right of re-entry for nonpayment of rent, this is a sufficient reversionary estate to make the instrument a sublease.

**Reasoning**
- The common law distinction between assignment and sublease is arbitrary and based on feudal property law, where each person held land by tenure and owed duties to an overlord.
    - If a tenant transferred their entire term, they parted with their interest, and the transferee held directly from the original lessor.
    - The intention of the parties was irrelevant; the sole question was whether the first lessee retained a reversion.
- The common law rule can lead to injustice for both the subtenant (double liability for rent) and the sublessor (merger preventing collection of increased rent).
- A majority of American courts have adopted the English doctrine, but some have tried to soften its harshness by:
    - Following the parties' intention in disputes between sublessor and sublessee.
    - Finding a "reversion" in what the parties intended as a sublease, such as a reserved right of re-entry (the Massachusetts rule).
- The court is not compelled to adhere to an unjust rule that was logical only in feudalism.
    - The rule is a "trap" for laymen and less skilled lawyers, leading to hardship and injustice by frustrating the parties' intent.
    - It is "revolting" to maintain a rule when its original grounds have vanished.
- The court adopts the rule that the intention of the parties governs.
    - The duration of the primary term compared to the sublease length may be a factor, but not the sole consideration.
- In this case, the parties clearly intended an assignment.
    - The document is titled "Contract and Assignment."
    - All language is that of an assignment.
    - The consideration was for the sale of the lease, not rent.
    - The deferred payments were evidenced by promissory notes, not typical for a lease.
- The assignment did not provide for fire contingency, which is unfortunate for Miller, but the court will not supply a provision not demanded by the assignees.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important because it rejects a long-standing common law rule based on feudal concepts in favor of a more modern approach centered on the parties' intent. It connects to the theme of how property law evolves to meet contemporary needs, similar to how courts might interpret other ancient doctrines. It contrasts with cases that strictly adhere to formalistic rules, like those sometimes seen in **Johnson v. M'Intosh** regarding discovery.
2. What if the "assignment" had reserved a right of re-entry for the last day of the term? Would the court still prioritize intent, or would the formalistic common law rule creep back in?

**Takeaway:** Courts may abandon outdated common law property rules in favor of principles that reflect the parties' intent and modern commercial realities.
