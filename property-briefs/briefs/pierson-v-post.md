## Pierson v. Post (New York Supreme Court of Judicature, 1805)

**Facts**
- Post was hunting a fox with his dogs and hounds on "wild and uninhabited, unpossessed and waste land."
- Post was in view of the fox and actively pursuing it.
- Pierson, knowing the fox was being hunted by Post, killed the fox and carried it away.

**Procedural History**
- Post sued Pierson in a justice's court for trespass on the case.
- The justice's court rendered a verdict for Post (the plaintiff below).
- Pierson (the defendant below) sued out a certiorari, assigning as error that the declaration was not sufficient in law to maintain an action.

**Issue**
Can a hunter acquire a property right in a wild animal by merely pursuing it, such that another person who intercepts and kills the animal is liable for trespass?

**Holding**
Pierson (the now plaintiff) wins. Mere pursuit of a wild animal does not create a property right in the pursuer. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Occupancy.** Property in wild animals (ferae naturae) is acquired by occupancy only.
- **Rejected: Mere Pursuit.** Mere pursuit of a wild animal, even with hounds and in view, does not vest property or right in the huntsman.
- **Rejected: Wounding without Capture.** Wounding a wild animal is not sufficient to establish property rights unless the animal is actually taken.
- **Occupancy (Majority Definition).** Occupancy requires actual corporal possession, or at least mortal wounding, circumventing, or ensnaring the animal to deprive it of its natural liberty and bring it within the pursuer's certain control.

**Reasoning**
- The court relies on ancient legal writers (Justinian, Fleta, Bracton, Puffendorf, Grotius) who define occupancy as requiring actual physical possession or control.
- Mere pursuit, even if continuous, does not constitute occupancy because it does not deprive the animal of its natural liberty.
- The court acknowledges that mortal wounding, or securing an animal with nets or snares, could be considered occupancy because it demonstrates an unequivocal intention to appropriate the animal and deprives it of its natural liberty.
- The facts of this case involve "mere pursuit" and do not meet the definition of occupancy.
- Adopting a rule that mere pursuit creates a property right would lead to uncertainty, quarrels, and litigation, which the court seeks to avoid for the sake of "certainty, and preserving peace and order in society."
- Pierson's act, though "uncourteous or unkind," caused no legal injury for which a remedy could be applied.

**Dissent (Livingston, J.)**
- The question should be decided by sportsmen, not ancient legal texts, as they would understand the customs of hunting.
- The court should consider the public policy of encouraging the destruction of "noxious beasts" like foxes.
- Allowing an interloper to take a fox after a long chase would discourage hunting and thus reduce the destruction of such pests.
- Property in wild animals can be acquired without bodily touch or manucaption, provided the pursuer has a reasonable prospect of taking the animal and has demonstrated an intention to convert it to his own use.
- The dissent embraces Barbeyrac's view that actual bodily seizure is not always necessary for possession.
- The pursuit in this case, with "dogs and hounds of imperial stature," would have "inevitably and speedily terminated in corporal possession," thus conferring a right to the fox.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is foundational for understanding the **rule of capture** in property law, particularly for wild animals. It establishes a bright-line rule for when property rights attach to unowned resources. It contrasts with cases like *Keeble v. Hickeringill*, which focused on malicious interference with trade, rather than the property right itself.
2. What if Post had shot the fox, but it ran off and Pierson found it still alive but mortally wounded? Would that be "occupancy" under the majority's rule?

**Takeaway:** To acquire a property right in a wild animal, one must not merely pursue it, but must actually capture it or at least deprive it of its natural liberty.
