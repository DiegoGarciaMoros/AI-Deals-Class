## Jacque v. Steenberg Homes (Wisconsin Supreme Court, 1997)

**Facts**
- In the fall of 1993, a neighbor of the Jacques purchased a mobile home from Steenberg Homes, with delivery included.
- Steenberg Homes determined the easiest delivery route was across the Jacques' land.
- Steenberg Homes asked the Jacques multiple times for permission to cross their land.
- The Jacques refused permission each time, due to a prior adverse possession loss.
- On February 15, 1994, Steenberg Homes decided to deliver the mobile home across the Jacques' land despite their refusals.
- Mr. Jacque confronted Steenberg employees who were planning to cross his land and reiterated that permission was denied.
- Steenberg's assistant manager came to the Jacques' home, where the Jacques showed proof of ownership and again refused permission.
- The assistant manager asked Mr. Jacque how much money it would take, but Mr. Jacque stated it was not about money and told them to use the road.
- After leaving the Jacques' home, Steenberg's assistant manager allegedly told employees to "get the home in there any way you can" and to block the road to conceal their actions.
- Steenberg employees used a "bobcat" to cut a path through the Jacques' snow-covered field and hauled the mobile home across their land.
- A neighbor informed the Jacques that Steenberg had crossed their land.
- Mr. Jacque called the sheriff's department, and an officer issued a $30 citation to Steenberg's assistant manager.

**Procedural History**
- The Jacques sued Steenberg Homes in circuit court for intentional trespass, seeking compensatory and punitive damages.
- Steenberg Homes conceded intentional trespass but argued no compensatory damages were proven and punitive damages could not be awarded without them.
- The jury awarded the Jacques $1 in nominal damages and $100,000 in punitive damages.
- The circuit court set aside the $100,000 punitive damage award.
- The court of appeals affirmed the circuit court's decision, feeling bound by precedent that nominal damages cannot support punitive damages.
- The Jacques appealed to the Wisconsin Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Can an award of nominal damages for an intentional trespass to land support an award of punitive damages?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Jacques) wins. When nominal damages are awarded for an intentional trespass to land, punitive damages may be awarded in the discretion of the jury. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Punitive Damages (General Rule).** Punitive damages cannot be awarded if only nominal compensatory damages are found.
- **Rejected: Punitive Damages (Intentional Trespass Exception).** The general rule that nominal damages cannot support punitive damages does not apply when the tort is an intentional trespass to land.
- **Right to Exclude.** The private landowner's right to exclude others from their land is one of the most essential rights of property.
- **Intentional Trespass Harm.** In intentional trespass to land, actual harm occurs even if immeasurable in dollars, as the legal right to exclude is violated.
- **Prospective Application (Sunbursting).** A judicial holding that departs from past precedent is generally applied retroactively, but prospective application (sunbursting) may be used to mitigate hardships when retroactive application would be inequitable, especially when there has been reliance on an overruled decision by a substantial number of persons and considerable harm could result.
- **Punitive Damages (Excessiveness Factors).** When determining if a punitive damage award violates the Due Process Clause, courts consider: (1) the degree of reprehensibility of the conduct; (2) the disparity between the harm or potential harm suffered and the punitive damage award; and (3) the difference between the remedy and comparable civil or criminal penalties.

**Reasoning**
- The court recognized that the general rule requiring compensatory damages to support punitive damages (from *Barnard v. Cohen*) had never been squarely addressed in the context of intentional trespass to land.
- The court found the rationale for the general rule (that society has little interest in deterring harmless conduct) inapposite for intentional trespass to land.
    - **Individual Interest:** The right to exclude others from one's land is a fundamental property right, recognized by both state and federal courts.
        - This right is "hollow" if the legal system provides insufficient means to protect it.
        - A nominal damage award (like a "halfpenny") does not adequately protect this right.
    - **Actual Harm:** The law infers some damage from every direct entry upon another's land, meaning actual harm occurs in every trespass, even if not measurable in dollars.
    - **Potential for Harm:** A series of intentional trespasses can lead to loss of ownership through adverse possession, demonstrating the potential for significant harm.
- **Societal Interest:** Society has an interest in punishing and deterring intentional trespassers beyond protecting individual landowners.
    - **Integrity of the Legal System:** Landowners should have confidence that wrongdoers will be punished, reducing the likelihood of "self-help" remedies.
    - **Deterrence:** A $30 forfeiture and $1 nominal damage award are insufficient to deter future intentional trespasses by companies like Steenberg Homes, who might otherwise find it more profitable to trespass.
        - Punitive damages must exceed the profit from illegal activity to be effective.
    - **Prosecution of Claims:** Allowing punitive damages incentivizes plaintiffs to pursue claims involving egregious conduct that might otherwise go unpunished.
- The court found that Steenberg's "reliance" on the *Barnard* rule was misleading because they only conceded intentional trespass after overwhelming evidence was presented.
    - Applying the new rule to Steenberg was justified to reward the Jacques for challenging the unsound old rule.
- The court found the $100,000 punitive damage award was not excessive based on the *Gore* factors.
    - **Reprehensibility:** Steenberg's conduct was highly reprehensible due to its indifference to the law, repeated refusals, and deceitful actions.
    - **Disparity:** While the ratio of nominal to punitive damages was large (1:100,000), a fixed multiplier is not appropriate when nominal damages do not reflect the actual harm of violating a fundamental right.
    - **Comparable Penalties:** The $30 forfeiture for simple trespass was largely irrelevant because it did not contemplate the egregious nature of Steenberg's actions and was insufficient to deter future conduct.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is crucial for understanding the **right to exclude** as a fundamental "stick in the bundle" of property rights, echoing the sentiment in *Jacque v. Steenberg Homes*. It demonstrates that even without measurable economic harm, the violation of this right can warrant significant punitive damages. It connects to the concept of **trespass introductory concepts** by emphasizing the importance of protecting property boundaries and the state's role in endorsing the private citizen's right to exclude. The court's discussion of adverse possession also links it to **acquisition and claim scope**.
2. This case raises the question of how courts balance the need for deterrence and vindication of rights against concerns about excessive punitive damage awards, especially when actual damages are minimal. What if the trespass had been accidental but still caused emotional distress? Would the outcome be the same?

**Takeaway:** The right to exclude others from one's land is so fundamental that its intentional violation, even without measurable economic harm, can justify substantial punitive damages to deter future misconduct and uphold the integrity of the legal system.
