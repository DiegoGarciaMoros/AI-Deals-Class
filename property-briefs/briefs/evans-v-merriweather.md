## Evans v. Merriweather (Illinois Supreme Court, 1842)

**Facts**
- In 1834, Smith & Baker bought land with a branch (small stream) and built a steam mill, relying on a well and the branch for water.
- One or two years later, John Evans bought land on the same branch, immediately upstream from Smith & Baker, and built a steam mill, also relying on a well and the branch for water.
- In 1836 or 1837, Smith & Baker sold their mill to Merriweather.
- In the fall of 1837, during a drought, the branch did not provide enough water to continuously run Evans's upstream mill.
- Evans instructed his workers not to stop or divert the water in the branch.
- One of Evans's workers built a dam across the branch below Evans's mill, diverting all the water into Evans's well.
- Evans lived half a mile from the mill and was frequently there.
- After the diversion, the branch below Evans's mill went dry, and Merriweather's mill could only run about one day a week, supplied by his well.
- Merriweather sued Evans three or four weeks after the dam was built.
- The branch usually provided enough water for both mills without significantly affecting the current.
- At the time of the grievance, Evans also had water hauled to supply his boilers.
- The dam diverted both the natural branch water and the hauled water poured into the branch above Evans's well.

**Procedural History**
- Merriweather (plaintiff) sued Evans (defendant) in the Greene Circuit Court.
- The jury returned a verdict for Merriweather for $150 damages, and judgment was entered.
- Evans appealed to the Illinois Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Can an upstream riparian proprietor divert an entire stream for manufacturing purposes if it deprives a downstream proprietor of water necessary for their manufacturing, even if the upstream proprietor instructed their worker not to divert the water?

**Holding**
Merriweather (appellee) wins. An upstream riparian proprietor cannot entirely consume the water of a stream for artificial purposes if it injures a downstream proprietor. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Riparian Rights (Usufructuary).** The property in water, by virtue of riparian ownership, is usufructuary, consisting of the advantage of its impetus rather than the fluid itself.
- **Reasonable Use Doctrine.** A riparian proprietor has a right to use the water of a stream but must do so without injury to other riparian proprietors.
- **Natural vs. Artificial Wants.** Human wants for water are categorized as natural (absolutely necessary for existence, e.g., drinking, household, livestock) or artificial (increase comfort and prosperity, e.g., irrigation, manufacturing).
- **Priority of Natural Wants.** If a stream is small and does not furnish enough water for all proprietors' natural wants, none can use it for irrigation or manufacturing.
- **Shared Benefit for Artificial Wants.** Where water is not needed for natural wants, and there is not enough for all proprietors' manufacturing purposes, all have a right to participate in its benefit, and no one has a right to use all the water.
- **Jury Determination of Reasonable Use.** When all have a right to participate in a common benefit and no exclusive enjoyment, a jury must determine if a party has used more than their just proportion.
- **Employer Liability for Servant's Illegal Act.** If a party avails themselves of the illegal act of their servant, the law presumes they authorized it.
- **Appellate Review of Damages.** Where damages are small and justice has been done, the Supreme Court ought not to remand a cause even if jury instructions were not perfectly correct.

**Reasoning**
- The court applies the **reasonable use doctrine**, stating that a riparian proprietor must use water in a way that causes as little injury as possible to those below, consistent with a valuable benefit to themselves.
- The court distinguishes between natural and artificial wants for water.
    - Natural wants (drinking, household, livestock) are essential for existence.
    - Artificial wants (irrigation, manufacturing) increase comfort but are not essential.
- An upstream proprietor can consume all water for natural wants if necessary.
- However, for artificial wants, if there is a lower proprietor whose natural wants are essential, the upstream proprietor must leave enough water for them.
- When water is not needed for natural wants, and there isn't enough for all proprietors' manufacturing, no single proprietor can use all the water; all have a right to participate.
- The complete diversion of the stream by Evans for his mill's artificial use, which deprived Merriweather's mill of water, was an illegal injury.
- Even though Evans claimed he forbade the diversion, the jury could infer his knowledge and acquiescence because he lived nearby and was often at the mill.
- By benefiting from the diversion, Evans is presumed to have authorized it.
- The court affirmed the judgment despite potential issues with jury instructions because the damages were small and justice was served. *(dicta)*

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes the **reasonable use doctrine** for riparian rights in Illinois, distinguishing between natural and artificial uses of water. It contrasts with the "natural flow" theory (which would allow no diminution) and the "prior appropriation" doctrine (which grants rights based on first use). This case is foundational for understanding how common law balances competing interests in shared resources. It connects to **Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.** which adopted the prior appropriation doctrine, highlighting the different approaches states take to water rights.
2. The court states that "no rule... can be laid down, as to how much each may use" for artificial wants when there isn't enough for all, leaving it to the jury. This leaves significant ambiguity regarding the precise allocation of water rights in such scenarios. [CHECK: How does a jury determine "just proportion" without a clear rule?]

**Takeaway:** Riparian owners have a right to reasonable use of water, prioritizing natural needs over artificial ones, and cannot entirely consume water for artificial purposes to the detriment of downstream users.
