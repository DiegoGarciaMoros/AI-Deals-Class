## Blackett v. Olanoff (Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, 1977)

**Facts**
- Landlords leased a building for residential purposes.
- Landlords leased a nearby building to others for use as a bar and cocktail lounge (lounge).
- The lounge generated loud music and disturbances into the early morning hours.
- Residential tenants complained to the landlords about the noise.
- The landlords made some attempts to remedy the problem but were unsuccessful.
- Residential tenants vacated their apartments within a reasonable time.

**Procedural History**
- Landlords sued tenants for rent in Municipal Court of the City of Boston.
- Cases were transferred to the Housing Court of the City of Boston.
- The Housing Court judge found for the tenants, ruling that the landlords breached the implied warranty of quiet enjoyment.
- Landlords appealed.
- The appeals were transferred to the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court.
- The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court affirmed the judgments.

**Issue**
Can landlords be held responsible for a breach of the implied warranty of quiet enjoyment due to noise from another tenant's commercial establishment, when the landlords had the ability to control the noise?

**Holding**
Defendant tenants win. Landlords breached the implied warranty of quiet enjoyment because they introduced a commercial activity that disturbed residential tenants and had the ability to control the objectionable conditions but failed to do so. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Implied Warranty of Quiet Enjoyment.** A landlord's conduct, not their intent, is controlling in determining a breach of the implied warranty of quiet enjoyment.
- **Constructive Eviction.** A tenant is constructively evicted when the landlord's actions or inactions, or what they permit to be done, result in the tenant being substantially deprived of the quiet enjoyment of their leased premises for a substantial time, and the tenant vacates within a reasonable time.
- **Landlord Control Over Nuisance.** A landlord may be held responsible for a nuisance created by one tenant that affects another if the landlord has the right and ability to control the objectionable conditions.
- Rejected: **Landlord Intent for Breach.** A landlord must perform some act with the intent of depriving the tenant of the enjoyment and occupation of the whole or part of the leased premises.

**Reasoning**
- The judge was warranted in finding that the landlords had control over the conditions.
    - The landlords introduced a commercial activity into a residential area.
    - The lounge's lease explicitly required entertainment to be conducted so it would not disturb residents.
    - The landlords complained to the lounge tenants, and the noise would temporarily abate.
- The disturbing condition was a natural and probable consequence of the landlords permitting the lounge to operate.
- The landlords had the right to control the objectionable noise from the lounge.
- This situation differs from traditional cases where a landlord is not responsible for one residential tenant annoying another.
    - Here, the landlords created the situation by leasing to the lounge.
    - The landlords had the ability to control the conditions.
- The court declines to maintain a distinction between landlord nonfeasance and malfeasance when the landlord creates the situation and has the right to control the objectionable conditions.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the **implied warranty of quiet enjoyment** and **constructive eviction**, particularly how it applies when a landlord's actions regarding one tenant impact another. It expands the landlord's responsibility beyond direct actions to include situations where they have control over a nuisance created by another tenant. It connects to cases like *Javins v. First National Realty Corp.* by showing a judicial willingness to imply protections for tenants, though *Javins* specifically addresses the implied warranty of habitability.
2. What if the lounge's lease did not contain a clause about not disturbing residents? Would the landlords still be deemed to have control over the noise?

**Takeaway:** Landlords can be held liable for constructive eviction due to noise from another tenant if they have the power to control the noise and fail to do so, even if they did not intend to disturb the tenant.
