## Murphy v. Financial Development Corp. (New Hampshire Supreme Court, 1985)

**Facts**
- In 1966, the Murphys bought a house with a mortgage.
- In March 1980, the Murphys refinanced their loan with Financial Development Corporation, executing a new promissory note and power of sale mortgage, which was later assigned to Colonial Deposit Company (collectively, "lenders").
- In February 1981, Richard Murphy became unemployed.
- By September 1981, the Murphys were seven months behind on mortgage payments and owed utility and tax assessments.
- After unsuccessful discussions about payment revisions, the lenders gave notice of intent to foreclose on October 6, 1981.
- The Murphys paid the seven months of mortgage arrearage but did not pay $643.18 in foreclosure costs and legal fees.
- The lenders scheduled the foreclosure sale for November 10, 1981.
- At the Murphys' request, the lenders postponed the sale to December 15, 1981, advising it would cost an additional $100 and the sale would proceed unless $743.18 plus current mortgage payments were received by December 15.
- Notice of postponement was posted on the property, at Nashua City Hall, and the Post Office on November 10. No prospective bidders were present for the original sale date.
- In late November, the Murphys paid the October mortgage payment but made no further payments.
- An attempt by lenders to arrange new financing for the Murphys failed when the Murphys refused to pay for a new appraisal.
- On the morning of December 15, 1981, the Murphys tried to get another postponement, but the lenders refused unless costs and fees were paid.
- The sale proceeded at 10:00 a.m. on December 15. Only the Murphys, a lenders' representative, and an attorney conducting the sale were present.
- The lenders' representative made the only bid of $27,000, roughly the amount owed, which was accepted.
- Later that day, the attorney conducting the sale informed William Dube, a representative of Southern New Hampshire Home Traders, Inc., about the sale.
- Dube offered to buy the property for $27,000, which the lenders rejected, counter-offering $40,000.
- Within two days, Dube and the lenders agreed on a purchase price of $38,000, and the sale was completed.

**Procedural History**
- The Murphys sued to set aside the sale or for damages.
- The trial court (Master) found for the Murphys, awarding $27,000 against the lenders, finding they failed to exercise good faith and due diligence. The Master also ruled Southern was a bona fide purchaser. The Master awarded attorney's fees.
- The lenders appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a mortgagor sue a mortgagee for failing to obtain a fair price at a foreclosure sale if the mortgagor did not seek an injunction before the sale?
- Did the lenders fulfill their duty of good faith and due diligence in conducting the foreclosure sale?

**Holding**
- The Murphys (plaintiffs) win on the issue of liability for due diligence, but the damages calculation and attorney's fees are reversed. A mortgagor can sue after a foreclosure sale for unfairness occurring during the sale itself, even without a prior injunction. The lenders failed to exercise due diligence in obtaining a fair price. Reversed in part; affirmed in part; remanded.

**Rules**
- **Statutory Bar to Action (RSA 479:25, II).** A statute barring actions based on foreclosure validity if an injunction is not sought prior to sale only applies to facts the mortgagor knew or should have known in time to seek an injunction.
- **Mortgagee's Duty in Power of Sale.** A mortgagee executing a power of sale must comply with statutory procedural requirements and has a duty to protect the mortgagor's interests through good faith and due diligence.
- **Mortgagee's Fiduciary Duty.** In its role as a seller, a mortgagee's duty of good faith and due diligence is essentially that of a fiduciary.
- **Due Diligence in Foreclosure Sale.** A mortgagee must exert every reasonable effort to obtain a "fair and reasonable price under the circumstances," which may include adjourning the sale or establishing an upset price.
- **Inadequacy of Price.** Inadequacy of price alone is not sufficient to demonstrate bad faith unless the price is so low as to shock the judicial conscience.
- **Bad Faith.** To constitute bad faith, there must be an intentional disregard of duty or a purpose to injure.
- **Lack of Due Diligence.** Lack of due diligence is determined by whether a reasonable person in the mortgagee's place would have adjourned the sale or taken other measures to receive a fair price.
- **Damages for Bad Faith.** Where bad faith is found, damages are the difference between the fair market value and the price obtained at sale.
- **Damages for Lack of Due Diligence.** Where only lack of due diligence is found, damages are the difference between a fair price for the property and the price obtained at the foreclosure sale.

**Reasoning**
- The statutory bar (RSA 479:25, II) does not apply because the alleged unfairness (sale price) occurred during the sale itself, meaning the Murphys could not have known about it in time to seek an injunction.
- The lenders did not act in bad faith because there was no evidence of intentional disregard of duty or purpose to injure (e.g., discouraging other buyers).
- The lenders failed to exercise due diligence:
    - They did not reappraise the house despite knowing the Murphys had substantial equity ($19,000 based on a 1980 appraisal).
    - They were the only bidders and their bid only covered the debt, providing no return of the Murphys' equity.
    - They had reason to know they could make a substantial profit on a quick turnaround sale, evidenced by their immediate offer to sell for $40,000 and actual sale for $38,000 shortly after buying it for $27,000.
    - They should have taken more steps to ensure a higher price, such as establishing an upset price or commercially advertising the postponed sale.
    - Their statutory notice efforts were insufficient to demonstrate due diligence, as no one else appeared at the sale.
- The proper measure of damages for lack of due diligence is the difference between a "fair price" (not necessarily fair market value) and the price obtained at sale.
- Attorney's fees are not warranted because there was no finding of bad faith or oppressive conduct.

**Dissent (Brock, J.)**
- Agrees with the majority on the mortgagee's fiduciary duty and its definition.
- Disagrees that the record supports the master's finding of lack of due diligence.
- The master made no findings about what a voluntary seller would have done that the lenders did not.
- There is no evidence that a voluntary seller would have reappraised or set an upset price, nor what an appropriate upset price would have been.
- The lenders' subsequent offer to sell for $40,000 does not, by itself, show what a reasonable person would have done to ensure a fair price at the foreclosure sale.
- The master's implication that the lenders should have made a higher bid is not supported by authority, as the mortgagee's duty as seller ends once reasonable efforts are made to obtain a fair price.
- The sale price was not "so low as to shock the judicial conscience."

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the limits on a mortgagee's power of sale, imposing a duty of "due diligence" beyond mere statutory compliance. It connects to **owner sovereignty and its limits** by showing that even a party with a contractual right to sell another's property (the mortgagee) is constrained by equitable duties. It contrasts with cases like *Williams v. Ford Motor Credit Co.* (though not directly cited), which might focus more on the strict terms of self-help repossession, by emphasizing the fiduciary-like duties in a foreclosure context.
2. What if the lenders had waited a month to sell the property to Dube? Would that change the court's finding that they "had reason to know" they could make a quick profit?

**Takeaway:** A mortgagee conducting a foreclosure sale has a fiduciary-like duty of due diligence to obtain a fair price for the property, even if statutory notice requirements are met, and failure to do so can result in damages.
