## Hinman v. Pacific Air Transport (United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 1936)

**Facts**
- Appellants owned 72.5 acres of land in Burbank, California.
- Appellants claimed ownership of the airspace above their land to an altitude of at least 150 feet.
- Appellees, commercial airlines, operated aircraft through this airspace at altitudes as low as 5 feet above the surface.
- Appellants notified appellees to stop trespassing.
- Appellees continued to operate aircraft through the airspace.
- Appellees' aircraft followed two defined courses over appellants' land.

**Procedural History**
- Appellants filed a bill against Pacific Air Transport and another against United Air Lines Transport Corporation.
- The trial court sustained motions to dismiss the original bills.
- Appellants filed amended bills.
- The trial court sustained motions to dismiss the first amended bills.
- Appellants filed second amended bills (or first amended in one case).
- The trial court sustained motions to dismiss these bills.
- Appellants appealed the decrees sustaining the motions to dismiss.

**Issue**
Can a landowner claim absolute ownership of the airspace above their property to an indefinite height, and sue for trespass when aircraft fly through it without causing actual damage or interference with the landowner's use?

**Holding**
Defendant (appellees) wins. A landowner does not have absolute ownership of the airspace above their property to an indefinite height, and aircraft flying through it without causing actual damage or interference with the landowner's use does not constitute trespass. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Ad Coelum Doctrine (Rejected).** The ancient maxim "cujus est solum ejus est usque ad coelum et ad inferos" (whoever owns the soil, owns up to the sky and down to the depths) is not the law and was never intended to be taken literally.
- **Property Rights in Airspace.** The essence of property is dominion over it, and property must be capable of exclusive possession.
- **Air as Property.** The air, like the sea, is by its nature incapable of private ownership, except insofar as one may actually use it.
- **Extent of Airspace Ownership.** A landowner owns as much of the space above the ground as they can occupy or make use of in connection with the enjoyment of their land, and this right varies with their needs and is coextensive with them, but only so long as they use it. All that lies beyond belongs to the world.
- **Trespass to Airspace.** Any use of airspace by others that is injurious to the landowner's land, or constitutes an actual interference with their possession or beneficial use thereof, would be a trespass for which there would be a remedy.
- **Easement by Prescription (Airspace).** It is not legally possible to obtain an easement by prescription through the airspace above another's land.
- **Damages for Trespass.** If a legal injury is proven but the extent of the injury is not shown, nominal damages only can be recovered.

**Reasoning**
- The **ad coelum doctrine** is rejected because it is a figurative phrase that would lead to absurd results if taken literally.
    - The doctrine was invented when use of space above land was confined to narrow limits and meant that the owner could use overlying space to the extent they were able, and no one could interfere with that use.
    - A literal construction would mean ownership of infinity, which is impossible.
- **Ownership of airspace** is limited to what is actually used or occupied in connection with the enjoyment of the land.
    - Title to airspace unconnected with the use of land is inconceivable and not known to the law.
    - The right to airspace is not fixed but varies with needs and is coextensive with them.
    - A landowner's title to the air is paramount only to the extent that no one can acquire a right to the space above him that will limit him in whatever use he can make of it as part of his enjoyment of the land.
- It would be **impracticable and lead to confusion** if landowners could claim definite, unused spaces in the air to protect contemplated future use.
    - Such a rule would be a departure from human experience and reason.
    - It would be difficult for courts to protect varying claims and enforce rights like ejectment.
    - This rule is not necessary to protect constitutional rights in property.
- **Flying through airspace is not inherently a trespass.**
    - It is a lawful act unless it causes actual injury to the landowner's possession.
    - Appellants did not allege facts showing actual or substantial damage, only a naked conclusion.
    - Therefore, the complaint did not state a case for injunctive relief or more than nominal damages.
- An **easement by prescription** cannot be obtained for airspace.
    - The primary thing being used by planes is the air itself, which is generally held not subject to prescriptive easements.

**Dissent (Mathews)**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case significantly limits the traditional ad coelum doctrine, establishing that a landowner's rights in airspace are not absolute but are tied to actual use and enjoyment of the surface. It connects to *Jacque v. Steenberg Homes* by showing that while the right to exclude is fundamental, it is not limitless, especially when the "trespass" occurs far above the surface and causes no actual harm. It also contrasts with cases like *Edwards v. Sims*, which dealt with subsurface rights, by highlighting the different considerations for air rights.
2. This case raises the question of how high "actual use" extends, and what constitutes "actual interference" in the context of modern technology. What if the planes were flying so low that they created significant noise or vibrations, even if they didn't physically touch the property?

**Takeaway:** A landowner's property rights in airspace are limited to the portion they can actually use and occupy in connection with their land, and mere flight through unused airspace without actual damage is not a trespass.
