## Fisher v. Steward (New Hampshire Superior Court, 1804)

**Facts**
- The plaintiffs found a swarm of bees in a tree on the defendant’s land.
- The plaintiffs marked the tree.
- The plaintiffs notified the defendant.
- The defendant cut down the tree in September 1803.
- The defendant converted the honey to his own use.

**Procedural History**
- The plaintiffs brought an action of trover for a swarm of bees and for honey in the comb.
- The jury returned a verdict for the defendant.

**Issue**
- Does a person who finds a swarm of bees in a tree on another's land and marks the tree acquire property in the honey, even if they notify the landowner?

**Holding**
- Defendant (landowner) wins. The plaintiffs did not acquire property in the honey because their actions constituted a trespass and interfered with the landowner's rights. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Occupancy.** To acquire property by occupancy, one must appropriate the item to themselves.
- **Trespass.** An act performed on another's land without permission is a trespass.
- **Rejected: Local Custom.** A local custom or usage is not binding if it is not recognized by the court and is not uniformly applied throughout the state.
- **Ratione Soli.** Wild animals, such as bees, that hive themselves in a tree on one's land belong to the owner of the soil.
- **Wild Animals.** Game killed on the land of another by a trespasser belongs to the owner of the land.

**Reasoning**
- The plaintiffs did not have property in the land or the tree, nor in the bees themselves.
- The plaintiffs' actions (seeing bees, hearing them, marking the tree) did not amount to occupancy of the honey.
- Marking the tree was a trespass, and therefore cannot create a right for the plaintiffs.
- The doctrine contended for by the plaintiffs is injurious to the rights of property.
- Allowing the plaintiffs to acquire title to the honey would imply a right to cut down the tree and pass over the defendant's land, which would interfere with the defendant's vested rights.
- The bees and honey in the defendant's trees belong to him for the same reason that mines and minerals belong to the owner of the soil.
- The court does not recognize a local usage that finding bees acquires property in them, as such a usage would need to be binding throughout the state.

- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the strong protection afforded to landowners' rights, particularly against trespassers, and how the principle of *ratione soli* can extend ownership to wild animals found on one's land. It contrasts with cases like *Pierson v. Post* by emphasizing the landowner's prior claim over a finder's claim when the item is on the landowner's property.

**Takeaway:** A trespasser cannot acquire property rights in wild animals or their products found on another's land, as the landowner's rights *ratione soli* prevail.
