## Pardee v. Camden Lumber Co. (Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia, 1911)

**Facts**
- The plaintiffs, Barton Pardee and others, owned land with standing timber.
- The defendants, Camden Lumber Company and others, were cutting timber on the plaintiffs' land.
- The plaintiffs filed a bill for an injunction to prevent the cutting of timber.
- An action of ejectment to determine title to the land was pending between the parties.
- The trial court dissolved the injunction.
- A jury verdict in the ejectment action was rendered in favor of the defendants, but no judgment was entered on the verdict.

**Procedural History**
- The Circuit Court of Webster County dissolved an injunction against the defendants.
- The plaintiffs appealed to the Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia.
- Reversed and Remanded.

**Issue**
- Can a landowner obtain an injunction to prevent the cutting of timber on their land when the title is in dispute, without alleging the trespasser's insolvency or other circumstances making a legal remedy inadequate?

**Holding**
- The plaintiffs (landowners) win. An owner of land with standing timber can obtain an injunction to prevent its cutting, even if the trespasser is solvent, because the legal remedy of damages is inadequate for the destruction of growing timber. Reversed and Remanded.

**Rules**
- **Injunction for Injury to Growing Timber.** The wrongful destruction of growing timber, which is "part and parcel of the land on which it stands," is an injury to the land itself that is not adequately remediable by an action at law.
- **Protection of Owner of Standing Timber.** An owner of land has a legal right to keep timber in its natural state until they choose to alter it, and equity will protect this right.
- **Insolvency Not Required for Timber Injunction.** To obtain an injunction to prevent timber trespass, it is not necessary to allege the trespasser's insolvency or any other circumstance rendering an action at law futile.
- **Injunction Pending Title Dispute.** When land title is in dispute and an ejectment action is pending or imminent, the claimant out of possession may enjoin the other party from cutting timber until the title question is determined by the law court.
- **Verdict Not Judgment.** A jury verdict in a law action, not yet carried into judgment, does not justify the dissolution of an injunction.
- Rejected: **Insolvency Requirement for Timber Injunction.** To obtain an injunction against timber cutting, the plaintiff must aver good title, trespass by the defendant, and the defendant's insolvency or other circumstances rendering a damages action futile.

**Reasoning**
- The court re-examined the long-standing rule requiring proof of insolvency for an injunction against timber cutting due to public and professional dissatisfaction and the increased value of timber.
- Overruling the prior rule does not disturb property rights but rather conserves and protects them by providing a more complete remedy for property owners.
- The prior rule was based on the supposed adequacy of legal remedies for timber cutting, which the court now finds unsound.
- Standing timber is part of the real estate and has no legal equivalent in nature or value; cutting it changes its legal nature and incidents.
- Legal remedies (damages or detinue) are inadequate because they cannot restore the timber to its natural state or replace it with an equivalent.
- The general policy of the law guarantees an owner the right to possession, dominion, and immunity from injury, especially for property that cannot be substantially replaced.
- The court analogizes to specific performance for unique personal property (heirlooms, limited stock) and real estate, where damages are inadequate.
- The distinction between contractual rights concerning real estate (always subject to equitable cognizance) and trespasses thereon (historically denied equitable relief without insolvency) is inconsistent.
- The wrong done by a tenant (waste) and a stranger (trespass) to timber is of equal gravity, yet historically, injunctions were granted against tenants but not strangers, based on a flawed theory of legal remedy adequacy.
- The historical view of timber as an encumbrance rather than a valuable asset led to the erroneous rule, but conditions have changed, making timber scarce and valuable.
- The rule is contrary to the weight of authority in other jurisdictions.
- A jury verdict alone, without a judgment, is not a final adjudication of title and does not justify dissolving the injunction.

**Dissent (Brannon, Judge)**
- None. (Concurrence, but not a dissent)

**Concurrence (Brannon, Judge)**
- Agrees with the majority's decision to overrule prior decisions denying injunctions against timber cutting.
- Believes the rule of those decisions is "so bad that it ought not to stand."
- States that an erroneous decision should be overruled if its continued operation does more harm than its reversal.
- The overruled decisions have been "doing mischief all the time," and overruling them will avoid that mischief without harm, especially since it relates only to remedy.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important because it significantly expands the availability of injunctive relief for trespass to land, particularly for the destruction of natural resources like timber. It connects to cases like *Jacque v. Steenberg Homes* by emphasizing the owner's right to exclude and the inadequacy of damages for certain property interests, even when the harm is not "irreparable" in the traditional sense of the trespasser being insolvent. It also highlights the evolving nature of property law in response to changing societal values and economic conditions, similar to how nuisance law evolved in cases like *Jost v. Dairyland Power Cooperative* or *Spur Industries Inc. v. Del E. Webb Development Co.*
2. This case raises the question of how courts should balance the principle of *stare decisis* with the need to correct what they perceive as erroneous or outdated legal rules, especially when those rules impact property rights and remedies.

**Takeaway:** Courts may grant injunctive relief for trespass to land, even without a showing of the trespasser's insolvency, when the injury involves the destruction of unique or irreplaceable natural resources like standing timber, as damages are an inadequate remedy.
