## Howard v. Kunto (Washington Court of Appeals, 1970)

**Facts**
- As early as 1932, McCall occupied a house on a 50-foot parcel of land on Hood Canal, but his deed described an adjacent 50-foot parcel to the west.
- Over the years since 1946, several conveyances occurred using the same incorrect legal description, with each new owner taking possession of the house and land McCall occupied.
- The Millers, predecessors to the Kuntos, had a survey done that mistakenly confirmed their deed description matched their physical occupation, and they built a dock and other improvements.
- In 1959, the Kuntos took possession of the disputed property from the Millers under a deed with the same incorrect description.
- In 1960, the Howards, who owned land east of the Kuntos, had a survey performed for their own property, which revealed that the deed descriptions and land occupancy of several parties, including the Kuntos, did not coincide with the government survey.
- The survey showed the Howards were record owners of land occupied by the Moyers, and the Moyers held record title to the land occupied by the Kuntos.
- Howard obtained a conveyance from Moyer for the land the Kuntos occupied in April 1960.
- Until this conveyance, neither Moyer nor his predecessors asserted any right to the property occupied by the Kunto chain of possession.
- The Howards then initiated this action to quiet title.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court quieted title in the Howards and Yearlys, denying the Kuntos' claim of adverse possession.
- The Kuntos appealed.

**Issue**
- Can periods of possession by previous owners be "tacked" together to meet the statutory period for adverse possession when the deed mistakenly describes an adjacent parcel, and can summer-only occupancy satisfy the continuity requirement?

**Holding**
- The Kuntos (appellants) win. Successive periods of possession can be tacked when there is a reasonable connection between occupants, even if the deed description is erroneous, and summer occupancy can be continuous for adverse possession. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Adverse Possession Elements.** To constitute adverse possession, there must be actual possession which is uninterrupted, open and notorious, hostile and exclusive, and under a claim of right made in good faith for the statutory period.
- **Continuity of Possession.** The requisite possession requires such possession and dominion "as ordinarily marks the conduct of owners in general, in holding, managing, and caring for property of like nature and condition."
- **Tacking.** Tacking of adverse possession is permitted if the successive occupants are in "privity."
- **Privity (for Tacking).** The deed running between the parties purporting to transfer the land possessed traditionally furnishes the privity of estate which connects the possession of the successive occupants.
- **Rejected: Privity (Strict Deed Match).** Where the deed does not describe any of the land which was occupied, the actual transfer of possession is insufficient to establish privity.

**Reasoning**
- **Continuity of Possession:**
    - Summer occupancy of a summer beach home does not destroy the continuity of possession.
    - The nature and condition of the property dictate what constitutes "uninterrupted" possession.
    - Occupancy during the period of the year the land is capable of use is sufficient continuity.
- **Tacking and Privity:**
    - The court rejects the trial court's strict interpretation of privity, which would prevent tacking when the deed description does not match the occupied land.
    - The historical reason for privity was to prevent a succession of trespasses from defeating record title, and to ensure the adverse possessor had a "claim of right."
    - There is a substantial difference between a squatter and a property purchaser who occupies the wrong land due to an inaccurate survey.
    - Public policy favors early certainty as to land ownership, especially where exact boundary determinations are difficult and expensive.
    - It is not reasonable or customary for every purchaser to hire a surveyor to verify deed boundaries for a beach home.
    - The technical requirement of "privity" should not upset long periods of occupancy by those who received erroneous deed descriptions in good faith.
    - "Privity" is merely a judicial recognition of the need for a reasonable connection between successive occupants to elevate their claim above that of a wrongdoer.
    - Such a reasonable connection exists when successive purchasers receive record title to tract A under the mistaken belief they are acquiring tract B, and possession of tract B is transferred and occupied continuously for the statutory period.
    - This is analogous to cases where a purchaser claims more land than described in their deed, where tacking is permitted.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the flexibility of adverse possession requirements, particularly regarding continuity and tacking. It contrasts with cases that might require more stringent proof of continuous possession or a direct link in the chain of title for tacking. It shows how courts can interpret the elements of adverse possession to achieve what they perceive as equitable outcomes and to reflect common practices (like summer home use).
2. What if the Kuntos had known their deed was incorrect but still occupied the land? Would the court's reasoning about "good faith" and "mistaken belief" still apply, or would they be considered mere trespassers?

**Takeaway:** Adverse possession requirements for continuity and privity can be interpreted flexibly to reflect the nature of the property and the good faith of the possessors, allowing tacking even when deeds contain errors.
