## Howard v. Kunto (Washington Court of Appeals, 1970)

**Facts**
- As early as 1932, McCall resided in a house on a 50-foot parcel of land on Hood Canal, but his deed described an adjacent 50-foot parcel to the west.
- Over the years since 1946, several conveyances occurred, using the same incorrect legal description, with each new owner taking possession of the house and land McCall occupied.
- The Millers, Kunto's immediate predecessors, had a survey done to build a dock, which mistakenly indicated their deed description and physical occupation were in conformity.
- The Kuntos took possession of the disputed property under a deed from the Millers in 1959.
- In 1960, the Howards, who owned land east of the Kuntos, had a survey performed to convey an interest to the Yearlys.
- The Howards' survey revealed that the deed descriptions and land occupancy of the parties did not coincide: the Howards were record owners of the land occupied by the Moyers, and the Moyers held record title to the land occupied by the Kuntos.
- Howard approached Moyer, and Moyer conveyed to the Howards record title to the land upon which the Kunto house stood.
- Until April 1960, neither Moyer nor his predecessors asserted ownership rights to the property possessed by Kunto and his predecessors.
- The Howards and Yearlys initiated an action to quiet title in August 1960.
- At the time of the action, the Kuntos had been in occupancy of the disputed property for less than a year.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court quieted title in the plaintiffs (Howards and Yearlys).
- The defendants (Kuntos) appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a person establish adverse possession by tacking the periods of possession of predecessors in interest when the deed mistakenly describes an adjacent parcel, and when the occupancy was limited to summer use?

**Holding**
- The defendants (Kuntos) win. A person can establish adverse possession by tacking the periods of possession of predecessors in interest when there is a reasonable connection between successive occupants, even if the deed mistakenly describes an adjacent parcel, and summer occupancy can constitute continuous possession. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Adverse Possession Elements.** To constitute adverse possession, there must be actual possession which is uninterrupted, open and notorious, hostile and exclusive, and under a claim of right made in good faith for the statutory period.
- **Continuity of Possession.** The requisite possession requires such possession and dominion "as ordinarily marks the conduct of owners in general, in holding, managing, and caring for property of like nature and condition."
- **Summer Occupancy.** Continuity of possession may be established although the land is used regularly for only a certain period each year, provided there is no break so as to sever two possessions.
- **Tacking.** Tacking of adverse possession is permitted if the successive occupants are in "privity."
- **Privity (Traditional).** The deed running between the parties purporting to transfer the land possessed traditionally furnishes the privity of estate which connects the possession of the successive occupants.
- **Rejected: Privity (Strict Construction).** Where the deed does not describe any of the land which was occupied, the actual transfer of possession is insufficient to establish privity.
- **Privity (Modern).** The requirement of "privity" is no more than judicial recognition of the need for some reasonable connection between successive occupants of real property so as to raise their claim of right above the status of the wrongdoer or the trespasser.

**Reasoning**
- The court rejected the conclusion that summer occupancy only of a summer beach home destroys the continuity of possession.
    - The nature and condition of the property (a summer recreational retreat) must be considered.
    - Occupancy during the period the land is capable of use is sufficient continuity.
- The court found sufficient privity of estate to permit tacking.
    - The traditional requirement of privity had roots in preventing a succession of trespasses from defeating record title.
    - There is a substantial difference between a squatter and a property purchaser who, due to an inaccurate survey, occupies and improves property adjacent to what their deed describes.
    - There is a strong public policy favoring early certainty as to the location of land ownership.
    - Exact determination of land locations and boundaries on the irregular perimeters of Puget Sound is difficult and expensive, and it is not reasonable or customary for every purchaser to engage a surveyor.
    - The technical requirement of "privity" should not upset long periods of occupancy by those who in good faith received an erroneous deed description.
    - A reasonable connection exists where successive purchasers received record title to tract A under the mistaken belief they were acquiring tract B, and possession of tract B was transferred and occupied continuously for more than 10 years by successive occupants.
    - This application of the privity requirement is especially relevant where the holder of record title to tract B acquired it with knowledge of the discrepancy.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the **adverse possession** elements of "continuity" and "privity." It relaxes the strict interpretation of these elements, particularly in the context of mistaken boundaries and seasonal use, connecting to the idea of protecting good faith possessors over strict adherence to record title. It contrasts with cases like *Carpenter v. Ruperto* which emphasize the "good faith" requirement for adverse possession, though *Howard v. Kunto* still requires a "claim of right made in good faith."
2. The court's reasoning regarding "privity" suggests a policy preference for protecting long-term, good-faith possessors over strict adherence to deed descriptions, especially when surveys are difficult and errors are common. This raises the question of how far this relaxation of privity could extend. What if the mistake wasn't immediately contiguous, or if the chain of title was broken by a period of non-occupancy?

**Takeaway:** Adverse possession requirements for continuity and privity can be flexible to accommodate the nature of the property and good-faith mistakes in deed descriptions, allowing for tacking of possession across successive owners.
