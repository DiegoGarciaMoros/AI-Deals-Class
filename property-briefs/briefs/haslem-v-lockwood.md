## Haslem v. Lockwood (Connecticut Supreme Court, 1871)

**Facts**
- Manure accumulated in a public street in Stamford, Connecticut.
- On the evening of April 6, 1869, the plaintiff, Haslem, hired two men to rake the scattered manure into eighteen heaps.
- The men finished at 8 PM and left the heaps in the street, intending for Haslem to remove them the next evening.
- The heaps consisted chiefly of horse manure from animals hitched to the public park railing, mixed with dirt and straw.
- The borough of Stamford owned the fee of the land where the manure lay.
- The removal of the manure was calculated to improve the appearance and health of the borough.
- On the morning of April 7, the defendant, Lockwood, saw the heaps.
- Lockwood inquired of the warden if anyone had permission to remove the manure and was told no.
- Before noon on April 7, Lockwood removed the heaps and the remaining scattered manure to his own land.
- Haslem demanded payment for the manure, which Lockwood refused.

**Procedural History**
- Haslem brought an action of trover against Lockwood before a justice of the peace.
- Lockwood appealed to the Court of Common Pleas for Fairfield County.
- The Court of Common Pleas ruled against Haslem, finding he had not established sufficient interest or right of possession, and rendered judgment for Lockwood.
- Haslem moved for a new trial due to error in this ruling.

**Issue**
Can a person who gathers abandoned property into heaps on a public highway claim a right to it against a third party if they leave it for a reasonable time to arrange for its removal?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Haslem) wins. A person who expends labor to gather abandoned property into heaps on a public highway acquires a right to it against a mere wrongdoer, provided they remove it within a reasonable time. Reversed and remanded for a new trial.

**Rules**
- **Personal Property.** Manure scattered on a public highway is personal property, not real estate.
- **Abandonment.** Property that is worthless to its original owners and left behind is considered abandoned.
- **First Occupancy.** Abandoned property belongs to the first occupant who takes possession of it.
- **Labor and Value Enhancement.** When a party finds comparatively worthless property and greatly increases its value by labor and expense, they acquire a right to it.
- **Reasonable Time for Removal.** A party who has enhanced the value of abandoned property by labor is allowed a reasonable time to remove it, and during that time, their right to it is protected.
- **Possession Against Wrongdoer.** Peaceable and quiet possession of property is sufficient against a mere wrongdoer.

**Reasoning**
- The manure on the highway was personal property, not real estate, because it was not produced in the course of husbandry and was a detriment, not a benefit, to the highway.
- The original owners (travelers) abandoned the manure because it was worthless to them.
- Even if the borough owned the fee, the removal of the manure improved the borough, and no objection was made to Haslem's actions.
- Haslem's labor in raking the manure into heaps greatly enhanced its value and changed its original condition.
- To deny Haslem a right to the manure after his labor would be unjust, especially against a "mere wrongdoer" like Lockwood.
- A party who expends labor to collect abandoned property should have a reasonable time to remove it.
    - This principle is illustrated by the hypothetical of a teamster's scattered grain.
    - The court cites the statute for seaweed removal (24 hours) as a guide for what constitutes a reasonable time.
- Lockwood's conduct is not commendable, as he appropriated the fruits of Haslem's labor.
- Haslem had peaceable and quiet possession, which is sufficient against Lockwood, who had no superior right.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the concept of **first possession** and how labor can create or strengthen a property right, even in abandoned property. It connects to **Pierson v. Post** by exploring what constitutes "possession" and how it is acquired, but here the labor is applied to an inanimate object rather than a wild animal. It also touches on the idea of **accession** by recognizing that labor can add value to property.
2. The court's reliance on the seaweed statute for "reasonable time" raises a question about how such a standard would be applied in other contexts without a specific statute. What if the property was much larger or more difficult to move?

**Takeaway:** Labor expended on abandoned property can create a temporary property right, protected for a reasonable time to allow for removal.
