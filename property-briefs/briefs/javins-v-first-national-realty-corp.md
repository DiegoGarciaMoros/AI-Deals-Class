## Javins v. First National Realty Corp. (United States Court of Appeals, District of Columbia Circuit, 1970)

**Facts**
- Appellants rented apartments in Clifton Terrace, a three-building apartment complex in Northwest Washington, D.C., from First National Realty Corporation.
- First National Realty Corporation filed separate actions seeking possession of the apartments because the appellants had not paid rent for April.
- Appellants admitted they had not paid rent but alleged numerous violations of the Housing Regulations of the District of Columbia as a defense.
- Appellants offered to prove approximately 1500 housing code violations in the building, some directly affecting their premises and others indirectly, all arising since the term of the lease commenced.

**Procedural History**
- The Landlord and Tenant Branch of the District of Columbia Court of General Sessions ruled proof of housing code violations inadmissible as a defense to an eviction action for nonpayment of rent and entered judgment for the landlord.
- The District of Columbia Court of Appeals upheld this ruling.
- The United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit granted appellants' petitions for leave to appeal.
- The United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit reversed and remanded.

**Issue**
Can housing code violations that arise during the term of a lease be used as a defense to an eviction action for nonpayment of rent?

**Holding**
The tenants win. A warranty of habitability, measured by the standards in the Housing Regulations for the District of Columbia, is implied by operation of law into leases of urban dwelling units, and breach of this warranty gives rise to the usual remedies for breach of contract. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Traditional Leasehold Law.** Historically, a lease was considered a conveyance of an interest in land, and courts applied real property rules, often absolving landlords of repair obligations unless explicitly covenanted.
- **Modern Contract Law.** Modern contract law recognizes implied warranties of quality in sales of goods and services, protecting buyers' legitimate expectations.
- **Implied Warranty of Habitability.** A warranty of habitability, measured by housing code standards, is implied by operation of law into leases of urban dwelling units.
- **Contractual Interdependence.** Under contract principles, a tenant's obligation to pay rent is dependent upon the landlord's performance of their obligations, including the implied warranty of habitability.
- **Waiver of Statutory Duties.** Duties imposed by housing regulations cannot be waived or shifted by agreement if the regulations specifically place the duty upon the lessor.

**Reasoning**
- The traditional view of a lease as a conveyance of land is outdated for urban dwelling units.
    - In an agrarian society, the land was paramount, and tenants could make repairs.
    - Modern urban tenants seek shelter and a "package of goods and services," not just bare possession of land.
    - Urban tenants often lack the skills, resources, or long-term interest to make repairs, especially in complex multiple dwellings.
- Modern contract principles, including implied warranties, are more appropriate for interpreting urban dwelling leases.
    - Buyers of goods and services rely on suppliers for quality, and implied warranties protect these expectations.
    - This principle has extended to sales of new homes and other non-sales transactions.
    - The landlord is a commercial businessman with greater capacity and incentive to maintain the property, and the tenant relies on the landlord's skill.
- The District of Columbia Housing Regulations require an implied warranty of habitability.
    - The Housing Regulations provide a comprehensive scheme for housing standards, maintenance, and penalties.
    - Previous court decisions (Whetzel, Kanelos, Brown) established that these regulations create privately enforceable rights and duties for landlords.
    - The regulations reflect a legislative policy judgment that landlords have a duty to maintain habitable conditions, rendering the old common law rule obsolete.
    - The law existing at the time of contract formation is deemed a part of the contract, implying the housing code into leases.
- Breach of the implied warranty of habitability affects the tenant's obligation to pay rent.
    - The tenant's obligation to pay rent is dependent on the landlord's performance of the warranty.
    - The finder of fact must determine if violations existed and what portion of rent obligation was suspended.
    - If the entire obligation is extinguished, the eviction action fails; if partial, the tenant must pay the due amount to avoid eviction.
    - The court may require tenants to pay rent into the court registry during litigation to protect both parties.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is foundational for understanding the shift from property law to contract law in landlord-tenant relationships, particularly for residential leases. It directly challenges the common law doctrine of *caveat emptor* in this context. It connects to cases like *Berg v. Wiley* by limiting a landlord's self-help remedies when a tenant has a valid defense based on habitability. It also relates to *Medio-Dental Building Co. v. Horton & Converse* in its discussion of applying contract principles to leases.
2. What if a tenant intentionally causes the housing code violations? The court states that the landlord can defend by proving the damage was caused by the tenant's wrongful action.

**Takeaway:** Residential leases are contracts that include an implied warranty of habitability, meaning landlords must maintain premises according to housing codes, and tenants can use breach of this warranty as a defense against nonpayment of rent.
