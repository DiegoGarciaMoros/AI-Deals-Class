## United States v. Corrow (United States Court of Appeals, Tenth Circuit, 1997)

**Facts**
- Ray Winnie, a Navajo religious singer, used Yei B'Chei (ceremonial masks) for over 25 years.
- Traditionally, Yei B'Chei are passed to family members or loaned to other clans.
- When Mr. Winnie died in 1991, he made no provision for the Yei B'Chei, and no family member requested them.
- Richard Corrow, owner of Artifacts Display Stands, visited Mr. Winnie's widow, Mrs. Fannie Winnie, in August 1993.
- Mr. Corrow inquired about the Yei B'Chei, and the Winnie family eventually showed him 22 masks.
- Mr. Corrow offered $5,000 for the Yei B'Chei, claiming he would deliver them to a young Navajo chanter in Utah to keep them sacred.
- Mr. Corrow agreed to the family's price of $10,000 for the Yei B'Chei, five headdresses, and other artifacts.
- Mrs. Winnie, who spoke no English, placed her thumbprint on a receipt after her granddaughter read it to her in Navajo.
- In November 1994, Mr. Corrow was contacted by the East-West Trading Company about a wealthy Chicago surgeon interested in purchasing Yei B'Chei.
- The purported buyer was an undercover National Park Service ranger, James Tanner, investigating questionable trade.
- Agent Tanner saw photographs of 17 of the 22 Yei B'Chei Mr. Corrow purchased, noting eagle and owl feathers.
- Agent Tanner agreed to a purchase price of $70,000 for the Yei B'Chei, with $50,000 for Mr. Corrow.
- On December 9, 1994, Mr. Corrow arrived in Albuquerque with suitcases and a box containing Navajo religious objects, bundles, herbs, prayer sticks, and Yei B'Chei adorned with eagle and owl feathers.
- FBI agents executed a search warrant in Santa Fe and found the items.

**Procedural History**
- Mr. Corrow was charged with trafficking in Native American cultural items (Count 1) and selling protected bird feathers (Count 2).
- The district court denied Mr. Corrow's pretrial motion to dismiss Count 1, which alleged the definition of "cultural patrimony" in NAGPRA was unconstitutionally vague.
- The jury convicted Mr. Corrow of Count 1 (illegal trafficking in cultural items).
- The jury acquitted Mr. Corrow of Count 2 (selling protected feathers) but found him guilty of the lesser included offense of possession of protected feathers.
- The district court denied Mr. Corrow's post-trial motion challenging the constitutionality of NAGPRA and the sufficiency of the evidence.
- Mr. Corrow was sentenced to two concurrent five-year probationary terms and 100 hours of community service.
- Mr. Corrow appealed to the Tenth Circuit.

**Issue**
Is the definition of "cultural patrimony" in the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA) unconstitutionally vague as applied to Mr. Corrow?

**Holding**
The government (appellee) wins. The definition of "cultural patrimony" in NAGPRA is not unconstitutionally vague as applied to Mr. Corrow. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Void-for-Vagueness Doctrine.** A penal statute must define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness for ordinary people to understand what conduct is prohibited and in a manner that does not encourage arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement.
- **Vagueness Challenges (Non-First Amendment).** Vagueness challenges to statutes not involving First Amendment freedoms must be examined in light of the facts of the case at hand.
- **Facial Challenge (Non-First Amendment).** In a facial challenge raising no First Amendment or other claim that the act reaches constitutionally protected conduct, the complainant must demonstrate that the law is impermissibly vague in all of its applications.
- **NAGPRA Definition of Cultural Patrimony.** An object is "cultural patrimony" if it has ongoing historical, traditional, or cultural importance central to the Native American group or culture itself, rather than property owned by an individual Native American, and which, therefore, cannot be alienated, appropriated, or conveyed by any individual regardless of whether or not the individual is a member of the Indian tribe or Native Hawaiian organization, and such object shall have been considered inalienable by such Native American group at the time the object was separated from such group.
- **Scienter Requirement.** A statutory requirement that an act must be willful or purposeful may mitigate a criminal law's vagueness by ensuring it punishes only those who are aware their conduct is unlawful.
- **Migratory Bird Treaty Act (MBTA) Misdemeanor Violations.** Misdemeanor violations under 16 U.S.C. § 703 are strict liability crimes, meaning proof of scienter is not required.

**Reasoning**
- Mr. Corrow cannot meet the burden of demonstrating the law is impermissibly vague in all its applications because his conduct falls squarely within the core of the Act.
- **Fair Notice:**
    - The district court found Mr. Corrow knowledgeable about Navajo traditions and culture and aware that tribal members viewed ownership differently.
    - Mr. Corrow had inquired about prices of Navajo artifacts from an author who expressed objections to commercial sales of cultural objects and reminded him of NAGPRA.
    - Mr. Corrow's representation that he wanted to buy the Yei B'Chei to pass them to another chanter suggests he understood their inherent inalienability in Navajo culture.
    - While experts differed on alienability, none testified it was acceptable to sell Yei B'Chei to non-Navajos for profit.
    - The Yei B'Chei were powerfully connected to Navajo religion and culture, and Mr. Corrow had notice of this.
    - The statute's scienter requirement ("knowingly sells, purchases, uses for profit") further ensures that only those aware of their unlawful conduct are punished.
- **Arbitrary and Discriminatory Enforcement:**
    - The statute, as applied, requires law enforcement officers to consult with tribal officials to determine if an item is cultural patrimony.
    - In this case, a National Park Service officer examined photographs and discussed the Yei B'Chei's significance with knowledgeable Park Service officers and Navajo Nation representatives before determining they were cultural patrimony.
    - This consultation process provides sufficient guidance to law enforcement, dispelling fears of subjective enforcement.
- The evidence was sufficient for a rational jury to find beyond a reasonable doubt that the Yei B'Chei are cultural patrimony.
- The court joins other circuits in holding that misdemeanor violations under MBTA § 703 are strict liability crimes, meaning scienter is not required for possession.
    - The plain language of § 703 makes simple possession unlawful.
    - The penalties are small, and there is no grave harm to an offender's reputation.
    - Even if scienter were required, the evidence showed Mr. Corrow knowingly possessed the feathers.

None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the **Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA)** and the concept of **cultural patrimony**. It highlights how a statute can define property rights in a way that prioritizes communal, inalienable cultural significance over individual ownership, even when individual tribal members might believe they have the right to alienate such items. This contrasts with traditional Western property law concepts of individual ownership and alienability. It also touches on the **void-for-vagueness doctrine** in criminal law, which is relevant to how property rights and restrictions are defined.
2. The conflicting expert testimony regarding the alienability of the Yei B'Chei within Navajo culture raises a tension: how should courts reconcile differing interpretations of cultural practices when applying a statute that relies on those practices for its definitions? What if the "group itself cannot agree on that point," as Mr. Corrow argued?

**Takeaway:** The court upheld the constitutionality of NAGPRA's definition of "cultural patrimony" against a vagueness challenge, emphasizing that the defendant had sufficient notice of the cultural significance and inalienability of the items, and that the statute's enforcement mechanisms provided adequate guidance.
