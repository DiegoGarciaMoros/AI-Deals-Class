## Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon (Supreme Court of the United States, 1922)

**Facts**
- In 1878, Pennsylvania Coal Company (PCC) conveyed the surface rights of a property to Mahon's predecessor in title.
- The deed expressly reserved PCC's right to remove all coal beneath the surface, and the grantee waived all claims for damages from such mining.
- Mahon acquired the surface rights to the property.
- In 1921, Pennsylvania enacted the Kohler Act, which made it unlawful to mine anthracite coal in a way that caused subsidence of, among other things, any structure used as a human habitation.
- PCC intended to mine under Mahon's property, which would cause subsidence of Mahon's house.
- PCC gave timely notice of its intent to mine.

**Procedural History**
- Mahon sued PCC in equity to enjoin mining that would cause subsidence.
- The Court of Common Pleas denied the injunction, finding the Kohler Act unconstitutional if applied to this case.
- The Supreme Court of Pennsylvania reversed, holding the statute was a legitimate exercise of police power.
- PCC appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court.

**Issue**
- Can a state law prohibit coal mining that causes surface subsidence, even when the coal company previously reserved the right to mine and the surface owner waived damages, without compensating the coal company?

**Holding**
- Pennsylvania Coal Company wins. The Kohler Act, as applied, is an unconstitutional taking of property without compensation. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Police Power Limits.** While property may be regulated to a certain extent under the police power, if regulation goes too far, it will be recognized as a taking requiring compensation.
- **Taking.** What makes the right to mine coal valuable is that it can be exercised with profit; to make it commercially impracticable to mine certain coal has very nearly the same effect for constitutional purposes as appropriating or destroying it.
- **Public Interest.** A strong public desire to improve the public condition is not enough to warrant achieving the desire by a shorter cut than the constitutional way of paying for the change.
- **Reciprocity of Advantage.** (Implicitly applied) Regulations that secure an "average reciprocity of advantage" among affected parties are more likely to be upheld.

**Reasoning**
- The Kohler Act destroys previously existing property and contract rights.
- While government can diminish property values to some extent without compensation, there are limits to this implied limitation on property rights.
    - The extent of diminution is a key factor; when it reaches a certain magnitude, it becomes a taking.
- This case involves a single private house, and the public interest in preventing damage to it is not sufficient to warrant such an extensive destruction of PCC's rights.
    - The damage is not a public nuisance because it is not common or public.
    - The statute's limited scope (not applying when the coal owner also owns the surface) suggests a limited public interest.
    - Protection of personal safety could be provided by notice, which PCC gave.
- The extent of the taking is great, abolishing a valuable estate in land (the right to mine coal) and a binding contract.
- The Act cannot be sustained as an exercise of police power where the right to mine coal has been reserved, because it makes mining commercially impracticable, which is equivalent to appropriating or destroying the coal.
- The public's rights in a street are only those it has paid for; if surface rights were acquired without support rights, the state cannot supply the latter without compensation.
- The Rent Cases (e.g., *Block v. Hirsh*) are distinguishable because they involved temporary emergencies and provided for reasonable compensation.

**Dissent (Brandeis)**
- The Kohler Act is a legitimate exercise of police power to prevent a noxious use of property.
- Restriction imposed to protect public health, safety, or morals from danger is not a taking; the state merely prevents a use that interferes with paramount public rights.
- The purpose of the restriction is public, even if private persons incidentally benefit.
- Keeping coal in place is an appropriate means to prevent subsidence, and it does not become inappropriate just because it deprives the owner of the only profitable use.
- The value of the restricted coal should be compared to the value of the whole property, not just the coal itself. Dividing property interests (surface and subsoil) does not increase the owner's rights against the public.
- If public safety is imperiled, neither grant nor contract can prevail against the police power.
- Reciprocity of advantage is not necessary when the police power is exercised to protect the public from detriment and danger, as opposed to conferring benefits.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is foundational for understanding the **Takings Clause** of the Fifth Amendment (applied to states via the Fourteenth Amendment). It establishes the concept of a "regulatory taking," where a government regulation goes "too far" and effectively takes private property, even without formally condemning it. This contrasts with earlier understandings where a physical invasion or direct appropriation was generally required for a taking. It sets up the tension between the state's police power and private property rights, a theme explored in cases like *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.* and *Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City*.
2. The "too far" test is notoriously vague. What constitutes "too far"? Is it a percentage of value lost, or the destruction of a "distinct investment-backed expectation"? The majority focuses on the diminution of value of the *mining rights* specifically, while the dissent argues for considering the value of the *entire property*. This difference in the "denominator" for the taking fraction is a persistent issue in takings jurisprudence.

**Takeaway:** A government regulation that goes "too far" in diminishing the value or use of private property will be considered a taking requiring just compensation, even if it does not involve a physical appropriation.
