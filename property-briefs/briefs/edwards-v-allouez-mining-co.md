## Edwards v. Allouez Mining Co. (Michigan Supreme Court, 1878)

**Facts**
- In 1874, Allouez Mining Company (defendant) built a stamp mill on Hill Creek at a cost of $60,000.
- The mill's operations caused large quantities of sand to be carried down the creek and deposited on downstream bottom lands.
- In 1875, Edwards (complainant) purchased a piece of land downstream from the mill, through which the creek ran, and upon which sand was being deposited.
- Edwards did not purchase the land for use or occupation, but for speculation, intending to force the defendant to buy it at a large advance.
- Edwards offered to sell the land to Allouez Mining Co. soon after purchase, valuing it at three to five times his purchase price.
- Allouez Mining Co. declined to purchase the land.
- Edwards filed a bill seeking an injunction to stop the defendant from depositing sand on his land and polluting the stream.

**Procedural History**
- The circuit court refused the injunction but ordered a reference to a jury for an assessment of damages.
- Complainant appealed.

**Issue**
Can a landowner obtain an injunction against a mining company for depositing sand on his land if he purchased the land solely for speculative purposes, intending to force the company to buy it?

**Holding**
Defendant (Allouez Mining Co.) wins. An injunction will not be granted when the complainant purchased the land for speculative purposes, and the injury can be adequately compensated by damages. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Injunctions are discretionary.** The allowance of an injunction is discretionary and not a matter of right.
- **Irreparable mischief.** Injunctions are intended to prevent irreparable mischief, where the consequences could not be adequately compensated by damages.
- **Balancing inconveniences.** A court considering an injunction will balance the inconveniences of granting or denying the writ.
- **Good faith.** The petitioner for an injunction must act in good faith.
- **Motive of the complainant.** If a complainant demands more than strict legal rights, their motives may become important.
- **Adequate legal remedy.** If the common law provides an adequate remedy in damages, equity will generally not intervene with an injunction.

**Reasoning**
- The defendant's operations constitute an appropriation of the complainant's property, for which the complainant is entitled to legal redress.
- However, an injunction is a powerful remedy that should not be lightly ordered, especially when it could force the defendant to purchase the complainant's land at an inflated price or sacrifice their valuable property.
- The purpose of an injunction is to prevent irreparable injury, which is not present here.
    - Irreparable injury typically involves destroying the comfortable occupation of a homestead, breaking up a business, or situations where damages are impossible to measure.
    - The land in question was not purchased for a homestead or business, but for speculation.
- The complainant bought the land with the preconceived purpose of forcing a sale to the defendant.
    - While motives generally don't affect lawful actions, they become relevant when seeking the "gracious ear of equity" for a special remedy beyond strict legal rights.
    - The complainant "invited an injury" by purchasing the land for this purpose.
- A legal award of damages would be a suitable and just redress for land bought for speculation, as it furthers the purpose of the purchase.
- The defendant is not alleged to be irresponsible, so damages are likely collectible.
- The elements of irreparable injury are entirely absent from this case.

**Dissent (Campbell, C. J.)**
- The defendant is continuously invading the complainant's freehold and rights in the water without color of right.
- Rights to equitable relief, when it is the only adequate remedy, are as absolute as rights to legal relief.
- No legal remedy is adequate for such a grievance because it cannot secure the complainant the use of his own property.
- The complainant's speculative motives should not lessen his claims to protection; it is a dangerous principle to hold that a civil wrong can be lessened by the motives of the injured party.
- Property is entitled to protection regardless of the owner's motives, otherwise private interests would be subject to judicial discretion.
- The court should have granted a perpetual injunction.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the limits of injunctive relief in property disputes, particularly when the plaintiff's motives are questionable. It connects to the idea of "coming to the nuisance" and the balancing of equities, which is also seen in cases like *Pile v. Pedrick* (where an injunction was granted despite high cost to defendant) and *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands* (where an injunction was denied due to minimal damage and high cost). The court's focus on the plaintiff's motive and the adequacy of damages contrasts with a strict property rule approach that would prioritize the owner's right to exclude, as advocated by the dissent.
2. The case raises the question of whether a property owner's subjective intent or motive in acquiring property should influence the remedies available for trespass or nuisance. Does this open the door for courts to judge the "worthiness" of property owners?

**Takeaway:** Courts may deny injunctive relief for property invasions if the plaintiff's primary motive for acquiring the property was speculative and damages can adequately compensate the injury, balancing the equities between the parties.
