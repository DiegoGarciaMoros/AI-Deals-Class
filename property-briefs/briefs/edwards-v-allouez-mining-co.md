## Edwards v. Allouez Mining Co. (Michigan Supreme Court, 1878)

**Facts**
- In 1874, the defendant, Allouez Mining Company, built a stamp mill on the banks of Hill Creek at a cost of about $60,000.
- The mill's operations caused large quantities of sand to be carried down the creek and deposited on bottom lands below.
- In 1875, the plaintiff, Thomas W. Edwards, bought a piece of land downstream from the mill, through which the creek ran.
- The land was purchased for speculation, with the expectation of forcing the defendant to buy it at a higher price.
- The plaintiff offered to sell the land to the defendant, valuing it at three to five times his purchase price.
- The defendant declined to purchase the land.
- The plaintiff then filed a bill seeking an injunction.

**Procedural History**
- The circuit court refused the injunction but ordered a reference to a jury for an assessment of damages.
- The complainant appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a landowner obtain an injunction to stop a mining company from depositing sand on his land when he bought the land for speculative purposes, intending to force the company to buy it?

**Holding**
- Defendant (Allouez Mining Co.) wins. An injunction will not be granted when the complainant's purpose in acquiring the property was to force a sale, and monetary damages are an adequate remedy. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Injunctions are discretionary.** The allowance of an injunction is discretionary and not a matter of right.
- **Irreparable mischief.** Injunctions are intended to prevent irreparable mischief, meaning consequences that cannot be adequately compensated by damages.
- **Balancing inconveniences.** Courts will balance the inconveniences of granting or denying an injunction, and may withhold it if it would inflict greater injury than the grievance complained of.
- **Motive of petitioner.** Where a petitioner invites an injury and then seeks an equitable remedy, their motives can be inquired into, even if they ground themselves on a strict legal right.
- **Adequacy of legal remedy.** If the common law provides adequate redress for an injury, equity will not intervene.
- **Rejected: Absolute right to equitable relief.** The right to equitable relief, when it is the only adequate remedy, is as absolute as the right to legal relief.

**Reasoning**
- The defendant's operations constitute a legal injury to the plaintiff's property, for which he is entitled to redress.
- However, an injunction is a powerful remedy that should not be lightly ordered.
- Granting the injunction would force the defendant to either purchase the plaintiff's land at his price or sacrifice a valuable mill.
- The plaintiff bought the land for speculation, with the preconceived purpose of forcing the defendant to buy it.
- The plaintiff's motive in acquiring the land is relevant when he seeks the "special interposition of equity" rather than just strict legal rights.
- The injury to the plaintiff's land is not "irreparable" because the land was bought to make money from its sale, and a legal award of damages would further this purpose.
- The defendant is not alleged to be irresponsible, and a jury can award reasonable damages.
- The elements of irreparable injury are absent from this case.

**Dissent (Campbell, C. J.)**
- The defendant is continuously invading the complainant's freehold by depositing sand and polluting the stream, which is equivalent to taking or destroying his property.
- The right to equitable relief, when it is the only adequate remedy, is as absolute as the right to legal relief.
- No legal remedy is adequate for such a grievance because no legal remedy can secure the complainant the use of his own property.
- The complainant's speculative motives for purchasing the land should not lessen his claim to protection, as a civil wrong should not be lessened by the injured party's motives.
- Holding otherwise would make private interests subject to judicial discretion and make the judicial conscience the only arbiter of rights.

**NOTES:**
1. This case highlights the equitable doctrine of **clean hands** and the court's discretion in granting injunctive relief, especially when the plaintiff's motives are questionable. It contrasts with cases like *Pile v. Pedrick* where an injunction was granted despite significant cost to the defendant, suggesting a different approach to property rights and remedies. The court's balancing of conveniences here is also relevant to *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C.*, which discusses the four-factor test for injunctive relief.
2. The dissent argues that property rights should not be subject to judicial discretion based on the owner's motives, raising the question of whether a property owner's intent should ever diminish their right to protection against trespass or nuisance.

**Takeaway:** Courts may deny injunctive relief for property invasions if the plaintiff's primary motive for acquiring the property was to leverage the defendant's operations for a speculative sale, and monetary damages are deemed an adequate remedy.
