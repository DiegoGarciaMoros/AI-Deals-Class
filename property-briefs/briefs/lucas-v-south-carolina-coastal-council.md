## Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council (Supreme Court of the United States, 1992)

**Facts**
- In 1986, David Lucas purchased two beachfront lots on the Isle of Palms, South Carolina, for $976,000, intending to build single-family homes.
- At the time of purchase, the lots were zoned for single-family residential construction, and there were no restrictions on such use.
- In 1988, the South Carolina Legislature enacted the Beachfront Management Act.
- The Act established a "baseline" landward of Lucas's parcels and prohibited the construction of permanent habitable structures seaward of a line 20 feet landward of this baseline.
- The Act's prohibition rendered Lucas's parcels "valueless" according to a state trial court.

**Procedural History**
- Lucas sued in the South Carolina Court of Common Pleas, arguing the Act constituted a taking without just compensation.
- The trial court found a taking and ordered compensation of $1,232,387.50.
- The South Carolina Supreme Court reversed, finding that the Act was designed to prevent serious public harm, and thus no compensation was due.
- Lucas appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court, which granted certiorari.

**Issue**
Does a state regulation that deprives a landowner of all economically beneficial use of their property constitute a taking requiring just compensation, even if the regulation is intended to prevent serious public harm?

**Holding**
Lucas (petitioner) wins. A state regulation that deprives a landowner of all economically beneficial use of their property constitutes a taking requiring just compensation, unless the proscribed use was already prohibited by background principles of the state's law of property and nuisance. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Categorical Regulatory Taking.** When a regulation deprives land of all economically beneficial or productive use, it is a categorical taking requiring just compensation.
- **Physical Invasion Rule.** Regulations that compel a property owner to suffer a physical invasion of their property are categorical takings.
- **Noxious Use/Harm-Preventing Principle (Rejected as sole justification).** The government may not avoid compensation for a total taking by simply asserting that the regulation prevents a "harmful or noxious use" of property, as the distinction between harm-preventing and benefit-conferring regulation is often subjective.
- **Background Principles of Nuisance and Property Law Exception.** A state may resist compensation for a regulation that deprives land of all economically beneficial use only if the proscribed use interests were not part of the owner's title to begin with, meaning they were already prohibited by background principles of the state's law of property and nuisance.
- **Legislative Declaration Insufficient.** A state cannot avoid compensation for a total taking merely by declaring that the prohibited uses are inconsistent with the public interest or violate a common-law maxim; it must identify specific background principles of nuisance and property law that prohibit the intended uses.

**Reasoning**
- The Court has recognized two categories of regulatory action that are compensable without a case-specific inquiry into the public interest: physical invasions and regulations that deny all economically beneficial or productive use of land.
- Total deprivation of beneficial use is, from the landowner's perspective, equivalent to a physical appropriation.
- Regulations that leave land economically idle carry a heightened risk that private property is being pressed into public service under the guise of mitigating public harm.
- The "harmful or noxious uses" principle was an early attempt to explain why government could affect property values without compensation, but the distinction between "harm-preventing" and "benefit-conferring" regulation is often subjective.
- The legislature's declaration that a use is harmful cannot be the sole basis for departing from the categorical rule that total regulatory takings must be compensated.
- To avoid compensation for a total taking, the state must show that the proscribed use was never part of the owner's title, based on background principles of state property and nuisance law.
- This inquiry involves analyzing the degree of harm to public resources or adjacent property, the social value of the activities, their suitability to the locality, and the ease of avoiding harm.
- It is unlikely that common-law principles would prevent the erection of any habitable or productive improvements on Lucas's land, as they rarely prohibit the "essential use" of land.
- The case is ripe for review because the South Carolina Supreme Court decided the case on the merits, and Lucas had no reason to pursue a "temporary taking" theory at trial when the Act was permanent.

**Dissent (Blackmun)**
- The Court should not have reviewed the case because Lucas's claim was not ripe, as he had not sought a special permit under the amended Act or challenged the administrative determination of his property's status.
- The trial court's finding that the property was "valueless" was erroneous, as Lucas still retained other attributes of ownership (e.g., right to exclude, recreational uses, right to alienate).
- The Court improperly shifted the burden of proof to the state to justify its legislative judgments, departing from the traditional presumption of constitutionality.
- The new categorical rule for total takings is inconsistent with prior precedent, which consistently weighed public and private interests even when regulations had significant economic effects.
- The "background principles of nuisance and property law" exception is unworkable because the distinction between harm-preventing and benefit-conferring is subjective, and common-law nuisance principles are vague and manipulable.
- The Court's historical compact argument is flawed; early American law often permitted extensive regulation of property for public benefit without compensation, and the Takings Clause was not initially understood to apply to regulations.

**Dissent (Stevens)**
- The Court should have exercised judicial restraint and avoided premature adjudication of a constitutional question, as Lucas had not exhausted administrative remedies under the amended Act.
- The categorical rule for total regulatory takings is unsound and unwise, as it is arbitrary (e.g., 95% diminution gets nothing, 100% gets full value) and will lead to manipulation of property definitions by developers.
- The Court's justification for the categorical rule is weak; the landowner's perspective of total deprivation is not sufficient, the rarity of total takings does not justify a per se rule, and the risk of "singling out" is not necessarily correlated with total takings.
- The nuisance exception is too rigid and narrow, effectively freezing the state's common law and denying legislatures their traditional power to adapt law to changing circumstances (e.g., environmental concerns).
- The Court's approach conflicts with the ad hoc, factual inquiry required for takings analysis, which involves weighing fairness and justice and balancing private and public interests.
- The generality of the Beachfront Management Act, which regulated the entire coastline and affected developed and undeveloped land alike, indicates it was not an effort to expropriate specific owners.
- The compelling purpose of the Act to protect life and property from storm damage, combined with the generality of the regulation and the inherent risks of beachfront investment, means the Act should not be considered a taking.

**Statement (Souter)**
- The writ of certiorari should be dismissed as improvidently granted because the unreviewable assumption that the property was rendered valueless is questionable and frustrates the Court's ability to clarify the legal premises.
- The concept of "total deprivation" is uncertain under existing law, and the Court's indirect approach to defining it through exceptions is problematic.
- Nuisance law typically focuses on conduct and usually leaves property owners with other reasonable uses, making it unlikely that nuisance abatement would result in a complete deprivation of all beneficial land use.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a landmark in **regulatory takings** jurisprudence, establishing a bright-line rule for when a regulation goes "too far" and requires compensation. It builds on Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon by defining a specific scenario where a regulation is a per se taking, rather than relying solely on the ad hoc balancing test. It contrasts with Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City, which articulated the multi-factor balancing test for partial regulatory takings, by creating a categorical rule for total deprivations.
2. The "background principles of nuisance and property law" exception is a key area for discussion. How broadly or narrowly should courts interpret these "background principles"? Could a state legislature effectively codify new "nuisance" laws to avoid compensation for future regulations?

**Takeaway:** A regulation that deprives a landowner of all economically beneficial use of their property is a categorical taking unless the prohibited use was already unlawful under existing state property and nuisance law.
