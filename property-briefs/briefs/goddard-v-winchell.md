## Goddard v. Winchell (Iowa Supreme Court, 1892)

**Facts**
- Since 1857, John Goddard owned land in Winnebago County, Iowa.
- On May 2, 1890, an aerolite weighing about 66 pounds fell onto Goddard's land and buried itself three feet deep.
- The next day, Peter Hoagland dug up the aerolite with a spade in the presence of Goddard's tenant, Elickson.
- Hoagland took the aerolite to his house and claimed ownership.
- On May 5, 1890, Hoagland sold the aerolite to H.V. Winchell for $105.
- Winchell took possession of the aerolite.

**Procedural History**
- The district court found for the plaintiff, Goddard.
- The defendant, Winchell, appealed.

**Issue**
- Does an aerolite that falls from the sky and buries itself in land belong to the landowner or to the person who discovers and digs it up?

**Holding**
- Plaintiff (Goddard) wins. An aerolite that falls and becomes embedded in the soil becomes part of the land and belongs to the landowner. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Quicquid plantatur solo, solo cedit (Whatever is affixed to the soil belongs to the soil).** This rule means that anything permanently attached to the land becomes part of the realty.
- **Rejected: Title by Occupancy.** This rule states that occupancy is taking possession of things that belonged to nobody, and movables found on the surface of the earth and unclaimed by any owner belong to the first occupant or finder.
- **Rejected: Finder's Keepers (Lost or Abandoned Property).** The finder of lost or abandoned property is the owner against all the world except the former owner.

**Reasoning**
- The aerolite became a part of the soil because it came to its position through natural causes and was embedded three feet deep.
- It was a "natural deposit" and not a "movable thing 'on the earth'" but "in the earth."
- The aerolite's substance, a stone, was not foreign or unnatural to the soil.
- The rule of "title by occupancy" applies to movables found "on the surface of the earth or in the sea," not to things embedded in the earth.
- The aerolite was never lost or abandoned, so the rules for lost or abandoned property do not apply.
- The court analogizes the aerolite to other natural deposits like boulders and drift left by glaciers, which belong to the landowner.
- The fact that the aerolite may have scientific value does not change its ownership; the landowner is as capable of contributing to scientific advancement as the finder.
- The court acknowledges the uniqueness of the case and the lack of exact precedent but finds its conclusion most analogous to generally accepted rules of law.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a classic example of the **accession** doctrine, where something becomes part of a larger, more valuable thing. It contrasts with cases like *Pierson v. Post* or *Keeble v. Hickeringill* which deal with the capture of wild animals (ferae naturae) and the concept of first possession for unowned things. Here, the court determines the aerolite was not "unowned" once it became embedded in the land. It also touches on the distinction between chattels and realty, a theme seen in cases involving fixtures.
2. What if the aerolite had just bounced off the surface and was lying on top of the grass? Would it still belong to Goddard, or would it be considered a "movable" subject to the finder's rule?

**Takeaway:** Things that become naturally embedded in the land are considered part of the realty and belong to the landowner, not the finder.
