## Moore v. Regents of the University of California (Supreme Court of California, 1990)

**Facts**
- In October 1976, John Moore (Plaintiff) sought treatment for hairy-cell leukemia at UCLA Medical Center.
- Dr. David Golde (Defendant), Moore's physician, confirmed the diagnosis and recommended a splenectomy, which Moore consented to.
- Before the splenectomy, Golde and researcher Shirley Quan (Defendant) planned to obtain portions of Moore's spleen for research unrelated to his medical care, but did not inform Moore.
- On October 20, 1976, surgeons removed Moore's spleen.
- Between November 1976 and September 1983, Moore made several follow-up visits at Golde's direction, during which Golde withdrew additional samples of blood, bone marrow, skin, and sperm, representing these were necessary for Moore's health.
- During this period, defendants conducted research on Moore's cells and planned to financially benefit from exploiting them, concealing these activities from Moore.
- Sometime before August 1979, Golde established a cell line from Moore's T-lymphocytes.
- On January 30, 1981, the Regents of the University of California (Defendant) applied for a patent on the cell line, listing Golde and Quan as inventors, with a policy for them to share in royalties.
- On March 20, 1984, the patent was issued to the Regents, naming Golde and Quan as inventors.
- Golde negotiated commercial development agreements with Genetics Institute, Inc. (Defendant) and Sandoz Pharmaceuticals Corporation (Defendant), becoming a paid consultant and acquiring stock rights, with payments also made to the Regents.

**Procedural History**
- Moore filed a complaint alleging 13 causes of action, including conversion, breach of fiduciary duty, and lack of informed consent.
- The superior court sustained all defendants' demurrers to the entire complaint, reasoning that the conversion claim was defective and the other claims incorporated those defects.
- The superior court later sustained demurrers from Genetics Institute and Sandoz without leave to amend, finding no cause of action for conversion and secondary liability allegations too conclusory.
- The Court of Appeal reversed, holding that the complaint stated a cause of action for conversion, but agreed the allegations against Genetics Institute and Sandoz were insufficient, directing leave to amend. The Court of Appeal also directed the superior court to rule on the remaining causes of action.
- Defendants appealed to the Supreme Court of California.

**Issue**
Can a patient sue his physician and other parties for conversion of his cells used in medical research without his permission, or for breach of fiduciary duty and lack of informed consent due to undisclosed research and economic interests?

**Holding**
Moore (Plaintiff) wins in part. The complaint states a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty and lack of informed consent, but not for conversion. Affirmed in part and reversed in part.

**Rules**
- **Informed Consent.** A physician has a fiduciary duty to disclose all information material to a patient's decision to consent to medical treatment, including personal interests unrelated to the patient's health (research or economic) that may affect professional judgment.
- **Conversion.** To establish conversion, a plaintiff must prove actual interference with their ownership or right of possession of personal property.
    - Rejected: A patient retains sufficient ownership interest in excised cells to support a cause of action for conversion.
    - Rejected: The tort of conversion should be extended to cover the use of human cells in medical research.

**Reasoning**
- **Breach of Fiduciary Duty and Lack of Informed Consent:**
    - A physician must disclose personal interests (research or economic) unrelated to the patient's health that may affect their professional judgment.
    - Failure to disclose such interests can lead to a cause of action for performing medical procedures without informed consent or breach of fiduciary duty.
    - Moore alleged Golde had undisclosed research interests in his cells before the splenectomy and concealed economic interests during postoperative procedures.
    - These allegations are sufficient to state a cause of action against Golde.
    - The other defendants (Regents, Quan, Genetics Institute, Sandoz) are not physicians and did not have a direct fiduciary duty to Moore; their liability would depend on secondary liability theories.
- **Conversion:**
    - Moore's claim for conversion fails because he does not retain an ownership interest in his excised cells.
        - No reported judicial decision supports such a claim.
        - California statutory law (Health and Safety Code § 7054.4) drastically limits a patient's control over excised cells by requiring their disposal after scientific use, eliminating many rights ordinarily attached to property.
        - The patented cell line and products derived from it are factually and legally distinct from Moore's original cells, as the patent rewards inventive effort, not the discovery of naturally occurring raw materials.
    - Extending conversion liability to this context is inappropriate due to policy considerations:
        - It would threaten socially useful medical research with disabling civil liability, as conversion is a strict liability tort that would impact innocent researchers.
        - Uncertainty about legal title to biological materials would hinder research and product development in the biotechnology industry.
        - The issue is better suited for legislative resolution, given the complex policy choices involved and the legislature's ability to gather evidence and hear from all interested parties.
        - The tort of conversion is not necessary to protect patients' rights, as the existing fiduciary duty and informed consent theories adequately protect patients by requiring full disclosure.

**Concurrence (Arabian, J.)**
- Joins the majority but emphasizes the moral issue of treating human tissue as a commercial commodity.
- Believes the question of whether cells should be treated as property is not for the courts to decide due to the profound moral, philosophical, and religious values at stake.
- Suggests the Legislature is the proper forum to create a licensing scheme for profit sharing.

**Concurring and Dissenting (Broussard, J.)**
- Concurs with the breach of fiduciary duty holding, noting the unusual allegations that the doctor knew the cells' value *before* removal.
- Dissents on the conversion claim, arguing that a patient has a right to determine the use of a body part *before* its removal.
- The Uniform Anatomical Gift Act supports the principle of "donor control" over the use of donated body parts.
- Traditional conversion principles protect against unauthorized use or interference with the right to control property.
- The majority's policy concerns about hindering research are overstated and do not justify abrogating all conversion liability, especially against parties who directly misappropriate a patient's control over their body parts.

**Dissent (Mosk, J.)**
- Disagrees with the majority's conclusion that Moore has no cause of action for conversion.
- Argues that Moore retained an ownership interest in his cells, as "property" is a broad concept, and limitations on certain rights do not destroy ownership.
- California statutory law does not preclude Moore's ownership; Health and Safety Code § 7054.4's "scientific use" does not encompass commercial exploitation.
- The patented cell line is not so factually or legally distinct from Moore's cells to negate his claim, especially since the unauthorized use began before the patent.
- Policy considerations weigh in favor of recognizing conversion:
    - It respects the human body and prevents unjust enrichment of researchers at the patient's expense.
    - The fear of hindering research is overstated; proper record-keeping can mitigate liability, and the biotechnology industry already restricts access to cell lines through patents and trade secrets.
- The Legislature's competence does not relieve courts of their duty to fashion remedies. Existing statutes on organ and blood sales do not prohibit compensation for tissue used in research.
- The nondisclosure cause of action is inadequate because it is difficult to prove causation, only grants a right to refuse consent (not to share in profits), and fails to reach all parties involved in commercial exploitation.
- Criticizes the majority's inclusion of "amateur biology lecture" as improper and irrelevant.

**Takeaway:** While a patient does not retain property rights in excised biological materials for conversion claims, physicians have a fiduciary duty to disclose all personal research and economic interests that may influence medical decisions, ensuring informed consent.
