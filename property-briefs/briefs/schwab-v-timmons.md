## Schwab v. Timmons (Wisconsin Supreme Court, 1999)

**Facts**
- Before 1854, the United States owned Lots 2, 3, and 4, which were located between Green Bay and a bluff.
- In 1854, the United States granted Lot 4 to Ingebret Torgerson but retained Lots 2 and 3. At this time, the lots extended to a public roadway above the bluff.
- In 1882, the United States granted Lots 2 and 3 to Halvor Anderson.
- At some point, Lots 2, 3, and 4 were subdivided into parcels.
- The Schwabs' parents purchased their parcels in Lot 2 in the 1940s. These parcels extended from Green Bay to above the bluff, with access to a public roadway.
- In 1965 and 1974, the Schwabs inherited these parcels.
- After 1974, the Schwabs conveyed the property above the bluff to relatives, retaining the parcel below the bluff.
- McCormick inherited her parcel, which also originally included land above and below the bluff with highway access. She conveyed the property above the bluff to a third party, retaining the parcel below.
- The petitioners' parcels are now bordered by water on the west and a bluff on the east, making them landlocked.
- A private road runs north from Hobler's parcel across the respondents' properties, terminating on the Lenz parcel.
- Negotiations for the petitioners to extend this private road to their properties failed.
- In 1988, the petitioners requested the Village of Ephraim to extend a public road to their properties, but the request was denied.

**Procedural History**
- Petitioners (Schwabs and McCormick) brought a declaratory judgment action seeking an easement by necessity or implication.
- The circuit court granted the respondents' motions to dismiss, concluding that the historical circumstances did not fit typical situations for implied easements and that any easement would not have survived due to lack of recording or notice.
- The court of appeals summarily affirmed the circuit court's decision.
- Petitioners appealed to the Wisconsin Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Can landowners claim an easement by necessity or implication over neighboring properties when their parcels became landlocked due to their own actions in conveying away their highway access, or due to geographical barriers and historical conveyances by the federal government?

**Holding**
Defendant (respondents) wins. The petitioners are not entitled to an easement by implication or necessity because their parcels were not landlocked at the time of the original conveyances by the United States, and they created their own landlocked parcels by conveying away their highway access. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Easement by Implication.** An easement by implication arises when there has been a separation of title, a use before separation that was so obvious and continuous as to show it was meant to be permanent, and the easement is necessary for the beneficial enjoyment of the land granted or retained.
- **Necessity for Implied Easement.** The necessity for an implied easement must be "so clear and absolute that without the easement the grantee cannot enjoy the use of the property granted to him for the purposes to which similar property is customarily devoted."
- **Easement by Necessity.** An easement of necessity arises where an owner severs a landlocked portion of their property by conveying such parcel to another.
- **Elements of Easement by Necessity.** To establish an easement by necessity, a party must show common ownership of the two parcels prior to severance of the landlocked parcel, and that the owner of the now landlocked parcel cannot access a public roadway from their own property.
- **Location of Easement by Necessity.** If established, an easement by necessity will be implied over the land retained by the grantor.
- **Rejected: Geographical Barriers for Easement by Necessity.** Wisconsin courts have not recognized geographical barriers alone as circumstances warranting an easement by necessity.
- **Way of Necessity is Not Merely Convenience.** A way of necessity is not merely one of convenience, and the law will not imply such a way where another method for obtaining access at a reasonable expense exists.
- **Grantor Cannot Claim Easement by Necessity Over Grantee.** An easement by necessity exists where an owner sells a landlocked parcel to another, recognizing a way of necessity in the grantee over the land retained by the grantor.
- **Public Policy Against Implied Covenants.** Public policy in Wisconsin strongly opposes the implication of covenants of conveyance, including easements.
- **Reliance on Public Records.** A buyer of land may rely on public records as information of all conveyances and on the words of instruments for all rights thereunder.
- **Sources of Information for Land Rights.** A purchaser of real estate has three sources of information: reviewing the chain of title, searching other public records, and inspecting the land itself.
- **Notice of Easement.** A purchaser of land without knowledge or actual or constructive notice of an easement takes title relieved of the burden of the easement.

**Reasoning**
- The petitioners failed to establish an easement by implication because they did not allege that any use by the United States was so obvious, manifest, or continuous as to show it was meant to be permanent.
- The petitioners failed to establish an easement by necessity because the parcels were not landlocked at the time of the original conveyances by the United States.
    - At the time the United States conveyed Lot 4 in 1854, the eastern boundary of the lots extended to a public roadway above the bluff, providing access.
    - The United States never severed a landlocked portion of its property that was inaccessible from a public roadway.
- The court rejected the argument that geographical barriers alone warrant an easement by necessity.
    - A way of necessity is not merely for convenience; other methods of access, even if less convenient or more expensive, preclude an easement by necessity.
    - The petitioners had access to a public road, which they sold off, creating their own landlocked situation.
- The petitioners are grantors, not grantees, of the conveyances that resulted in their landlocked property, which is contrary to the principle of an easement by necessity being implied over the grantor's retained land.
- Granting an easement in this case would be contrary to Wisconsin's public policy against encumbrances and the reliance on recording and conveyance statutes.
    - It would sanction "hidden easements" not apparent from public records or inspection.
    - The respondents had no actual or constructive notice of any potential easement from the 1854 conveyance.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case reinforces the **strong presumption against implied easements** and the importance of the **recording acts** in Wisconsin. It connects to cases like *Miller v. Hoeschler* and *Backhausen v. Mayer* in its emphasis on the disfavor of implied easements and the need for clear notice. It also highlights the principle that a party cannot create their own necessity and then demand an easement, similar to how a party cannot claim necessity if they have other reasonable access, as seen in *Ludke v. Egan*.
2. What if the original conveyance by the United States had explicitly reserved an easement for future access to Lots 2 and 3, but this reservation was not properly recorded? Would the current landowners be bound by it, or would the lack of recording defeat the claim?

**Takeaway:** Courts are reluctant to grant easements by necessity or implication, especially when the necessity is self-created or when it would undermine the principles of notice and reliance on public records.
