## Stop the Beach Renourishment Inc. v. Florida Department of Environmental Protection (Supreme Court of the United States, 2010)

**Facts**
- In Florida, the State owns the land permanently submerged beneath navigable waters and the foreshore (land between low-tide and mean high-water lines). The mean high-water line is the boundary between private beachfront property and state-owned land.
- Littoral owners have special property rights, including the right to receive accretions (gradual additions of land) and relictions (land exposed by receding water).
- If land is added by avulsion (sudden change), the boundary does not change, and the new land belongs to the owner of the seabed (usually the State).
- In 1961, Florida passed the Beach and Shore Preservation Act, which allows for beach restoration projects where sand is deposited on eroded beaches.
- When a project is undertaken, an "erosion control line" is set, which replaces the fluctuating mean high-water line as the boundary between private and state property. Littoral owners retain all common-law riparian rights except the right to accretions.
- In 2003, the city of Destin and Walton County applied for permits to restore 6.9 miles of beach, which would add about 75 feet of dry sand seaward of the mean high-water line (the new erosion-control line).
- Stop the Beach Renourishment, Inc. (Petitioner), a nonprofit of beachfront property owners, challenged the project.

**Procedural History**
- Petitioner's administrative challenge was unsuccessful.
- Petitioner challenged the Department's approval in state court. The District Court of Appeal set aside the order, finding it eliminated two littoral rights (right to accretions, right to contact with water) and would be an unconstitutional taking. It remanded for a showing that local governments owned or had property interests in the upland property.
- The Florida Supreme Court answered a certified question in the negative, finding the Act did not unconstitutionally deprive upland owners of littoral rights. It quashed the remand, reasoning that the doctrine of avulsion applied and the right to accretions was a future contingent interest.
- Petitioner sought rehearing, arguing the Florida Supreme Court's decision itself effected a taking. Rehearing was denied.
- The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari.

**Issue**
Can a state supreme court's decision that redefines property rights constitute a taking under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments, and if so, did the Florida Supreme Court's decision do so here?

**Holding**
Defendant (Florida Department of Environmental Protection) wins. The Florida Supreme Court's decision did not contravene established property rights under Florida law, so no taking occurred. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Takings Clause.** The Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applied to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, prohibits the taking of private property for public use without just compensation.
- **Judicial Takings.** The Takings Clause bars the State from taking private property without paying for it, no matter which branch of government (legislative, executive, or judicial) is the instrument of the taking.
- **Judicial Taking Standard.** A state effects a taking if it declares that what was once an established right of private property no longer exists.
- **State Law Defines Property.** State law generally defines property interests, including rights in navigable waters and submerged lands.
- **Accretion vs. Avulsion (Florida Law).** In Florida, littoral owners automatically take title to dry land added by gradual accretion. However, land that becomes dry by sudden avulsion continues to belong to the owner of the seabed (usually the State), and the boundary does not change.
- **State's Right to Fill (Florida Law).** The State, as owner of submerged land, has the right to fill that land, provided it does not interfere with public rights or littoral owners' rights.
- **State-Caused Avulsion (Florida Law).** When the State fills its own seabed, and this causes a sudden exposure of previously submerged land, it is treated like an avulsion for ownership purposes, and the land belongs to the State. The right to accretions is subordinate to the State's right to fill.
- Rejected: **Unpredictability Test for Judicial Taking.** What counts is not whether there is precedent for the allegedly confiscatory decision, but whether the property right allegedly taken was established. A decision that clarifies previously unclear entitlements is not a taking.

**Reasoning**
- **Judicial Takings are Possible:**
    - The Takings Clause is concerned with the act of taking, not the governmental actor.
    - It would be absurd to allow a State to do by judicial decree what the Takings Clause forbids by legislative fiat.
    - Precedent (e.g., *Webb's Fabulous Pharmacies, Inc. v. Beckwith*) supports that judicial recharacterization of private property as public property can be a taking.
- **No Taking Occurred Here:**
    - Petitioner must show that, before the Florida Supreme Court's decision, littoral owners had rights to future accretions and contact with the water superior to the State's right to fill its submerged land.
    - Florida law allows the State to fill its own seabed.
    - The resulting sudden exposure of previously submerged land is treated like an avulsion, meaning the land belongs to the State and the boundary does not change.
    - The right to accretions is subordinate to the State's right to fill.
    - The Florida Supreme Court's decision was consistent with these background principles, particularly *Martin v. Busch*, which held that land exposed by the State draining a lakebed continued to belong to the State.
    - The Florida Supreme Court did not abolish the right to future accretions but held it was not implicated because the doctrine of avulsion applied.
    - Petitioner's claim of a separate right to continuous contact with the water is rejected because Florida law does not recognize an independent right of contact separate from the right of access, and such a right would contradict avulsion law.
    - The Takings Clause protects property rights as established under state law, not as they might have been or ought to have been established.

**Concurrence (Kennedy)**
- Agrees with the outcome but argues the Court should not decide whether judicial decisions can violate the Takings Clause.
- The Due Process Clause (substantive and procedural) would likely prevent a State from doing by judicial decree what the Takings Clause forbids by legislative fiat.
- Courts are not designed to make policy decisions about expropriation and are not politically accountable like legislatures or the executive.
- Recognizing judicial takings could give courts more power, not less, by allowing them to make sweeping changes to property law knowing the State would pay compensation.
- Historically, the Takings Clause applied to physical appropriations by legislatures, not judicial decisions.
- There are practical difficulties: how to raise such a claim, and what remedy a reviewing court could enter (likely only damages, not invalidation of the state court's judgment).

**Concurrence (Breyer)**
- Agrees with the outcome but argues the plurality unnecessarily addresses constitutional questions better left for another day.
- Deciding whether judicial decisions can be takings and establishing the standard for such takings invites a host of federal takings claims without mature consideration of procedural or substantive legal principles.
- This approach threatens to open federal courts to review many state-law property cases, an area primarily of state interest and expertise.
- Courts can resolve cases without specifying the precise standard for winning or losing.

**NOTES:**
1. This case establishes the controversial doctrine of "judicial takings," where a state court's redefinition of property rights can be a Fifth Amendment taking. It connects to *Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council* by emphasizing that the Takings Clause protects property rights as defined by state law, and a regulation (or judicial decision) that merely applies background principles of state property law is not a taking. It also reinforces the idea that the source of a property right (common law or statute) does not matter as much as its existence.
2. The plurality's assertion that the Takings Clause applies to judicial action is a significant expansion. An open question is how federal courts will apply the "established property right" standard, especially given the common law's inherent flexibility and evolution. How much change is too much change before a court's decision becomes a "taking" rather than a clarification or incremental modification?

**Takeaway:** A state court's decision can constitute a taking if it eliminates an established private property right, but the Florida Supreme Court's decision here did not do so because it was consistent with existing state property law regarding avulsion and the State's right to fill submerged lands.
