## Mullendore Theatres Inc. v. Growth Realty Investors Co. (Washington Court of Appeals, 1984)

**Facts**
- In 1969, Conner Theatres Corporation leased part of the Jones Building and provided a $22,500 security deposit.
- The lease stated that the deposit would be applied to damages if the tenant defaulted, or returned to the tenant at the lease's expiration if there was no default.
- The lease also stated that all covenants would run with the land.
- Before 1974, the original landlord transferred the property to North Pacific World Trade Center, Ltd.
- In 1974, Conner assigned its leasehold interest to Mullendore Theatres, Inc., and the security deposit was reduced to $6,000.
- In 1975, Growth Realty acquired the property when North Pacific defaulted on a note and deed of trust.
- Growth later sold the property to the City of Tacoma.
- The City was concerned about the potential $6,000 liability for the security deposit and wanted to reduce the purchase price by that amount.
- Growth agreed to indemnify the City for any liability related to the security deposit.
- In 1980, Mullendore negotiated a new lease with the City and released any claims against the City for the security deposit, but reserved claims against others.
- Mullendore then sued Growth for the return of the security deposit.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court held that the covenant to refund the security deposit ran with the land and bound Growth through the indemnification clause.
- Growth appealed.

**Issue**
- Does a landlord's covenant to refund a tenant's security deposit run with the land, obligating a successor landlord to refund it, when the lease permits but does not require the deposit to be used for the benefit of the leased property?

**Holding**
- Defendant (Growth Realty Investors Co.) wins. A landlord's covenant to refund a security deposit does not run with the land if the lease does not restrict the use of the deposit for the benefit of the property. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Covenant Running with the Land.** A lease covenant runs with the land only if it "touches or concerns" the land.
- **Touch and Concern Test.** To touch or concern the land, a covenant must be so related to the land as to enhance its value and confer a benefit upon it.
- **Collateral and Personal Obligation.** If a covenant does not touch or concern the land, it is a collateral and personal obligation of the original lessor.
- **Promise to Pay Money.** For a promise to pay money to be a running covenant, it must restrict the use of the funds to the benefit of the property.
- **Intent to Run.** Intent alone is not sufficient to make a covenant run with the land if its nature is personal.

**Reasoning**
- The covenant to refund the security deposit did not "touch or concern" the land.
    - The landlord was not required to spend the deposit for repairs, maintenance, or any other purpose related to the property.
    - The landlord was not required to transfer the deposit to successors.
    - The covenant was not directly related to the property itself.
- Because the covenant did not touch and concern the land, it could not run with the land, despite the lease's language stating that all covenants would run.
    - Intent is not enough to transform a personal covenant into a running covenant.
- The City had no obligation to return the security deposit.
    - Growth's agreement to indemnify the City did not create a new liability for Growth; it only protected the City from potential liability.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the "touch and concern" test for covenants running with the land, a concept also relevant to easements and equitable servitudes. It emphasizes that a covenant must directly affect the physical use or value of the land, not just be a personal financial obligation, to bind successors. This contrasts with cases where covenants for maintenance assessments (like in *Rodruck*) are found to run because they directly benefit the property.
