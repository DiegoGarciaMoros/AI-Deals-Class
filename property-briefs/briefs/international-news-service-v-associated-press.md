## International News Service v. Associated Press (Supreme Court of the United States, 1918)

**Facts**
- Associated Press (AP) is a cooperative organization that gathers news globally and distributes it to its member newspapers for publication. Members pay assessments for this service and agree to use the news exclusively for their specified newspaper, not furnishing it to non-members before publication. Members also provide local news to AP.
- International News Service (INS) is a for-profit corporation that gathers and sells news to its client newspapers.
- INS systematically took news from AP's bulletin boards and early editions of AP member newspapers.
- INS then sold this news, either verbatim or rewritten, to its own clients, enabling them to compete with AP members in prompt news publication.
- AP filed suit to stop INS's practices, including bribing employees, inducing contract violations, and copying news from bulletins and early editions.
- The District Court granted a preliminary injunction against bribing and inducing contract violations but refused to enjoin the copying of news from bulletins and early editions, stating it was an issue of first impression.
- The Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the injunction and modified it to also enjoin the bodily taking of AP's news until its commercial value had passed.

**Procedural History**
- AP sued INS in District Court.
- District Court granted a preliminary injunction in part, denying it for the copying of news from bulletins and early editions.
- Both parties appealed to the Circuit Court of Appeals.
- Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the injunction and modified it to include the copying of news from bulletins and early editions.
- INS appealed to the Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Can a news-gathering organization be restrained from taking news from a competitor's publicly available bulletins and early newspaper editions and selling it to its own clients for profit?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (AP) wins. A news-gathering organization has a quasi-property right in the news it gathers, as against a competitor, even after initial publication, and the systematic appropriation of that news by a competitor for commercial gain constitutes unfair competition. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Property in News (Rejected).** News, as information about current events, is generally not subject to absolute property rights at common law or under copyright law.
- **Copyright Law.** News articles, as literary productions, can be copyrighted, but the news element itself (the information) is not copyrightable.
- **Unfair Competition.** When competitors are engaged in the business of gathering and distributing news, each party has a duty to conduct its business so as not to unnecessarily or unfairly injure that of the other.
- **Quasi-Property Right.** As between competitors in the business of gathering and distributing news, the news itself, gathered at pains and expense, is considered quasi-property.
- **Misappropriation.** The systematic appropriation of a competitor's quasi-property (news) for commercial gain, where the appropriator reaps where it has not sown, constitutes unfair competition.
- **Abandonment.** Publication of news for limited purposes (e.g., to newspaper readers) does not constitute abandonment of the news to the public for all purposes, especially not for commercial appropriation by a competitor.
- **Unclean Hands.** The practice of using a rival's news as "tips" for independent investigation and verification, if commonly practiced by both parties and not alleged to be unlawful, does not constitute unclean hands sufficient to bar relief.

**Reasoning**
- The case concerns unfair competition between two news-gathering organizations, not the public's right to news.
- News, while not subject to absolute property rights against the public after publication, is "stock in trade" for news agencies, acquired through significant enterprise, organization, skill, labor, and money.
- As between competitors, news must be regarded as "quasi-property" because both parties seek to profit from it.
- INS's actions amount to reaping where it has not sown, diverting profit from AP who incurred the expense of gathering the news.
- This conduct is an unauthorized interference with AP's legitimate business at the point where profit is to be reaped.
- The value of news depends on its prompt transmission and freshness; allowing competitors to appropriate it immediately after initial publication would undermine the business model of news gathering.
- AP's publication of news through bulletins and early editions is for the benefit of its readers, not an abandonment of its commercial value to competitors.
- The "unclean hands" defense fails because using news as "tips" for independent investigation is a recognized practice by both parties and does not equate to the bodily appropriation of news.
- The court's decision does not grant AP a monopoly on news gathering or distribution, but rather postpones a competitor's participation in the distribution of news it did not gather, to prevent unfair advantage.

**Dissent (Brandeis)**
- News, as knowledge, truths, conceptions, and ideas, generally becomes free for common use after voluntary communication to others, unless it involves creation, invention, or discovery protected by copyright or patent law.
- The knowledge sought to be protected here is not of a kind that the law has historically recognized as property.
- The "ticker" cases, relied upon by the majority, are distinguishable because they involved breaches of contract or trust regarding confidential information, which is not present here.
- Cases protecting literary property are inapplicable because news lacks the creative element required for such protection and general publication (like in newspapers) would extinguish any common-law property rights.
- Unfair competition, as traditionally understood, involves fraud, force, or other illegal means, such as "passing off" one's goods as another's. Mere appropriation of another's uncopyrighted product without compensation, even if unseemly, is not legally unfair competition.
- The means by which INS obtained the news (publicly available papers and bulletins) were lawful, and there was no misrepresentation or breach of trust.
- Courts are ill-equipped to create new property rights in news, as this involves complex policy considerations best left to the legislature, which has previously declined to grant such protection.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is foundational for the concept of **quasi-property** and **misappropriation** in intellectual property law, extending property-like protection beyond traditional categories like copyright and patent. It connects to the idea of **labor and investment** as a justification for property rights, similar to Pierson v. Post (though in a different context), but here applied to intangible information. It also contrasts with cases like Moore v. Regents of the University of California, where the court was reluctant to extend property rights to human biological material, highlighting the judiciary's role in defining the scope of property.
2. The tension between protecting investment in news gathering and promoting the free flow of information is a key theme. A hypo could be: If a news organization invests heavily in developing a unique algorithm to predict stock market trends, and a competitor reverse-engineers the algorithm from publicly available data, would that constitute misappropriation under the principles of INS v. AP?

**Takeaway:** The Supreme Court recognized a "quasi-property" right in news between competitors to prevent unfair misappropriation of labor and investment, even if the news itself is not subject to traditional property rights against the public.
