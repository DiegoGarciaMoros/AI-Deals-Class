## Johnson v. M'Intosh (Supreme Court of the United States, 1823)

**Facts**
- On May 23, 1609, King James I granted a charter to the Virginia Company, giving them land in North America.
- At the time of the charter, various independent Indian tribes occupied and possessed the territory in full sovereignty.
- In 1624, the Virginia Company's charter was dissolved, and its rights reverted to the English Crown.
- On July 5, 1773, chiefs of the Illinois Indians sold a large tract of land to William Murray and others (plaintiffs' predecessors).
- On October 18, 1775, chiefs of the Piankeshaw Indians sold another large tract of land to Louis Viviat and others (plaintiffs' predecessors).
- The purchasers under these deeds were British subjects and entered the land, becoming seized as the law required.
- On May 6, 1776, Virginia declared independence from Great Britain.
- On December 20, 1783, Virginia authorized its delegates to convey its claim to the territory north-west of the Ohio River to the United States.
- On March 1, 1784, Virginia's delegates conveyed this territory to the United States.
- On July 20, 1818, the United States sold and conveyed a portion of this land to William McIntosh (defendant).
- The land granted to McIntosh was within the tracts previously conveyed by the Piankeshaw Indians to Viviat and others.
- McIntosh entered and possessed the land under his patent.
- Thomas Johnson, one of the original grantees from the Piankeshaw Indians, died in 1819, devising his share to Joshua Johnson and Thomas J. Graham (the lessors of the plaintiff).
- Neither the original grantees from the Indians nor their successors ever obtained actual possession of the lands due to the American Revolution and subsequent disputes.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs brought an action of ejectment in the District Court of Illinois.
- The District Court entered judgment for the defendant.
- Plaintiffs appealed to the Supreme Court of the United States.

**Issue**
Can a title to land derived from a purchase directly from Native American tribes be recognized in U.S. courts, when the U.S. government later granted the same land to another party?

**Holding**
Defendant wins. A title to lands under grant to private individuals made by Indian tribes cannot be recognized in the courts of the United States. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Discovery Doctrine.** The principle adopted by European nations was that discovery gave title to the government by whose subjects or authority it was made, against all other European governments, which title could be consummated by possession.
- **Exclusive Right of Acquisition.** The exclusion of all other Europeans gave the discovering nation the sole right to acquire soil from the natives and establish settlements.
- **Indian Right of Occupancy.** While European nations asserted ultimate dominion, they admitted the natives to be rightful occupants of the soil with a legal claim to retain possession and use it.
- **Limited Indian Sovereignty.** The rights of original inhabitants to complete sovereignty and to dispose of the soil at will were diminished by the principle that discovery gave exclusive title to the discoverers.
- **Governmental Pre-emption.** The exclusive right to extinguish the Indian title of occupancy, either by purchase or conquest, resides in the discovering sovereign (and its successors).
- **Incompatible Titles.** An absolute title to lands cannot exist at the same time in different persons or governments; the absolute title of the crown (and thus the U.S.) is subject only to the Indian right of occupancy, which the crown has the exclusive right to extinguish.
- **Rejected: Individual Purchase from Indians.** Private individuals cannot acquire a valid title to land directly from Indian tribes that can be sustained in U.S. courts against a title derived from the government.

**Reasoning**
- European nations, upon discovering the continent, established a principle among themselves: discovery gave title to the discovering government against other European governments.
    - This principle gave the discovering nation the exclusive right to acquire land from the natives.
    - While natives were recognized as rightful occupants, their power to dispose of the soil was denied.
    - The ultimate dominion was asserted by the discoverers, who could grant the soil subject to the Indian right of occupancy.
- This principle has been universally recognized and applied by all European powers claiming territory in America, including England.
    - English charters consistently granted land "not actually possessed by any Christian prince or people," asserting a right to take possession despite native occupancy.
    - The entire country was granted by the Crown while occupied by Indians, and these grants conveyed soil and dominion.
- The United States acceded to this rule upon independence.
    - The treaty ending the Revolutionary War transferred Great Britain's governmental powers and territorial rights to the U.S.
    - States, like Virginia, affirmed their exclusive right of pre-emption from Indians and annulled private purchases.
    - The U.S. has consistently exercised the exclusive right to extinguish Indian title and grant the soil, even for lands occupied by numerous tribes.
- The court cannot question the validity of the title derived from conquest and discovery.
    - The British government, whose rights passed to the U.S., asserted title to all lands occupied by Indians within its chartered limits and the exclusive right to extinguish their titles.
    - This title has been maintained by force and is the foundation of property for the mass of the community.
- The nature of the Indian tribes (fierce savages, hunters) made it impossible to govern them as a distinct people or incorporate them.
    - This necessitated a different rule than that applied to conquered civilized nations.
    - The rule that Indians are merely occupants, incapable of transferring absolute title, became indispensable to the system under which the country was settled.
- The proclamation of 1763 by the King of Great Britain further supports the defendant's title.
    - It reserved lands for Indians and forbade private purchases, reflecting the Crown's exclusive right to grant or reserve lands.
    - This power was never denied by the colonies to the Crown.
- Even if individuals could extinguish Indian title, they would only acquire that title, which is dependent on Indian laws and subject to their will.
    - The U.S. courts cannot protect such a title if the tribe chooses to annul it.
    - The tribes whose title the plaintiffs claim ceded the lands to the U.S. without reservation, implying they considered prior grants invalid.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is foundational for understanding the **discovery doctrine** and its impact on Native American land rights in U.S. property law. It establishes the principle of **governmental pre-emption** over Indian lands, meaning only the sovereign (first the European colonizers, then the U.S. government) could extinguish Native American title. It contrasts sharply with a pure **first possession** theory, as it denies the full property rights of the original inhabitants based on their "savage" character and the needs of the "civilized" conquerors. It sets the stage for later cases dealing with tribal sovereignty and land claims.
2. The opinion acknowledges the "natural right" of Indians to their lands but dismisses it in favor of established legal principles based on conquest and necessity. This raises questions about the moral legitimacy of the legal framework established. What if the court had decided to apply "abstract justice" instead of the "principles which our own government has adopted"?

**Takeaway:** The U.S. legal system does not recognize private land titles acquired directly from Native American tribes, as the sovereign holds the exclusive right to extinguish aboriginal title.
