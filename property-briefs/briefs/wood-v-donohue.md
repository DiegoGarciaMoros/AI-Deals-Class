## Wood v. Donohue (Ohio Court of Appeals, 1999)

**Facts**
- In 1983, Donohue and Schroot entered into a land installment contract with Wood to purchase a house for $87,900.
- Donohue and Schroot made a $30,000 down payment and agreed to make monthly payments with a seven-year balloon payment.
- The house was near the Fernald uranium processing plant.
- In 1985, a class action was initiated against the processing plant.
- Wood, Donohue, and Schroot filed claims in the class action.
- The balance of the purchase price for the house was paid in full in 1990.
- The class action settled, with members receiving money for diminution in property value as of December 18, 1984.
- The Fernald trustees awarded $9,478 for the diminished value of the property and issued a check in 1993 to Donohue, Schroot, and Wood.
- The check was not cashed.
- Wood filed a complaint seeking a declaration that she was entitled to 65.41% of the settlement check.
- The Fernald trustees deposited a new check into an escrow account, and Wood dismissed them from the case.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court entered judgment for Wood, awarding her 65.41% of the settlement and Donohue the remainder, based on the portion of the purchase money Donohue had paid as of December 18, 1984.
- Donohue appealed the trial court's decision.

**Issue**
- Is the buyer in a land installment contract entitled to the full amount of a settlement for property diminution that occurred after the contract was signed but before the full purchase price was paid, under the doctrine of equitable conversion?

**Holding**
- Donohue (appellant) wins. The buyer, as equitable owner, is entitled to the full settlement amount for property diminution that occurred after the land installment contract was executed. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Equitable Conversion.** Where land is contracted to be sold, equity treats the exchange as actually taking place when the contract becomes effective, passing equitable title to the buyer.
- **Vendor's Interest.** The seller's interest under a contract of purchase is a right to receive the balance of the purchase price, secured by retaining legal title.
- **Vendor's Lien.** A seller's retention of legal title in a land installment contract is analogous to a mortgage for the unpaid purchase price, with title kept as security for the debt.
- **Vendee's Equitable Estate.** A vendee obtains an equitable estate entitling them to all incidents of ownership, including the right to use the property free from vendor interference.
- **Equitable Owner.** One who is recognized in equity as the owner of property because the real and beneficial use and title belong to them, even if bare legal title is vested in another.
- **Land Installment Contract (Ohio R.C. 5313.01(A)).** An executory agreement where a vendee agrees to pay the purchase price in installments, while the vendor retains title to the property as security for the vendee’s obligation.

**Reasoning**
- The doctrine of equitable conversion applies to land installment contracts in Ohio.
- Upon execution of the land installment contract, equitable title transferred to Donohue, making him the equitable owner.
- As equitable owner, Donohue bore all losses and was entitled to all benefits that might accrue to the property.
- The court found no reason not to apply the equitable conversion analysis, typically used for insurance proceeds after accidental damage, to settlement proceeds for diminution in value.
- The land installment contract's terms indicated the parties intended Donohue to bear any loss, as he was responsible for insurance premiums, was a named insured, and had options regarding insurance proceeds for repair or application to the purchase price.
- Wood's retention of legal title was solely as security for the unpaid purchase money.
- Since Wood had received the full purchase price by the time the settlement money was paid, her security interest was no longer impaired.
- Awarding Wood a portion of the settlement would result in her unfair enrichment by recovering twice for the same property.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the application of the **equitable conversion** doctrine, which shifts the risk of loss and the benefit of appreciation to the buyer upon contract formation, even before legal title passes. It connects to the concept of **owner sovereignty** by determining who holds the beneficial interest in the property at a given time, and thus who is entitled to compensation for its diminution. It also touches upon the idea of a vendor's lien, similar to a mortgage, which is a common security interest in property law.
