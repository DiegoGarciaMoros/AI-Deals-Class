## Fontainebleau Hotel Corp. v. Forty-Five Twenty-Five Inc. (Florida District Court of Appeal, 1959)

**Facts**
- The Eden Roc Hotel was built in 1955, adjoining the Fontainebleau Hotel to the north.
- The Fontainebleau Hotel began constructing a 14-story addition 20 feet from its north property line.
- The addition, when completed, would cast a shadow over the cabana, swimming pool, and sunbathing areas of the Eden Roc during winter afternoons.
- The Eden Roc Hotel alleged the construction was motivated by malice and ill will.
- The City of Miami Beach enacted a "Shadow Ordinance" at the Eden Roc's behest, which was later held invalid.
- The Fontainebleau's construction was proceeding under a permit issued by the city, authorizing a 76-foot setback from the ocean bulkhead line.
- The Eden Roc's objection to the setback distance was raised almost a year after construction began, when the addition was approximately eight stories high.

**Procedural History**
- The Eden Roc Hotel (plaintiff) sought an injunction to stop the construction.
- The trial court granted a temporary injunction.
- The Fontainebleau Hotel (defendant) appealed the temporary injunction.

**Issue**
Can a landowner be enjoined from constructing a building on their property that casts a shadow on an adjoining landowner's property, in the absence of a contractual or statutory obligation, or an easement?

**Holding**
The defendant (Fontainebleau Hotel Corp.) wins. A landowner has no legal right to the free flow of light and air across an adjoining landowner's property, and a structure serving a useful purpose does not constitute a nuisance even if it causes injury by blocking light and air. Reversed with directions to dismiss the complaint.

**Rules**
- **Sic utere tuo ut alienum non laedas.** One must use their property so as not to injure the lawful rights of another.
- **Rejected: English doctrine of "ancient lights."** This doctrine, which grants a legal right to unobstructed light and air after uninterrupted use for a period of 20 years, has been unanimously repudiated in the United States.
- **Lawful use of property.** A property owner may put their own property to any reasonable and lawful use, so long as they do not deprive an adjoining landowner of any right of enjoyment of their property recognized and protected by law, and so long as their use is not a nuisance.
- **Right to light and air.** In the absence of a contractual or statutory obligation, or an easement, a landowner has no legal right to the free flow of light and air across the adjoining land of a neighbor.
- **Spite structures.** Where a structure serves a useful and beneficial purpose, it does not give rise to a cause of action, even if it causes injury by cutting off light and air and interfering with a view, and regardless of the fact that the structure may have been erected partly for spite.
- **Judicial legislation.** Changing the universal rule that adjoining landowners have an equal right to build to the line of their respective tracts and to such a height as desired (in the absence of restrictions) amounts to judicial legislation.
- **Violation of ordinance.** Even if a building ordinance is violated, a plaintiff may not have a cause of action if the violation would make no appreciable difference to the alleged injury.

**Reasoning**
- The maxim *sic utere tuo ut alienum non laedas* means one must use their property so as not to injure the lawful rights of another, not that one must never use their property in a way that causes any injury to a neighbor.
- There is no legal right to the free flow of light and air from adjoining land in the absence of a contract, statute, or easement.
- The English doctrine of "ancient lights" has been repudiated in the United States.
- A structure serving a useful purpose does not create a cause of action, even if it blocks light and air and was partly motivated by spite.
- To change this established rule would be judicial legislation; such changes should be made through comprehensive planning and zoning ordinances.
- There is no statutory basis, express or implied easement, or prescriptive right for the plaintiff's claim to light and air.
- Even if the setback ordinance was violated, the plaintiff would not have a cause of action because the violation would not significantly alter the shadow problem, and the construction was proceeding under a valid permit.
- The plaintiff's objection to the setback was made late in the construction process, after significant expenditure by the defendant.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a foundational example of the **owner autonomy** principle in property law, emphasizing the right of a landowner to use their property as they see fit, even if it negatively impacts a neighbor, as long as no "lawful right" of the neighbor is infringed. It directly rejects the English "ancient lights" doctrine, which is a key distinction in American property law. This case contrasts with cases like *Jost v. Dairyland Power Cooperative* or *Campbell v. Seaman*, which deal with nuisance where there is a direct physical invasion or interference with the use and enjoyment of land, rather than merely blocking light and air.
2. This case raises the question of whether there are any limits to a landowner's right to build on their property, particularly when the intent is malicious, and the harm is significant. What if the primary purpose of the addition was *solely* to cast a shadow, with no other beneficial use?

**Takeaway:** American property law generally does not recognize a right to light and air, prioritizing a landowner's right to develop their property over a neighbor's access to sunlight.
