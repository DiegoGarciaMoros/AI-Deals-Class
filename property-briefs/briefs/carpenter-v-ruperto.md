## Carpenter v. Ruperto (Iowa Supreme Court, 1982)

**Facts**
- In July 1951, Virginia Carpenter and her husband moved into a home they purchased in southeast Des Moines.
- Carpenter's lot was 40 feet by 125 feet.
- A larger undeveloped lot, owned by the defendants and their predecessors, bounded Carpenter's property to the north.
- Carpenter knew her lot's dimensions and that it did not include the cornfield to the north.
- In 1952, Carpenter and her husband cleared several feet of the property to the north, graded it, and planted grass.
- Carpenter used this land as an extension of her yard, planting peony bushes in the 1950s, installing a propane tank 30 feet north of her lot in 1964, constructing a dirt bank in 1965, and putting in a driveway infringing five feet onto the land in 1975.
- The Rosenfelds owned the adjacent lot from July 1960 to February 1978, storing junk and debris on it, and paying all taxes.
- Carpenter and her husband once obtained the Rosenfelds’ permission to keep a horse on the lot.
- In the 1960s, Carpenter examined the plat of the defendants’ lot in the courthouse.
- In 1978, defendant McCormick purchased his interest in the lot, aware of a potential boundary dispute due to Carpenter's propane tank and driveway.
- Defendants were unsuccessful in settling the dispute with Carpenter.
- Carpenter brought an action to quiet title to the south 60 feet of the defendants' lot, claiming adverse possession.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court found Carpenter did not establish her possession was under a claim of right and denied her adverse possession claim.
- The trial court ordered defendants to deed Carpenter the strip of land her driveway was on and pay to move the propane tank.
- Carpenter appealed the denial of her adverse possession claim.
- Defendants cross-appealed the equitable relief granted to Carpenter.
- The Iowa Supreme Court affirmed on the merits of the appeal and dismissed the cross-appeal for want of jurisdiction.

**Issue**
Can a party acquire title by adverse possession under a claim of right if they knew they had no title and no basis for claiming an interest in the property when they entered possession?

**Holding**
Defendant (record owner) wins. A party cannot establish a good faith claim of right for adverse possession if they knew they had no title and no basis for claiming an interest in the property. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Adverse Possession Elements.** One claiming title by adverse possession must establish hostile, actual, open, exclusive, and continuous possession, under a claim of right or color of title, for at least ten years, by clear and positive proof.
- **Strict Construction of Adverse Possession.** The doctrine of adverse possession is strictly construed because the law presumes possession under regular title.
- **Claim of Right.** A claim of right must be made in good faith.
- **Rejected: Squatter's Rights.** To permit a squatter to assert a claim of right would put a premium on dishonesty.
- **Knowledge of Defective Title.** Knowledge of a defect in title is not alone sufficient to preclude proof of good faith.
- **Knowledge of No Title and No Basis for Claim.** When knowledge of lack of title is accompanied by knowledge of no basis for claiming an interest in the property, a good faith claim of right cannot be established.

**Reasoning**
- The court found Carpenter failed to prove a good faith claim of right.
- Carpenter knew her lot did not include the cornfield to the north.
- Carpenter knew someone else had title to the land and that she had no interest in it or claim to it.
- This was not a case of confusion or mistake regarding the boundary.
- At the time she entered possession, Carpenter knew she had no legal right to do so.
- Allowing a claim of right based merely on entering possession would recognize "squatter's rights," which the court rejects.
- The court distinguished cases where knowledge of a *defective* title does not defeat good faith from cases where there is knowledge of *no* title and *no basis* for a claim.
- The purpose of the claim of right requirement is to bar mere squatters from the benefits of adverse possession.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the "claim of right" element of adverse possession, particularly the "good faith" requirement. It contrasts with cases where a mistaken belief about a boundary might still satisfy the claim of right, emphasizing that knowing you have no right to the land, as Carpenter did, defeats the claim. This case highlights the tension between rewarding productive use of land and protecting the rights of record owners, similar to the policy considerations in *Howard v. Kunto* regarding mistaken boundaries, but with a different outcome due to the possessor's knowledge.
2. What if Carpenter had *not* looked at the plat in the courthouse and genuinely believed the land was abandoned or part of her property due to the lack of clear boundaries? Would that change the outcome regarding "good faith"?

**Takeaway:** A possessor cannot establish a good faith claim of right for adverse possession if they knew they had no legal right to the property when they entered possession.
