## Carpenter v. Ruperto (Iowa Supreme Court, 1982)

**Facts**
- In July 1951, Virginia Carpenter and her husband moved into a home in Des Moines.
- Carpenter's lot was 40 feet by 125 feet.
- A larger, undeveloped lot, owned by defendants and their predecessors, bounded Carpenter's property to the north.
- The disputed property is the south 60 feet of the defendants' lot, adjacent to Carpenter's northern boundary.
- When Carpenter moved in, the lot to the north was a cornfield.
- Carpenter knew her lot's dimensions and that it did not include the cornfield.
- In 1952, Carpenter and her husband cleared several feet of the property to the north, graded it, and planted grass seed.
- Carpenter used the land as an extension of her yard, planting peony bushes in the 1950s, installing a propane tank 30 feet north of her lot in 1964, constructing a dirt bank in 1965, and putting in a driveway infringing five feet onto the land in 1975.
- The Rosenfelds owned the defendants' lot from July 1960 to February 1978, storing junk and debris on it and paying all taxes.
- Carpenter and her husband once obtained the Rosenfelds' permission to keep a horse on the lot.
- In the 1960s, Carpenter examined the plat of the defendants' lot at the courthouse to see if it extended to a street to the north.
- In 1978, defendant McCormick purchased his interest in the lot, aware of a potential boundary dispute due to Carpenter's propane tank and driveway.
- Efforts to settle the dispute failed.

**Procedural History**
- Carpenter sued to quiet title based on adverse possession.
- The trial court found Carpenter did not establish adverse possession because she lacked a good faith claim of right.
- The trial court ordered defendants to deed a strip of land for Carpenter's driveway and pay to move her propane tank.
- Carpenter appealed the adverse possession ruling.
- Defendants cross-appealed the equitable relief granted to Carpenter.

**Issue**
Can a person acquire title by adverse possession under a claim of right if they knew they had no legal right to the property when they took possession?

**Holding**
Defendant (record title holder) wins. A claim of right for adverse possession requires good faith, which is absent when the possessor knows they have no legal right to the property. Affirmed on the appeal; dismissed on the cross-appeal.

**Rules**
- **Adverse Possession Elements.** One claiming title by adverse possession must establish hostile, actual, open, exclusive, and continuous possession, under a claim of right or color of title, for at least ten years, by clear and positive proof.
- **Presumption of Regular Title.** The law presumes possession under regular title, so the doctrine of adverse possession is strictly construed.
- **Claim of Right (Good Faith).** Good faith is an essential component of a claim of right in adverse possession.
- **Knowledge of No Title.** When knowledge of lack of title is accompanied by knowledge of no basis for claiming an interest in the property, a good faith claim of right cannot be established.
- **Rejected: Squatter's Rights.** To permit a squatter to assert a claim of right would put a premium on dishonesty.
- **Rejected: Mere Knowledge of Defect.** Knowledge of a defect in title is not alone sufficient to preclude proof of good faith. *(dicta)*

**Reasoning**
- The court found Carpenter knew her lot did not include the cornfield to the north and that someone else had title to it, and she had no interest or claim to it.
- This was not a case of confusion or mistake.
- At the time she entered possession, Carpenter knew she had no legal right to do so.
- Allowing acquisition of a claim of right by merely entering possession would recognize squatter's rights, which the law does not permit.
- The good faith requirement prevents mere squatters from benefiting from adverse possession.
- The court distinguished cases where knowledge of a defect in title does not preclude good faith from situations where there is knowledge of no basis for claiming an interest.
- The cross-appeal was dismissed because it was not filed within the mandatory five-day period after the appeal was taken, as required by Iowa R.App. 5(a).

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the "claim of right" element of adverse possession, particularly the requirement of good faith. It contrasts with cases where a mistaken belief about a boundary line might still satisfy the claim of right, as seen in cases like *Howard v. Kunto* (though *Howard* focused more on continuity of possession and privity). *Carpenter* emphasizes that a knowing trespasser, or "squatter," cannot establish a claim of right.
2. The court's distinction between "knowledge of a defect in title" (which might be okay) and "knowledge of no basis for claiming an interest" (which is not okay) could be a point of tension. How clear must the knowledge be? What if Carpenter genuinely believed the land was "waste land" as in *Goulding v. Shonquist*?

**Takeaway:** A knowing trespasser cannot establish a good faith claim of right for adverse possession in Iowa.
