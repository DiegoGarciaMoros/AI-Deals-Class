## Hauck v. Crawford (South Dakota Supreme Court, 1953)

**Facts**
- Plaintiff Hauck is a 44-year-old farmer with an 8th-grade education, owning two sections of land in South Dakota and North Dakota.
- On May 23, 1951, three men, including Mr. Crawford, approached Hauck at a neighbor's place to discuss leasing his land for oil and gas.
- Crawford offered 25 cents per acre for a lease, which Hauck agreed to.
- One of the men prepared papers on a typewriter in the back seat of a car, using printed forms with fine print.
- The man indicated where Hauck should sign, and after signing once, partially turned the sheet and asked Hauck to sign again, stating it was part of the lease.
- Hauck testified no mention was made of a mineral deed, and Crawford corroborated this.
- Separate instruments were prepared for land in each state.
- Hauck never received copies of the instruments he signed.
- Hauck had signed a mineral deed conveying one-half of the minerals in his land to D.W. Crawford.
- This deed was filed for record on June 1, 1951.
- On May 29, 1951, Crawford conveyed these mineral rights to defendants White and Duncan in Gainesville, Texas.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court entered judgment canceling the deeds based on alleged fraud.
- Defendants appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a deed obtained through "fraud in the execution" be canceled against a subsequent bona fide purchaser for value if the original grantor was negligent in signing the deed?

**Holding**
- The defendants (appellants) win. The judgment is reversed.
- A deed obtained by "fraud in the execution" is void as between the original parties, but if the original grantor was negligent in signing, the deed may be valid against a bona fide purchaser for value.
- Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Fraud in the Execution (Fraud in the Factum).** This type of fraud relates to misrepresentation of the contents of a document by which one is induced to sign a paper thinking that it is other than it really is.
- **Void vs. Voidable.** An instrument obtained by fraud in the execution is void, meaning "the contractual knot was never tied" and it has no legal effect. An instrument obtained by fraud in the inducement is voidable.
- **Negligence and Fraud.** As between the original parties, the perpetrator of fraud cannot avoid his acts by showing the victim was negligent.
- **Bona Fide Purchaser.** A bona fide purchaser for value without notice may acquire good title even if the original transaction was fraudulent, unless the original instrument was void and the true owner was not negligent or did not create an estoppel.
- **Estoppel.** An estoppel arises when, by conduct or acts, a party intentionally or through culpable neglect induces another to believe certain facts exist, and the other party relies and acts on that belief to their prejudice.
- **Culpable Negligence.** In civil actions, "culpable negligence" means the same as actionable negligence.
- **Forgery (Rejected).** The court rejects the minority view that obtaining a signature through fraud in the execution constitutes forgery.

**Reasoning**
- The court agrees with the trial court that Crawford's actions constituted "fraud in the execution" because he misrepresented the papers and tricked Hauck into signing a deed he believed was a lease.
- Under the rule from *Federal Land Bank v. Houck*, a deed obtained by fraud in the execution is void between the original parties, and the grantor's negligence does not neutralize this fraud.
- Since the deed was void, Crawford had no title to convey to White and Duncan.
- However, the court notes that even if a deed is void, if the plaintiff was negligent or created an estoppel, they should bear the burden rather than a bona fide purchaser.
- The trial court did not make a specific finding on whether Hauck was negligent when he signed the deed.
- The question of whether Hauck acted as a person of reasonable and ordinary care, given his capacity and intelligence, is a question of fact for the trial court to determine.
- The court rejects the idea that obtaining a signature through fraud in the execution constitutes forgery, preferring its established rule regarding fraud in the execution.

**Dissent (Judge)**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case builds on the concept of "void" versus "voidable" instruments, which is crucial for understanding how property interests can be transferred and protected. It connects to cases like *Johnson v. M'Intosh* in establishing the validity of title, but here, the focus is on the *transfer* of title and the protection of subsequent purchasers.
