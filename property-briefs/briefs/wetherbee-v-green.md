## Wetherbee v. Green (Michigan Supreme Court, 1871)

**Facts**
- Green and Sumner owned land as tenants in common.
- Green orally authorized Sumner to sell timber from the land.
- Sumner, indebted to Camp and Brooks, conveyed his undivided half of the land to them by warranty deed, with an oral agreement for re-conveyance upon payment.
- Sumner sold timber from the land to Wetherbee.
- Wetherbee cut the timber and manufactured it into barrel hoops.
- Green, Camp, and Brooks brought an action to replevy the hoops.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs (Green, Camp, Brooks) brought an action of replevin against Defendant (Wetherbee) in the Circuit Court for the County of Bay.
- The Circuit Judge excluded testimony offered by Wetherbee regarding the nature of the deed from Sumner to Camp and Brooks (that it was a security) and the license from Sumner to cut timber.
- The jury found for the plaintiffs.
- Judgment was entered upon the verdict.
- Wetherbee appealed by writ of error to the Michigan Supreme Court.

**Issue**
Can an owner reclaim property that was taken in good faith and transformed by labor into an article immensely more valuable, or is the owner limited to damages for the original value?

**Holding**
Defendant (Wetherbee) wins. If a party in good faith transforms another's property into a substantially different and much more valuable article, the title to the improved article passes to the transformer, and the original owner's remedy is limited to damages for the value of the original materials. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Accession (Good Faith Transformation).** When a party in good faith takes another's property and, by their labor and money, transforms it into an article immensely more valuable and substantially different, title to the transformed article passes to the party who wrought the change.
- **Accession (Willful Trespass).** *(Dicta)* If a trespasser willfully appropriates property, no extent of conversion can give them title to the property so long as the original materials can be traced.
- **Accession (Identity Test Rejected).** The physical identity of the original material is not the sole or primary test for determining whether title passes after transformation; the relative increase in value due to labor is more important.
- **Accession (Relative Values).** The relative values contributed by the original materials and the labor expended are paramount in determining title by accession, aiming for substantial equity.
- **Deed as Security.** A deed absolute in form but intended as security may leave a right of redemption in the grantor, but it does not necessarily entitle the grantor to possession until foreclosure.
- **License to Cut Timber.** A mere license to enter land and cut timber does not confer a legal right but protects the licensee for actions taken under it before revocation.
- **Revocation of License.** A conveyance by one co-tenant of their interest in land does not automatically revoke an authority derived from their co-tenant to sell timber on the tract.

**Reasoning**
- The law aims to compensate the injured party, not to punish an unintentional wrongdoer excessively.
    - Inflicting a penalty equal to twenty or thirty times the original value for an unintentional wrong is contrary to justice.
- While an owner generally can reclaim property that has increased in value, there must be a limit when the property has undergone a substantial transformation.
    - The rule from Blackstone states that if a thing is changed into a different species (e.g., grapes into wine), the product belongs to the new operator, who only pays for the materials.
    - This rule applies when the appropriation is in good faith; willful trespassers may not acquire title even after radical conversion.
- The key question is whether standing trees manufactured into hoops are so changed that their identity is destroyed.
    - Physical identity (traceable by senses) is an unsatisfactory test. For example, a beam built into a house or timber made into a musical instrument cannot be reclaimed, even if identifiable, because the labor's value has dwarfed the material's value.
    - The purpose of the law is to achieve substantial justice, not arbitrary physical distinctions.
- The relative value contributed by labor versus the original materials is the most important factor.
    - When labor gives the transformed article nearly all its value, equity demands recognizing a change of title.
    - Here, timber worth $25 was converted into hoops worth $700, representing a very significant increase in value due to Wetherbee's labor.
- The court erred in rejecting evidence that Wetherbee acted in good faith, believing he had proper authority, as this good faith is crucial to applying the accession rule.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a foundational example of **accession**, where one person adds value to another's property. It contrasts with cases like *Armory v. Delamirie* and *Clark v. Maloney*, which deal with prior possession of chattels without significant transformation. *Wetherbee* introduces the idea that labor can create a new property right, especially when the value added is substantial and the transformation is done in good faith. It also highlights the tension between protecting the original owner's property rights and rewarding productive labor.
2. What if the value added was only 2x or 3x, not 28x? At what point does the increase in value become "immense" enough to trigger a change in title? This case establishes a high bar for transformation.

**Takeaway:** Good faith transformation of another's property into a substantially more valuable and different article can transfer title to the transformer, limiting the original owner to damages for the original materials.
