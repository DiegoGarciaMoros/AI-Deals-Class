## Delfino v. Vealencis (Connecticut Supreme Court, 1980)

**Facts**
- The Delfinos and Helen Vealencis own a 20.5-acre parcel of land in Bristol, Connecticut, as tenants in common.
- The Delfinos own an undivided 99/144 interest, and Vealencis owns a 45/144 interest.
- Vealencis lives in a dwelling on the property and operates a rubbish and garbage removal business from there. Her family has operated the business on the premises since the 1920s.
- The Delfinos, one of whom is a residential developer, want to develop the property into 45 residential building lots.
- The Delfinos brought an action seeking a partition by sale of the property.
- Vealencis moved for a partition in kind.

**Procedural History**
- The trial court ordered a partition by sale, concluding that a partition in kind could not be done without "material injury" to the parties' rights.
- Vealencis appealed to the Connecticut Supreme Court.

**Issue**
- Did the trial court err in ordering a partition by sale instead of a partition in kind, given the preference for partition in kind and the circumstances of the parties?

**Holding**
- Defendant (Vealencis) wins. The trial court erred in ordering a partition by sale because a partition in kind is practicable and better promotes the interests of all owners. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Partition in Kind Preference.** Courts generally favor a partition in kind over a partition by sale.
- **Partition by Sale Conditions.** A partition by sale should only be ordered when two conditions are met:
    1. The physical attributes of the land are such that a partition in kind is impracticable or inequitable.
    2. The interests of the owners would better be promoted by a partition by sale.
- **Burden of Proof for Partition by Sale.** The party requesting a partition by sale has the burden to demonstrate that such a sale would better promote the owners' interests.
- **Consideration of All Owners' Interests.** When deciding on partition, the court must consider the interests of all tenants in common, not just the economic gain of one tenant or group of tenants.
- **Rejected: Zoning Violation Inference.** An inference that a business use is in violation of zoning regulations, and thus unlikely to continue, is unfounded without sufficient competent evidence.
- **Rejected: Planning Commission Approval Speculation.** Speculation about a planning commission's future approval of a subdivision plan is not sufficient without substantial evidence.

**Reasoning**
- The trial court erred in concluding that a physical partition was not feasible.
    - The property is a 20.5-acre, basically rectangular parcel with roads abutting it and access via a right-of-way.
    - There are only two competing ownership interests, not numerous fractional owners.
    - These facts indicate that a partition in kind is clearly practicable.
- The trial court erred in concluding that a partition in kind would not promote the best interests of the parties.
    - The court's inference that Vealencis's business was a zoning violation and would not continue was unsupported by evidence.
        - The applicable zoning regulations and their enactment date were not introduced.
        - Vealencis's family has operated the business since the 1920s with appropriate permits.
    - The court's speculation that the planning commission would reject the Delfinos' subdivision plan was unfounded.
        - There was no substantial evidence to support this conclusion.
        - The defendant's business operations involve "mostly containerized . . . dumpsters" and no garbage is deposited on the property, which does not support the court's speculation.
    - The trial court failed to give due consideration to Vealencis's interests.
        - She has been in actual and exclusive possession of a portion of the property for a substantial time.
        - She has made her home on the property.
        - She derives her livelihood from the business operated on the property.
        - A partition by sale would force her to surrender her home and jeopardize her livelihood.
    - The court must consider the interests of all tenants in common, not just the economic gain of one group.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case highlights the strong preference for partition in kind over partition by sale, especially when one co-tenant has a long-standing possessory interest and livelihood tied to the property. It connects to the concept of **co-tenancy** and the various ways co-owned property can be divided. It also touches on the limits of judicial discretion in ordering a sale, emphasizing the need for concrete evidence rather than speculation about future events or zoning issues.
2. What if Vealencis's business was undeniably a nuisance to potential residential development, and there was clear evidence that the planning commission would reject any subdivision plan due to its presence? Would the court's reasoning change, or would the preference for partition in kind still prevail due to her reliance interest?

**Takeaway:** Courts strongly prefer partition in kind over partition by sale, especially when a co-tenant has a significant reliance interest in continued possession and use of the property, and will only order a sale if physical division is truly impracticable and a sale clearly better promotes the interests of all owners.
