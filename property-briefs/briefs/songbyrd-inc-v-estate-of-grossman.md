## Songbyrd Inc. v. Estate of Grossman (United States Court of Appeals, Second Circuit, 2000)

**Facts**
- In the early 1970s, Henry Roeland Byrd ("Professor Longhair") made master recording tapes in Baton Rouge.
- Arthur Davis and Parker Dinkins, acting for Byrd, sent these master tapes to Albert Grossman, president of Bearsville Records, Inc., in Woodstock, New York, as demonstration tapes only, without intent to transfer ownership.
- The tapes remained in Grossman's possession.
- In 1975, Dinkins sent two letters to Bearsville Records, Inc. requesting the return of the master recordings, but received no response.
- Grossman died in 1985. Bearsville Records, Inc. was dissolved, but Grossman's estate ("the Estate") continued doing business as Bearsville Records and possessed the tapes.
- In 1986, the Estate licensed some of the Byrd master recordings to Rounder Records Corporation for an advance against royalties.
- In 1987, Rounder released an album of Byrd's recordings, which won a posthumous Grammy Award.
- In 1991, the Estate licensed some master recordings to Rhino Records, which released an album.
- In 1993, SongByrd, Inc. was incorporated as a successor-in-interest to Byrd's intellectual property rights.

**Procedural History**
- SongByrd filed a "Petition in Revendication" against the Estate in Louisiana state court in 1995, seeking a declaration of ownership, return of tapes, and damages.
- The Estate removed the case to the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Louisiana.
- The Louisiana District Court initially dismissed the suit as time-barred, explicitly declining to consider personal jurisdiction.
- The Fifth Circuit reversed the dismissal, remanding for consideration of the time-bar defense and personal jurisdiction.
- On remand, the Louisiana District Court found it lacked personal jurisdiction over the Estate and sua sponte transferred the case to the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of New York.
- The New York District Court (Magistrate Judge Homer) granted summary judgment for the Estate, finding the suit time-barred under New York law.
- SongByrd appealed to the Second Circuit.

**Issue**
Is SongByrd's claim for wrongful possession of master recording tapes time-barred under New York law, given that the case was transferred from Louisiana due to a lack of personal jurisdiction?

**Holding**
Defendant (Estate of Grossman) wins. SongByrd's claim is time-barred under New York law because the cause of action for conversion accrued when the Estate licensed the master tapes in 1986, more than three years before the suit was filed. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Transfer for Lack of Personal Jurisdiction.** A district court lacking personal jurisdiction over a defendant may transfer the case to a district where personal jurisdiction can be exercised.
- **Law of Transferee Jurisdiction.** When a case is transferred due to a lack of personal jurisdiction in the transferor court, the law of the transferee jurisdiction applies.
- **New York Statute of Limitations for Conversion.** The relevant statute of limitations for conversion and recovery of chattels in New York is three years.
- **Accrual of Conversion Claim (New York).** A cause of action for conversion accrues when the possessor begins using the plaintiff's property as its own, not each time the property is subsequently used.
- **Accrual of Replevin Claim against Good-Faith Purchaser (New York).** A cause of action for replevin against the good-faith purchaser of a stolen chattel accrues when the true owner makes demand for return of the chattel and the person in possession refuses to return it.
- **Demand and Refusal Rule (New York).** The demand and refusal rule, which delays accrual of a claim for the benefit of the true owner, is not required for a conversion claim against a possessor who openly deals with the property as its own.
- **Unreasonable Delay in Demand (New York).** An owner, having discovered the location of lost property, cannot unreasonably delay making demand upon the person in possession of that property.

**Reasoning**
- The transfer from Louisiana to New York was proper because the Louisiana court lacked personal jurisdiction over the Estate.
    - Grossman's contacts with Louisiana were "extremely scant" and insufficient to establish personal jurisdiction.
    - His invitation to Byrd to come to New York was too insubstantial a contact for specific jurisdiction.
    - General jurisdiction was not satisfied.
- New York law applies because the transfer was due to lack of personal jurisdiction in Louisiana.
- Under New York law, the three-year statute of limitations for conversion applies.
- SongByrd's cause of action accrued no later than August 1986.
    - The Estate began using the master tapes as its own when it licensed them to Rounder in 1986.
    - This act constituted conversion, as established in *Sporn v. MCA Records, Inc.*, where commercial exploitation of master recordings triggered accrual.
    - The demand-and-refusal rule, as applied in *Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation v. Lubell* and *Hoelzer v. City of Stamford*, does not apply here because the Estate openly dealt with the property as its own, changing the character of its possession.
    - Even if a demand were required, SongByrd's predecessors unreasonably delayed making a demand after 1975 and certainly after the 1987 Grammy Award made the licensing widely known.
- Since the suit was filed in 1995, more than three years after the 1986 accrual, the claim is time-barred.

**NOTES:**
1. This case highlights the importance of the **statute of limitations** in property disputes, particularly for chattels. It distinguishes between the accrual rules for conversion and replevin under New York law, building on *Sporn v. MCA Records, Inc.* and *Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation v. Lubell*. It shows that openly treating property as one's own can trigger the statute of limitations for conversion, even without a formal demand and refusal. This contrasts with cases like *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* where the discovery rule might apply to extend the limitations period for conversion in certain contexts.
2. The court's discussion of the transfer order and the applicable law is important. It clarifies that when a case is transferred for lack of personal jurisdiction, the law of the transferee court applies, which can be outcome-determinative for the statute of limitations. This could lead to forum shopping or strategic choices about where to file.
