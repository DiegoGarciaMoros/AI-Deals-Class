## Nebraska v. Iowa (Supreme Court of the United States, 1892)

**Facts**
- Iowa was admitted to the Union in 1846, with its western boundary defined as the middle of the main channel of the Missouri River.
- Nebraska was admitted in 1867, with its eastern boundary likewise defined as the middle of the channel of the Missouri River.
- Between 1861 and 1877, the Missouri River channel near Omaha underwent significant changes.
- In 1877, the river above Omaha, which had followed an ox-bow course, suddenly cut through the neck of the bow, forming a new channel.
- Nebraska filed an original suit against Iowa to determine the boundary line between the two states.
- Iowa answered, claiming the disputed ground was part of its territory, and filed a cross-bill for affirmative relief.

**Procedural History**
- Nebraska filed an original suit in the Supreme Court of the United States.
- Iowa answered and filed a cross-bill.
- Replications were filed and proofs taken.

**Issue**
Does the boundary line between two states, defined by a river, change when the river's course shifts due to gradual accretion or sudden avulsion?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Nebraska) and Defendant (Iowa) both win in part. The boundary line between Iowa and Nebraska is a varying line where the river changes by accretion, but remains fixed in the old channel where the river changes by avulsion. Remanded for further proceedings to determine the exact boundary.

**Rules**
- **Accretion.** When land borders on running water and the banks change by gradual accretion, the riparian owner's boundary line remains the stream, including the accumulated soil.
- **Avulsion.** When a boundary stream suddenly abandons its old bed and seeks a new course, the boundary remains as it was, in the center of the old channel.
- **State Boundaries.** The rules of accretion and avulsion apply to states when a river forms one of their boundary lines.
- **Missouri River Accretion.** The law of accretion controls on the Missouri River, despite its rapid and great changes, because the process of deposition is still gradual and imperceptible.
- **Accretion Test.** The test for what is gradual and imperceptible is that though witnesses may see progress over time, they could not perceive it while the process was actually occurring.

**Reasoning**
- The court relies on established common law principles regarding riparian boundaries.
- **Accretion:**
    - When changes are gradual and imperceptible, the boundary shifts with the stream.
    - This rule is based on the idea that a proprietor subject to loss by gradual erosion should also benefit from gradual gain.
    - The Missouri River, despite its rapid changes, still experiences accretion because the earth particles disintegrate and deposit gradually, not as solid masses.
- **Avulsion:**
    - When a stream suddenly changes its course, the boundary remains in the center of the old, abandoned channel.
    - This is because a sudden, violent change does not alter the established boundary.
    - The 1877 change in the Missouri River, where it cut through an ox-bow, was an avulsion.
- These principles apply equally to boundaries between private landowners and between states or nations.
- The court cites numerous authorities, including international law scholars and civil law, to support the universal recognition of these rules.
- The court previously held in *Jefferis v. East Omaha Land Co.* that the general law of accretion applies to the Missouri River, and the current evidence supports this.
- The 1877 change in the river's course was a clear case of avulsion, fixing the boundary in the old channel.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is foundational for understanding how **riparian boundaries** are determined, particularly the distinction between **accretion** and **avulsion**. It connects to the concept of **owner sovereignty** by defining how property lines shift or remain fixed in response to natural changes in water bodies. It builds on the idea that property rights are not always static but can be influenced by natural phenomena, as seen in cases like *Ghen v. Rich* (custom in whaling) or *Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois* (public trust doctrine over submerged lands).
2. The court's definition of "imperceptible" for accretion, allowing for discernment "at intervals of not less than three or more months," seems to stretch the common understanding of the word. This raises the question of how frequently observation can occur before a change is no longer considered "imperceptible" and thus not accretion.

**Takeaway:** The boundary between properties or states defined by a river shifts with gradual changes (accretion) but remains fixed in the old channel if the river suddenly changes course (avulsion).
