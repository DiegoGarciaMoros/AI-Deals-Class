## Pile v. Pedrick (Supreme Court of Pennsylvania, 1895)

**Facts**
- Defendants intended to build a factory wall entirely on their own land.
- Defendants hired a city surveyor to locate their property line.
- Defendants built a foundation wall based on the surveyor's line.
- Subsequent surveys revealed the foundation wall encroached on plaintiffs' land by about one and three-eighths inches below the surface for a portion of its length. The brick wall above the foundation was entirely on defendants' land.
- Defendants offered to make the wall a party wall by agreement, allowing plaintiffs free use, if windows on the upper floors could remain open until plaintiffs needed the wall. Plaintiffs declined.
- Defendants sought permission to enter plaintiffs' land to chip off the projecting ends of the foundation stones, offering to pay for any inconvenience or injury. Plaintiffs refused.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs filed a bill in equity to compel removal of the wall and windows.
- The master found the foundation wall encroached on plaintiffs' property.
- The trial court ordered defendants to remove the encroaching portion of their wall, replacing it with a wall entirely on their own ground, without entering plaintiffs' land. The court divided costs equally.
- Both plaintiffs and defendants appealed.

**Issue**
- Can a court of equity order a defendant to remove an encroaching foundation wall, even if the encroachment is slight and unintentional, and the plaintiff refuses entry to allow a less costly removal method?

**Holding**
- Plaintiffs win. The court can compel removal of an encroaching foundation wall, and the defendant must remove it from their own side if the plaintiff denies entry. Affirmed (with modification to removal timeline).

**Rules**
- **Trespass.** A landowner has no right at law or in equity to occupy land that does not belong to them.
- **Equity Costs.** Costs in equity are not automatic; they are within the chancellor's discretion and may be given or withheld as equity and good conscience require.
- **Party Wall.** A wall not intended to be a party wall and built entirely on one's own land (except for an unintentional, slight subsurface encroachment) is not a party wall.

**Reasoning**
- The foundation wall was not a party wall because defendants intended to build entirely on their own land and did so, except for the unintentional subsurface encroachment.
- Defendants have no right to occupy plaintiffs' land, even if the encroachment is slight and unintentional.
- Since plaintiffs refused permission for defendants to enter their land to chip off the encroaching stones, the only remaining method to remedy the trespass is for defendants to take down and rebuild the entire wall from their own side.
- The court's division of costs was a proper exercise of discretion, given the circumstances and the parties' conduct, as equity often enforces legal rights under circumstances that cause hardship to the defendant.
- The decree was modified to allow one year for removal to avoid further controversy.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the strict protection of property boundaries, even for minor, unintentional encroachments. It contrasts with cases like *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands*, where a court might balance hardships and deny an injunction for a minor encroachment if the cost of removal is disproportionate to the injury. Here, the court emphasizes the owner's absolute right to exclude.
2. This case raises the question of whether a property owner has a duty to mitigate damages or cooperate with a trespasser to minimize the cost of removal, especially when the trespass was unintentional and minor. What if the cost of removing the entire wall was astronomically high compared to the value of the encroached land?

**Takeaway:** Even a slight, unintentional subsurface encroachment by a building's foundation constitutes a trespass that a court will order removed, and the encroacher must bear the full cost of removal, even if it means rebuilding the entire structure from their own side if the neighbor refuses access.
