## Fair Housing Council of San Fernando Valley v. Roommate.com LLC (United States Court of Appeals, Ninth Circuit, 2012)

**Facts**
- Roommate.com, LLC (Roommate) operates a website that helps people find roommates.
- Users create profiles by answering questions about their sex, sexual orientation, and whether children will live with them.
- Users also list preferences for roommate characteristics, including sex, sexual orientation, and familial status.
- Roommate matches users and provides lists based on these profiles and preferences.
- The Fair Housing Councils of San Fernando Valley and San Diego (FHCs) sued Roommate, alleging its website's practices violated the Fair Housing Act (FHA) and the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA).
- Prior to litigation, the FHCs investigated Roommate's alleged violations and started new education and outreach campaigns targeting discriminatory roommate advertising.

**Procedural History**
- The district court initially dismissed the claims, finding Roommate immune under the Communications Decency Act (CDA).
- The Ninth Circuit reversed, holding Roommate was not immune for posting questionnaires requiring disclosure of protected characteristics, limiting searches based on preferences, and matching users based on those preferences.
- On remand, the district court held Roommate's activities violated the FHA and FEHA and issued a permanent injunction.
- The district court also awarded the FHCs attorney's fees.
- Roommate appealed the summary judgment, injunction, and attorney's fees.
- The FHCs cross-appealed the amount of attorney's fees.

**Issue**
Does the Fair Housing Act (FHA) apply to the selection of roommates in shared living situations?

**Holding**
Defendant (Roommate.com) wins. The FHA does not apply to the selection of roommates in shared living situations. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Fair Housing Act (FHA) Scope.** The FHA prohibits discrimination in the "sale or rental of a dwelling" based on protected characteristics.
- **FHA Definition of Dwelling.** A "dwelling" is defined as "any building, structure, or portion thereof which is occupied as, or designed or intended for occupancy as, a residence by one or more families."
- **Right to Intimate Association.** The freedom to enter into and carry on certain intimate or private relationships is a fundamental element of liberty protected by the Bill of Rights.
- **Canon of Constitutional Avoidance.** Where an otherwise acceptable construction of a statute would raise serious constitutional problems, courts will construe the statute to avoid such problems unless such construction is plainly contrary to the intent of Congress.
- **California Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) Definition of Housing Accommodation.** FEHA defines "housing accommodation" similarly to the FHA's definition of "dwelling."
- **Rejected: Subsequent Legislative History.** The acts of a subsequent legislature tell nothing definitive about the meaning of laws adopted by an earlier legislature.

**Reasoning**
- The FHA's reach depends on the meaning of "dwelling."
    - Interpreting "dwelling" as an independent living unit and stopping the FHA at the front door makes practical sense.
    - Applying the FHA to sub-parts of a home or apartment leads to awkward results.
    - There is no indication Congress intended to interfere with personal relationships inside the home.
    - Congress aimed to address discrimination by landlords in the sale and rental of housing, which is different from arrangements between people sharing living space.
    - Applying the FHA to roommate selection would lead to results that defy mores prevalent when the statute was passed (e.g., requiring women to accept men as roommates).
- Constitutional concerns provide strong countervailing considerations.
    - The right to intimate association protects highly personal relationships, including the roommate relationship, which involves few people, selectivity, and exclusion of non-roommates from critical aspects.
    - Choosing a roommate implicates significant privacy and safety considerations due to unfettered access to the home, which is the center of private lives.
        - Roommates observe personal habits, see each other in various stages of undress, and learn intimate details.
        - Roommates have access to physical belongings and person, making individuals vulnerable.
        - Individuals are exposed to a roommate's belongings, activities, and habits, which could be offensive, dangerous, or annoying.
        - Government regulation of roommate selection would intrude into the home and restrict the ability to choose compatible roommates, seriously invading privacy, autonomy, and security.
    - Examples illustrate these concerns: women seeking female roommates for modesty/security, orthodox Jews seeking roommates with similar beliefs/dietary restrictions.
    - HUD has previously deferred to constitutional considerations in similar roommate advertising cases.
- The FHA can reasonably be read to either include or exclude shared living arrangements.
    - Because applying the FHA to shared living units raises substantial constitutional concerns, the court must choose the construction that avoids these problems.
    - Reading "dwelling" to mean an independent housing unit is a fair interpretation consistent with congressional intent and avoids constitutional issues.
- Since the FHA does not apply to the sharing of living units, Roommate's facilitation of roommate searches is not unlawful under the FHA.
    - Roommate has standing to raise the constitutional claims of its users.
- The same constitutional concerns apply to the FEHA.
    - The FEHA's definition of "housing accommodation" is ambiguous, allowing the application of the canon of constitutional avoidance.
    - The court interprets FEHA to exclude the sharing of living units to avoid constitutional problems.
    - A 1995 FEHA amendment regarding sex-specific advertisements for shared living areas does not definitively indicate the original intent of the statute.
    - The court rejects the argument that a FEHC decision clarifies FEHA's ambiguity, as the FEHC did not consider constitutional avoidance.

**Dissent (Ikuta)**
- Disagrees with the majority's standing analysis, arguing that the FHCs did not suffer an "injury in fact" under Supreme Court precedent (Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife). The FHCs' voluntary redirection of resources to combat online discrimination was adaptive management, not an injury.
- Dissents from the majority's application of constitutional avoidance to the FEHA. FEHA's language, particularly the 1995 amendment allowing sex-specific advertisements for shared living units but not other protected characteristics, makes it unambiguous that it applies to shared living arrangements for characteristics like race or religion. The California Fair Employment and Housing Commission's decision in Larrick confirms this interpretation.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the limits of anti-discrimination laws in the context of private living arrangements. It connects to the concept of **owner autonomy** and the **right to exclude** within one's home, which is a core theme in property law. It also highlights the tension between public policy goals (anti-discrimination) and individual constitutional rights (intimate association, privacy). This case could be compared with cases like *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.* or *Shelley v. Kraemer* to discuss the extent to which the government can regulate property use and private agreements.
2. The dissent raises an interesting point about the 1995 FEHA amendment. If the amendment explicitly carved out an exception for sex-based discrimination in shared living, does that imply that other forms of discrimination (e.g., race) are still covered? This creates a potential open question regarding the scope of FEHA for other protected classes in roommate situations.

**Takeaway:** Anti-discrimination laws like the FHA and FEHA do not apply to the selection of roommates in shared living situations due to constitutional concerns about the right to intimate association and privacy within the home.
