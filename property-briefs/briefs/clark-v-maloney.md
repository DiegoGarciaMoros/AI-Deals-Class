## Clark v. Maloney (Delaware Superior Court, 1840)

**Facts**
- The plaintiff, Clark, found ten white pine logs floating in the Delaware Bay after a freshet.
- Clark took the logs and moored them with ropes in the mouth of Mispillion Creek.
- The defendants, John and Robert Maloney, later possessed these logs.
- The defendants refused to return the logs to Clark, claiming they found them adrift and floating up the creek.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiff sued defendants in an action of trover.
- The jury found for the plaintiff.

**Issue**
- Does a first finder of lost chattel who subsequently loses it again have a superior property right to a second finder?

**Holding**
- Plaintiff wins. A first finder of lost chattel has a special property right that is superior to a second finder, even if the first finder loses possession. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Possession as Evidence of Property.** Possession of a chattel is prima facie evidence of property.
- **Finder's Rights.** The finder of a chattel acquires a property right in it that is good against all but the rightful owner.
- **Loss of Chattel.** The loss of a chattel does not change the right of property.
- **Special Property.** A finder's property right is a "special property" that is not divested by subsequent loss.

**Reasoning**
- The plaintiff showed possession of the logs by taking them from the bay and mooring them.
- Possession is prima facie evidence of property, rebuttable only by a better title.
- The defendants did not claim to be the rightful owners or claim under them.
- The defendants' argument that their title as second finders is as good as the plaintiff's is incorrect.
- The original owner's loss of the logs did not divest their absolute property.
- Similarly, the plaintiff's subsequent loss of the logs did not divest his special property.
- The plaintiff never abandoned his special property in the logs.

**Dissent ([Judge])** / **Concurrence ([Judge])**
- None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case reinforces the principle established in *Armory v. Delamirie* that a finder has a property right good against all but the true owner. *Clark v. Maloney* extends this by clarifying that this special property right is not lost if the finder subsequently loses possession, making it superior to a later finder. It contrasts with cases like *Pierson v. Post* and *Keeble v. Hickeringill* which deal with initial acquisition of wild animals, as here the chattel was already "owned" by someone, just lost.
2. What if the second finder had invested significant labor in retrieving the logs from a dangerous situation after the first finder lost them? Would that change the outcome, or is the "special property" of the first finder absolute against all but the true owner?

**Takeaway:** A first finder of lost property has a superior claim to that property against all subsequent finders, even if the first finder loses possession.
