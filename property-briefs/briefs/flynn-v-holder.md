## Flynn v. Holder (United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 2012)

**Facts**
- The National Organ Transplant Act (NOTA) was enacted in 1984, prohibiting compensation for "human organs," including "bone marrow."
- At the time NOTA was passed, bone marrow was extracted by aspiration, a painful procedure involving needles inserted into hip bones.
- Over the last twenty years, a new method called "peripheral blood stem cell apheresis" developed, which extracts hematopoietic stem cells from a donor's bloodstream after medication stimulates stem cell production. This method does not involve extracting the soft, fatty marrow from bones.
- Plaintiffs include parents of sick children needing bone marrow transplants, a physician, and a nonprofit organization (MoreMarrowDonors.org).
- MoreMarrowDonors.org proposes a pilot program to offer $3,000 awards (scholarships, housing allowances, or charitable gifts) to minority and mixed-race donors of bone marrow cells, acknowledging this would be "valuable consideration" under NOTA.
- Plaintiffs filed a complaint challenging the constitutionality of NOTA's ban on compensation for bone marrow donations, particularly as applied to peripheral blood stem cell apheresis.

**Procedural History**
- The district court dismissed the complaint for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted.
- Plaintiffs appealed.

**Issue**
Can the National Organ Transplant Act's prohibition on compensating "bone marrow" donors be applied to donations of hematopoietic stem cells extracted from peripheral blood via apheresis?

**Holding**
Plaintiffs (appellants) win. The National Organ Transplant Act does not prohibit compensation for donations of hematopoietic stem cells extracted from peripheral blood via apheresis. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Statutory Interpretation.** Courts must construe the words of a statute to see what they imply, considering ordinary usage.
- **Rational Basis Review.** A legislative classification must have a rational basis, but does not need to fit perfectly with that rational basis, and the basis need merely be rational, not persuasive to all.
- **Rejected: Statutory Definition of "Bone Marrow" Includes Peripheral Blood Stem Cells.** The government's argument that "bone marrow" includes hematopoietic stem cells in the veins as "subparts" of bone marrow is rejected because it would also prohibit compensation for blood donations, which the government concedes are not prohibited.
- **Rejected: Later Statutory Definition Applies.** A definition of "bone marrow" in a later statute (Stem Cell Therapeutic and Research Act of 2005) that includes cells found in peripheral blood is limited to provisions "in this part" of that statute and does not extend to NOTA.

**Reasoning**
- The court construes "bone marrow" in NOTA to mean the soft, fatty substance in bone cavities, consistent with ordinary usage at the time the statute was passed.
- The apheresis method of extracting hematopoietic stem cells does not involve removing the soft, fatty marrow from bones; it extracts cells from the bloodstream.
- The government concedes that compensation for blood donations is not prohibited by NOTA, and hematopoietic stem cells, when in the bloodstream, are a "subpart" of the blood, not the bone marrow.
- If the government's argument that anything originating from bone marrow is a "subpart" of bone marrow were accepted, it would illogically prohibit compensation for all blood donations, as red and white blood cells also originate in the bone marrow.
- The legislative history of NOTA indicates that Congress viewed replenishable tissues like blood as falling outside the definition of "human organ."
- While the prohibition on compensation for bone marrow donations by the aspiration method has a rational basis (policy concerns about commodification, exploitation, and philosophical concerns about bodily integrity), this reasoning does not apply to peripheral blood stem cell apheresis.
- The court does not need to address the constitutional challenge regarding apheresis donations because the statute does not prohibit compensation for them.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding how courts interpret statutory language, especially when new technologies emerge that were not contemplated at the time of enactment. It connects to the concept of **property rights in one's body** and the limits of commodification, similar to issues raised in *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* and *Hecht v. Superior Court*, though *Flynn* focuses on statutory interpretation rather than common law property rights. The court's discussion of the rational basis for prohibiting compensation for traditional bone marrow donations touches on the philosophical and policy arguments against commodification of body parts, which is a recurring theme in property law.
2. The court explicitly avoids deciding the constitutionality of prohibiting compensation for apheresis donations, instead finding that the statute simply doesn't apply. This leaves open the question of whether Congress *could* constitutionally prohibit such compensation if it chose to amend the statute.

**Takeaway:** The National Organ Transplant Act's ban on compensating "bone marrow" donors does not apply to hematopoietic stem cells collected from peripheral blood via apheresis, as these are considered part of the blood, not bone marrow, under the statute's ordinary meaning.
