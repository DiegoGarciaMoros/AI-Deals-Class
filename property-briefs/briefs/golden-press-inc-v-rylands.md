## Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands (Colorado Supreme Court, 1951)

**Facts**
- Rylands and Reid (plaintiffs) owned a parcel of land with a residence, garage, and rental cottages.
- Golden Press, Inc. (defendant) constructed a one-story business building on its adjoining property.
- The west wall of the defendant's building was two inches clear of the lot line at the front, on the line at the north end, and approximately 160 feet long.
- During construction, the defendant allegedly trespassed on the plaintiffs' property by dropping an I-beam on their garage roof, destroying a flower bed and fence, disturbing a driveway, and digging into their land.
- The plaintiffs and defendant each employed surveyors before construction, but their results were not fully disclosed, and the building progressed.
- During construction, plaintiff Rylands' brother, Thomas P. Nother, informed the defendant's construction superintendent that the building was about eight inches over the line, but the superintendent stated they had their own surveyor who put them on the line.
- The defendant's witness, Argo, who supervised wall construction, testified that after a complaint from the neighbor, their surveyor told them to move over about two inches, and they did.
- The plaintiffs continually complained about trespass by workmen but did not seek an injunction until after the foundation and upper wall were completed.
- A survey by Coberly, employed by the plaintiffs just before trial, showed the defendant's footings encroached two inches at the middle and three and a half inches at the north end.
- The encroaching footings were seven to nine feet below the surface and did not interfere with the plaintiffs' current use of the property as a driveway and iris bed.
- The plaintiffs refused the defendant permission to chip off the encroaching footings from their property and demanded removal from the defendant's side, even if it meant tearing down the wall.

**Procedural History**
- Plaintiffs sued for an injunction to remove footings, an injunction against trespass, and $1750 in damages plus exemplary damages.
- The case was tried to a jury on the issue of damages for trespass, with the court reserving the injunction issue.
- The jury returned a verdict in favor of the defendant on damages.
- The trial court found encroachment, granted a mandatory injunction requiring removal of the footings, and ordered the defendant to direct drivers and remove confusing signs.
- The trial court set aside the jury's verdict and granted a new trial on damages.
- Defendant appealed the injunction and the setting aside of the jury verdict.

**Issue**
Can a mandatory injunction be issued to compel the removal of a slight, unintentional building encroachment when the cost of removal is disproportionate to the harm caused to the plaintiff?

**Holding**
Defendant (Golden Press, Inc.) wins. A mandatory injunction for the removal of a slight, unintentional encroachment should be denied where the cost of removal is greatly disproportionate to the plaintiff's damage and the plaintiff's use of the property is not significantly affected. Reversed and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Mandatory Injunction for Encroachment.** Ordinarily, a mandatory injunction will issue to compel the removal of encroaching structures, but it is not to be issued as a matter of course.
- **Equitable Considerations.** On appeal to the court for an equitable remedy, the court must consider the peculiar equities of the case.
- **Willful Encroachment.** Where the encroachment is deliberate and constitutes a willful and intentional taking of another’s land, equity may well require its restoration regardless of the expense of removal as compared with damage suffered therefrom.
- **Good Faith Encroachment.** Where the encroachment was in good faith, the court should weigh the circumstances so that it shall not act oppressively.
- **Balance of Convenience (Rejected as sole test).** Rejected: The mere balance of convenience is not the proper test.
- **Relative Hardship.** Relative hardship may properly be considered, and the court should not become a party to extortion.
- **Denial of Mandatory Injunction.** Where defendant’s encroachment is unintentional and slight, plaintiff’s use not affected and his damage small and fairly compensable, while the cost of removal is so great as to cause grave hardship or otherwise make its removal unconscionable, mandatory injunction may properly be denied and plaintiff relegated to compensation in damages.
- **Clarity of Injunction.** A judgment must be definite and certain in itself, clearly fixing the rights and liabilities of the parties.

**Reasoning**
- The trial court's order setting aside the jury verdict and granting a new trial on damages was discretionary and not a final judgment, so it cannot be reviewed on appeal.
- The portion of the injunction regarding directing drivers and removing signs was too vague and indefinite to stand.
- The court found no evidence challenging the good faith of the defendant's representatives in locating the footings, despite ongoing arguments during construction.
- The encroachment was very slight (two to three and a half inches) and entirely below ground (seven to nine feet deep).
- The encroachment did not interfere with the plaintiffs' current use of the property.
- The only potential future damage was that the plaintiffs would have to detour around the footings if they built to their line with a basement.
- The value of the encroached land was very small (approximately $55 for the entire strip if taken).
- The expense and hardship of removing the footings (potentially requiring tearing down the wall) would be extremely great compared to the minimal advantage gained by the plaintiffs.
- Requiring removal would be unconscionable given the circumstances.
- The plaintiffs' delay in seeking an injunction until after construction was complete weighed against them.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding the **equitable remedy** of a mandatory injunction, particularly in the context of **encroachments**. It demonstrates that courts will not always grant an injunction, even when a property right has been violated, if the hardship to the defendant is disproportionate to the benefit to the plaintiff. This contrasts with cases like *Pile v. Pedrick*, where a mandatory injunction was granted for a slight encroachment, emphasizing a stricter adherence to property lines. It also connects to the broader theme of balancing property rights with equitable considerations, similar to the discussion of remedies in *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C.* regarding patent infringement.
2. The court's finding that there was "no evidence from the record challenging the good faith of defendant’s representative in locating the footings" despite testimony about warnings and adjustments raises a question about what constitutes "good faith" in an encroachment. If the defendant was warned and made an adjustment, but still encroached, how much more evidence of bad faith is required?

**Takeaway:** Courts may deny a mandatory injunction for a minor, good-faith encroachment if the cost of removal is unconscionably high compared to the plaintiff's minimal harm, relegating the plaintiff to damages.
