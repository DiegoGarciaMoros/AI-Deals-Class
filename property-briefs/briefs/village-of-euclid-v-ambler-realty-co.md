## Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co. (Supreme Court of the United States, 1926)

**Facts**
- Ambler Realty Co. owned a 68-acre tract of undeveloped land in the Village of Euclid, Ohio.
- The land was located in the path of industrial development from the nearby city of Cleveland.
- On November 13, 1922, the Village Council adopted a comprehensive zoning ordinance.
- The ordinance divided the village into six classes of use districts (U-1 to U-6), three classes of height districts (H-1 to H-3), and four classes of area districts (A-1 to A-4).
- Ambler Realty Co.'s land was classified into U-2 (two-family dwellings), U-3 (apartment houses, hotels, etc.), and U-6 (industrial uses).
- The zoning restrictions significantly reduced the market value of Ambler Realty Co.'s land.

**Procedural History**
- Ambler Realty Co. sued the Village of Euclid in federal district court, seeking an injunction to prevent the enforcement of the zoning ordinance.
- The district court held the ordinance unconstitutional and void, enjoining its enforcement.
- The Village of Euclid appealed to the Supreme Court of the United States.

**Issue**
Can a municipal zoning ordinance that restricts the use of private property, thereby reducing its value, be a valid exercise of the police power under the Fourteenth Amendment?

**Holding**
The Village of Euclid wins. A comprehensive zoning ordinance is a valid exercise of police power if its provisions are not clearly arbitrary and unreasonable, and have a substantial relation to public health, safety, morals, or general welfare. Reversed.

**Rules**
- **Police Power.** The scope of the application of constitutional guarantees must expand or contract to meet new and different conditions.
- **Police Power and Nuisance.** The question of whether the power exists to forbid a particular building or use, like the question of whether a particular thing is a nuisance, is determined by considering it in connection with the circumstances and locality, not abstractly.
- **Legislative Judgment.** If the validity of legislative classification for zoning purposes is fairly debatable, the legislative judgment must control.
- **Zoning and Public Welfare.** The exclusion of buildings devoted to business, trade, etc., from residential districts bears a rational relation to the health and safety of the community.
- **Judicial Scrutiny of Ordinances.** When an injunction is sought against an ordinance on the broad ground that its mere existence and threatened enforcement constitute an irreparable injury, and the court finds the ordinance in its general scope and dominant features valid, the court will not scrutinize its provisions sentence by sentence to find minor unconstitutional parts.

**Reasoning**
- The meaning of constitutional guarantees never varies, but their application must adapt to new conditions.
- Urban life has become more complex, requiring additional restrictions on land use. Regulations that were once considered arbitrary may now be necessary and valid.
- Zoning ordinances find their justification in the police power, asserted for the public welfare.
- The line between legitimate and illegitimate exercise of police power is not precise and varies with circumstances.
- The principle of *sic utere tuo ut alienum non laedas* (use your own property in such a manner as not to injure that of another) and the law of nuisances provide helpful analogies for understanding the scope of police power.
- The exclusion of industrial establishments from residential areas, even those that are not inherently offensive, can be justified as a reasonable margin to ensure effective enforcement and because the distinction between "good" and "bad" industries can be difficult to draw.
- The Village of Euclid, as a separate municipality, has the authority to govern itself and direct the course of industrial development within its borders, even if it diverts development from what would otherwise be its natural course.
- The exclusion of apartment houses, business houses, and retail stores from residential districts is a serious question, but many state courts have upheld such power.
- Reasons for upholding such exclusions include:
    - Promotion of health and safety for children by separating dwellings from trade/industry.
    - Suppression of disorder and facilitation of fire extinguishment and traffic regulation.
    - Easier and less expensive street construction and repair by concentrating heavy traffic.
    - Apartment houses can be parasitic in detached house sections, interfering with air circulation, sunlight, and bringing increased traffic and noise, ultimately destroying the residential character.
- These reasons are sufficiently cogent to prevent the Court from declaring the ordinance clearly arbitrary and unreasonable, or lacking a substantial relation to public health, safety, morals, or general welfare.
- The lawsuit attacked the ordinance as an entirety, claiming its mere existence caused irreparable injury. The Court will not engage in a piecemeal dissection of every provision when the general scope is valid.
- Specific complaints about particular provisions or applications should be addressed as they arise.

**Dissent (Van Devanter, McReynolds, Butler)**
- None. (The opinion states "Mr. Justice Van Devanter, Mr. Justice McReynolds and Mr. Justice Butler, dissent." but provides no reasoning.)

**NOTES:**
1. This case is foundational for understanding **zoning** as a legitimate exercise of the state's police power. It establishes the constitutionality of comprehensive land-use planning, moving beyond the traditional nuisance framework. It connects to cases like *Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon* by further defining the limits of government regulation on private property, but here, the regulation is upheld as a valid exercise of police power rather than a taking.
2. The Court explicitly states that some provisions, if attacked separately, might not withstand constitutional scrutiny. This leaves open the question of "as-applied" challenges to zoning ordinances, even if the general scheme is valid.

**Takeaway:** Zoning ordinances are generally constitutional if they bear a rational relation to public health, safety, morals, or general welfare, even if they diminish property value.
