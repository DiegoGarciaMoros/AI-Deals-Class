## Postema v. Postema (Michigan Court of Appeals, 1991)

**Facts**
- Kathryn and Miles Postema married on August 11, 1984.
- At the time of marriage, Miles was a cost accountant, and Kathryn was a licensed practical nurse pursuing an associate's degree.
- The couple planned for Miles to attend law school and Kathryn to work full-time to support them.
- Shortly after marriage, they moved to Detroit, and Miles attended Wayne State University Law School from September 1984 to May 1987.
- Kathryn worked full-time at a hospital, earning approximately $53,000 during Miles's law school attendance, and assumed primary household responsibilities.
- Miles worked as a law clerk during summers and part-time during his second and third years of law school, earning approximately $12,000.
- Miles's education was primarily financed by $15,000 in student loans.
- Miles graduated in May 1987, and they moved back to Grand Rapids, where Miles became an associate attorney with an annual salary of $41,000.
- In September 1987, Kathryn resumed classes for her associate's degree in nursing.
- In November 1987, the parties separated.
- Kathryn continued her classes and received her associate's degree in May 1988, supporting herself by working full-time.
- The trial court found the breakdown of the marriage was primarily Miles's fault.

**Procedural History**
- Kathryn L. Postema brought an action for divorce in Kent Circuit Court.
- The trial court entered a judgment of divorce, valuing Miles's law degree at $80,000 and awarding Kathryn $32,000 as her share.
- Miles appealed, and Kathryn cross-appealed.
- The Court of Appeals affirmed in part and remanded.

**Issue**
Can a law degree obtained by one spouse during marriage be considered a marital asset subject to equitable distribution upon divorce, and if so, how should the non-degree-earning spouse's claim be valued?

**Holding**
Plaintiff (Kathryn) wins in part. A postgraduate degree obtained during marriage through a concerted family effort gives rise to a divisible marital asset, subject to an equitable claim by the other spouse for compensation for unrewarded sacrifices and contributions. The valuation should focus on compensating the non-degree-earning spouse for their contributions, not on the degree's market value. Affirmed in part and remanded.

**Rules**
- **Equitable Distribution of Marital Assets.** The goal in dividing a marital estate is a fair and equitable distribution under all circumstances, not governed by rigid rules or mathematical formulas.
- **Postgraduate Degree as Marital Asset.** A postgraduate degree obtained by one spouse during the marriage as the end product of a concerted family effort involving mutual sacrifice and effort gives rise, upon divorce, to a divisible marital asset, subject to an equitable claim by the other spouse for compensation for unrewarded sacrifices, efforts, and contributions toward attainment of the degree.
- **"Concerted Family Effort."** This concept involves mutual sacrifice, effort, and contribution of both parties as part of a larger, long-range plan intended to benefit the family as a whole, including financial contributions, increased household responsibilities, shared emotional burdens, and postponement of personal educational or career pursuits by the non-student spouse.
- **Rejected: Degree as Alimony Factor.** An advanced degree is not properly considered as a factor in awarding alimony because alimony is for support, while compensation for a degree is based on equitable recognition of contributions, and alimony can be terminated upon remarriage.
- **Rejected: Degree as "Property" in the Classic Sense.** The physical or metaphysical definition of a degree as "property" is not decisive; courts must focus on the most equitable solution for dissolving the marriage and dividing holdings.
- **Valuation of Equitable Claim for Advanced Degree.** Valuation involves a two-step analysis:
    1. Examination of the sacrifices, efforts, and contributions of the non-student spouse toward attainment of the degree.
    2. Determination of the most equitable remedy or means of compensating the non-student spouse under the facts of the case, considering:
        - The length of the marriage after the degree was obtained.
        - The sources and extent of financial support given to the degree holder during the years in school.
        - The overall division of the parties’ marital property.
- **Rejected: Present Value Method for Degree Valuation.** The present value method, which attempts to estimate future earnings attributable to the degree, is not an appropriate means to evaluate the equitable claim because it misconstrues the underlying premise that compensation is for contributions, not for an interest in the degree itself.
- **Focus of Compensation.** The focus of an award involving an advanced degree is to reimburse the non-student spouse for unrewarded sacrifices, efforts, and contributions toward attainment of the degree, not for "loss of expectations" or the degree's potential income.

**Reasoning**
- **Fairness and Equity:** Michigan law requires a fair and equitable distribution of marital assets, and equitable considerations form the basis for recognizing a claim for compensation for an advanced degree.
- **Concerted Family Effort:**
    - Kathryn postponed her education, moved with Miles, and worked full-time, providing approximately 80% of the couple's financial support while Miles attended law school.
    - Kathryn bore primary responsibility for household tasks.
    - Both parties experienced the stress of law school, with Miles blaming his behavior on stress and Kathryn trying to avoid agitating him.
    - These facts demonstrate a concerted family effort, making Miles's law degree the product of mutual sacrifice and contribution.
- **Characterization as Marital Asset/Equitable Claim:**
    - The court rejects treating the degree as a factor for alimony because alimony is for support, while this compensation is for recognized contributions. Alimony can also be terminated upon remarriage, which would unfairly jeopardize the non-student spouse's right.
    - The court rejects the idea that a degree cannot be considered because it lacks traditional property attributes (like transferability or inheritable value). The focus is on equitable distribution, not strict property definitions.
    - The non-student spouse's interest is an "equitable claim" arising from the concerted family effort, not a pecuniary interest in the degree itself.
- **Valuation:**
    - The trial court's valuation was unclear and failed to account for relevant considerations.
    - The present value method (estimating future earnings) is inappropriate because it implies a pecuniary interest in the degree, which the court rejects.
    - The proper focus is on compensating the non-student spouse for their sacrifices, efforts, and contributions.
    - Relevant factors for revaluation include: the short duration of the marriage after the degree, Kathryn's significant financial and non-pecuniary support, and the fact that she had to pursue her own education independently.
    - Miles is responsible for the student loans that financed his education.
- **Other Property Distribution:**
    - The court found no error in the distribution of automobiles, considering the source of funds and remaining debt.
    - Kathryn's $10,000 certificate of deposit was properly excluded as it was acquired before marriage and held jointly with her grandfather.
    - Miscellaneous household items were properly awarded to the party in possession.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is important for understanding how courts may address non-traditional "assets" in divorce proceedings, particularly when one spouse's career advancement is a direct result of the other's sacrifices. It connects to the broader theme of **personhood_dignity** and **labor_or_investment** as justifications for property rights, even when the "property" doesn't fit traditional definitions. It contrasts with cases like *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* where the court was unwilling to recognize a property interest in human cells, highlighting the different contexts (divorce vs. medical research) and the equitable considerations at play.
2. This case raises the question of how to quantify "sacrifices, efforts, and contributions" in a way that is consistent and fair across different divorce cases, especially when non-pecuniary contributions are involved.

**Takeaway:** While a professional degree itself may not be traditional "property," a non-degree-earning spouse's significant contributions to its attainment during marriage can create an equitable claim for compensation upon divorce.
