## 40 West 67th Street v. Pullman (New York Court of Appeals, 2003)

**Facts**
- In 1998, David Pullman (defendant) bought into the 40 West 67th Street cooperative and acquired shares for apartment 7B.
- Pullman began complaining about his elderly upstairs neighbors, accusing them of noise and running an illegal business.
- The cooperative's Board investigated Pullman's claims and found them to be false.
- Hostilities escalated, including a physical altercation between Pullman and the upstairs neighbor.
- Pullman distributed flyers to residents, calling the neighbor a "psychopath" and making other inflammatory accusations.
- Pullman made unauthorized alterations to his apartment and had weekend construction work done in violation of house rules.
- Pullman commenced four lawsuits against the upstairs couple, the cooperative president, and management, and attempted to start three more.
- The cooperative called a special meeting to determine if Pullman's conduct was "objectionable" under the lease agreement.
- Shareholders, by a vote of 2,048 to 0, passed a resolution declaring Pullman's conduct "objectionable" and directed the Board to terminate his proprietary lease and cancel his shares.
- The Board sent Pullman a notice of termination, requiring him to vacate his apartment.
- Pullman ignored the notice and remained in the apartment.

**Procedural History**
- The cooperative sued Pullman for possession and ejectment, cancellation of his stock, and a money judgment.
- Supreme Court denied the cooperative's motion for summary judgment, holding that the cooperative must prove objectionable conduct by competent evidence to the court's satisfaction, and declined to apply the business judgment rule.
- The Appellate Division, in a divided decision, granted the cooperative summary judgment, applying the business judgment rule.
- Pullman appealed to the New York Court of Appeals.

**Issue**
Can a residential cooperative terminate a shareholder-tenant's lease for objectionable conduct based on a shareholder vote, reviewed under the business judgment rule, or must a court independently evaluate the reasonableness of the cooperative's action?

**Holding**
The cooperative (plaintiff) wins. The business judgment rule governs a cooperative's decision to terminate a tenancy in accordance with the terms of the parties' agreement. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Business Judgment Rule (for cooperatives).** A court should defer to a cooperative board's determination so long as the board acts for the purposes of the cooperative, within the scope of its authority, and in good faith.
- **RPAPL 711 (1).** A proceeding to recover possession of real property based on a lease termination for objectionable conduct requires the landlord to establish by competent evidence to the satisfaction of the court that the tenant is objectionable.
- **Rejected: Independent Judicial Review.** A court is not required to make its own independent evaluation of the reasonableness of a cooperative's action to terminate a tenancy for objectionable conduct.

**Reasoning**
- The **business judgment rule** best balances the individual and collective interests in a residential cooperative setting, protecting the interests of the entire community.
- The rule protects cooperative decisions against "undue court involvement and judicial second-guessing."
- The business judgment rule can be applied consistently with **RPAPL 711 (1)**; the competent evidence required by the statute is satisfied by the application of the business judgment rule to the shareholder vote and findings.
- The cooperative acted within the **scope of its authority** by following the procedures in the lease agreement for termination, which Pullman voluntarily entered into.
- The cooperative's action legitimately furthered the **corporate purpose** by terminating a tenancy after shareholders found Pullman's behavior intolerable, as the Board has a fiduciary duty to further the collective interests.
- Pullman failed to show any **bad faith, arbitrariness, favoritism, discrimination, or malice** on the cooperative's part.
- While the business judgment rule is deferential, courts must exercise "heightened vigilance" in examining tenancy terminations to ensure the board's action meets the Levandusky test and is not a "rubber stamp" for abuse.
- Cooperative living entails voluntary, shared control, and shareholder-tenants agree to submit to the authority of the board, which "may significantly restrict the bundle of rights a property owner normally enjoys."
