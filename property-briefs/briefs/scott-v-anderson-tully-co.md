## Scott v. Anderson-Tully Co. (Mississippi Court of Appeals, 2015)

**Facts**
- In 1925, Stewart Scott Jr. and Willie Scott inherited an undivided one-half interest in a 584.6-acre tract of land.
- In 1944, a plat prepared for a timber deed from the Scotts noted a wire fence on the east side of the property and stated that "20 1/10 [acres] outside of fence is included in total acreage," but also that the property "outside of fence & may be disputed."
- In 1954, Richard Scott, an heir, rebuilt the wire fence, moving it slightly west along the tree line.
- In 1969, Anderson-Tully Company purchased land adjacent to the Scotts' property via quitclaim deed.
- In 1969, Anderson-Tully's surveyor, E.C. Burkhardt, surveyed the property and determined the boundary line ran along the wire fence, making the disputed twenty acres part of Anderson-Tully's property.
- From 1969 to 2010, Anderson-Tully marked its perceived boundary line along the wire fence with blue paint, repainting it in 1986 and 1998.
- From 1969 to 2010, Anderson-Tully maintained the disputed acreage, cut and harvested timber in 1990, 1999, and 2010, and issued five hunting licenses for the property.
- In 2003, Scott objected to Anderson-Tully's possession.
- In 2007, Scott cancelled a hunting lease with the Linwood Hunting Club, which had leased a portion of the Scotts' property.
- In December 2008, Anderson-Tully's attorney sent Scott a letter stating Anderson-Tully owned the disputed tract.
- In early 2010, Anderson-Tully harvested timber on the disputed land.
- On March 19, 2010, Scott filed suit.

**Procedural History**
- Scott filed suit in Jefferson County Chancery Court seeking damages for timber removal, to quiet and confirm title, and to enjoin Anderson-Tully from entering the land.
- Anderson-Tully counterclaimed to quiet and confirm its own title, arguing it owned the property by deed or, alternatively, by adverse possession.
- The chancellor dismissed Scott's claim, finding Anderson-Tully had acquired title through adverse possession.
- Scott filed a Rule 60(b) motion for relief from judgment or a new trial, which the chancellor denied.
- Scott appealed to the Mississippi Court of Appeals.

**Issue**
Can a party acquire title to land through adverse possession if they continuously and openly possess the land for ten years, even if their deed does not clearly describe the property?

**Holding**
Defendant (Anderson-Tully) wins. A party can acquire title to land through adverse possession by proving all six elements by clear and convincing evidence, even if their deed does not accurately describe the disputed property. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Adverse Possession.** To establish adverse possession, the claimant must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the possession was (1) under claim of ownership; (2) actual or hostile; (3) open, notorious, and visible; (4) continuous and uninterrupted for a period of ten years; (5) exclusive; and (6) peaceful.
- **Claim of Ownership.** The possessor must "fly [his] flag over the property" in such a way as to put the actual owner on notice that the property is "being held under an adverse claim of ownership." The quality, not the quantity, of acts must be considered.
- **Rejected: Mere presence of a fence.** The mere presence of a fence, without more, is not sufficient to sustain a claim of adverse possession.
- **Rejected: Payment of taxes alone.** The payment of taxes alone is not dispositive of the claim of ownership.
- **Actual or Hostile Possession.** Possession is hostile and adverse when the adverse possessor intends to claim title notwithstanding that the claim is made under a mistaken belief that the land is within the calls of the possessor's deed. The adverse possessor must possess the property without the owner's permission.
- **Possessory Acts for Wild Lands.** Possessory acts necessary to establish a claim of adverse possession may vary with the characteristics of the land, and adverse possession of "wild" or unimproved lands may be established by evidence of acts that would be wholly insufficient in the case of improved or developed lands. Possession of such property may be established by a continued claim evidenced by public acts of ownership.
- **Open, Notorious, and Visible Possession.** The possessor "must unfurl his flag on the land, and keep it flying, so that the actual owner may see, and if he will, that an enemy has invaded his domains, and planted the standard of conquest."
- **Continuous and Uninterrupted Possession.** Adverse possession requires continuous and uninterrupted possession of a disputed property for at least ten years.
- **Exclusive Possession.** Exclusive possession means that the possessor "evinces an intention to possess and hold land to the exclusion of, and in opposition to, the claims of all others, and the claimant’s conduct must afford an unequivocal indication that he is exercising the dominion of a sole owner." It does not mean that no one else can use the property.
- **Peaceful Possession.** An adverse possessor’s use of a claimed property must be peaceful. Expected disputes associated with the use or ownership of the property are not indicative of the possession not being peaceful.

**Reasoning**
- The chancellor's findings of fact are reviewed for manifest error or clear erroneousness, and will be affirmed if supported by substantial evidence.
- **Claim of Ownership:** Anderson-Tully's actions of marking borders with blue paint, repainting, cutting timber, performing timber-stand improvements, and issuing hunting licenses were sufficient to "fly its flag" and put the owner on notice. Scott's payment of taxes was not dispositive.
- **Actual or Hostile:** Anderson-Tully's marking, managing, harvesting timber, and issuing hunting licenses without permission demonstrated hostile possession. Given the "wild" nature of the land, these public acts of ownership were sufficient.
- **Open, Notorious, and Visible:** The blue paint lines, timber harvesting, and hunting licenses were visible and known in the community, fulfilling the requirement to "unfurl his flag."
- **Continuous and Uninterrupted:** Anderson-Tully's actions from 1969 to 2010, including marking boundaries and managing timber, demonstrated continuous possession for more than the ten-year statutory period. Scott's objections in 2003 and 2007 came after Anderson-Tully's claim had already ripened.
- **Exclusive:** No evidence showed the Scotts or anyone else used the property east of the fence after 1969. Anderson-Tully leased the land for hunting and its employees were not notified of others crossing the line.
- **Peaceful:** Anderson-Tully's possession was peaceful because no objections or interference occurred until 2003, well after the adverse possession period had ripened.

**Dissent ([Judge])**
None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the practical application of **adverse possession** elements, particularly how the nature of the land (e.g., "wild" or unimproved) can affect the "quantum of proof" required for certain elements. It connects to cases like *Howard v. Kunto* in discussing the continuity of possession, though *Kunto* focused on tacking and seasonal use. It also highlights the importance of visible acts of ownership, similar to the concept of "flying the flag" seen in other adverse possession cases. The court's discussion of the fence as a "convenience fence" versus a boundary marker is a common issue in boundary disputes.
2. The chancellor's finding that neither deed "accurately describe[s] the disputed tract to any certainty" but then finding adverse possession raises an interesting point about the relative importance of record title versus actual possession, especially when boundaries are unclear. What if the original deed *had* clearly included the disputed twenty acres for Scott? Would Anderson-Tully's adverse possession claim still have been as strong, or would the record title have carried more weight?

**Takeaway:** Adverse possession can establish title even when record title is ambiguous, provided the claimant's actions clearly demonstrate all elements of adverse possession for the statutory period.
