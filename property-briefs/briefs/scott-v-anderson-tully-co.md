## Scott v. Anderson-Tully Co. (Mississippi Court of Appeals, 2015)

**Facts**
- In 1925, Stewart Scott Jr. and Willie Scott inherited an undivided one-half interest in a 584.6-acre tract of land in Jefferson County, Mississippi.
- In 1944, a plat prepared for a timber deed from the Scotts noted a wire fence on the east side of the property and stated that "20 1/10 [acres] outside of fence is included in total acreage" but also that the property "outside of fence & may be disputed."
- In 1954, Richard Scott, an heir, rebuilt the wire fence, moving it slightly west along the tree line to avoid digging new post holes.
- In 1969, Anderson-Tully Company came into possession of an adjacent tract of land through a quitclaim deed.
- In 1969, Anderson-Tully marked its perceived boundary line along the wire fence with blue paint.
- From 1969 to 2010, Anderson-Tully maintained the disputed twenty acres, repainting the blue line in 1986 and 1998.
- Anderson-Tully cut and harvested timber from the disputed land in 1990, 1999, and 2010.
- Anderson-Tully issued five hunting licenses for the disputed property.
- In 1993, Herman Scott began paying property taxes on the disputed tract.
- In 2003, Scott objected to Anderson-Tully's possession.
- In 2007, Scott cancelled a hunting lease with the Linwood Hunting Club, which had also leased from Anderson-Tully.
- In December 2008, Anderson-Tully's attorney sent Scott a letter asserting ownership of the disputed tract.
- In early 2010, Anderson-Tully harvested timber from the disputed land.
- On March 19, 2010, Herman Scott, as administrator of Stewart Scott Jr.'s estate, filed suit.

**Procedural History**
- Scott filed suit in Jefferson County Chancery Court seeking damages for timber removal, an injunction, and to quiet and confirm title.
- Anderson-Tully counterclaimed to quiet and confirm its own title, asserting ownership by deed or, alternatively, by adverse possession.
- The chancellor dismissed Scott's claim, finding Anderson-Tully had acquired title through adverse possession.
- Scott filed a Rule 60(b) motion for relief from judgment or a new trial, which the chancellor denied.
- Scott appealed to the Mississippi Court of Appeals.

**Issue**
Did the chancellor err in finding that Anderson-Tully Company acquired title to the disputed twenty acres through adverse possession?

**Holding**
Defendant (Anderson-Tully) wins. The chancellor did not err in finding that Anderson-Tully proved adverse possession by clear and convincing evidence. Affirmed.

**Rules**
- **Adverse Possession (Mississippi).** To establish adverse possession, the claimant must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the possession was (1) under claim of ownership; (2) actual or hostile; (3) open, notorious, and visible; (4) continuous and uninterrupted for a period of ten years; (5) exclusive; and (6) peaceful.
- **Claim of Ownership.** The possessor must "fly [his] flag over the property" to put the actual owner on notice of an adverse claim.
- **Actual or Hostile Possession.** Possession is hostile when the possessor intends to claim title even under a mistaken belief that the land is within their deed, and without the owner's permission.
- **Actual or Hostile Possession (Wild Lands).** Possessory acts necessary for adverse possession may vary with the characteristics of the land; for "wild" or unimproved lands, the quantum of proof is lower, and possession may be established by continued public acts of ownership.
- **Open, Notorious, and Visible Possession.** The possessor must "unfurl his flag on the land, and keep it flying, so that the actual owner may see... that an enemy has invaded his domains."
- **Continuous and Uninterrupted Possession.** Possession must be maintained for at least ten years, and a mistaken belief about deed calls does not defeat this element if the land was occupied for the statutory period under a claim of ownership.
- **Exclusive Possession.** The possessor must evince an intention to possess and hold the land to the exclusion of all others, and their conduct must unequivocally indicate dominion of a sole owner, though it does not mean no one else can use the property.
- **Peaceful Possession.** The adverse possessor's use of the claimed property must be peaceful, and expected disputes associated with use or ownership are not indicative of non-peaceful possession.

**Reasoning**
- **Claim of Ownership:** Anderson-Tully marked its borders with recognizable blue paint along the fence, which multiple witnesses observed and understood to mark Anderson-Tully's property. They also cut timber, performed timber-stand improvements, and issued hunting licenses, all indicating an ownership claim. Scott's payment of taxes alone was not dispositive.
- **Actual or Hostile:** Anderson-Tully marked, managed, harvested timber, and issued hunting licenses without permission. The acts were sufficient for wild or unimproved land, where a lower quantum of proof is required.
- **Open, Notorious, and Visible:** The blue paint lines were visible and recognized in the community as Anderson-Tully's boundary. Timber cutting and hunting licenses were also public acts.
- **Continuous and Uninterrupted:** Anderson-Tully's possession began in 1969 and continued until Scott's suit in 2010, exceeding the ten-year statutory period. Scott's objections in 2003 and 2007 came after Anderson-Tully's claim had already ripened.
- **Exclusive:** No evidence showed the Scotts or anyone else used the property east of the fence after 1969. Anderson-Tully leased the land for hunting and its employees testified no one crossed the blue line.
- **Peaceful:** Anderson-Tully's possession was peaceful because Scott's first dispute occurred in 2003, well after the ten-year statutory period for adverse possession had ripened.

None.

**NOTES:**
1. This case illustrates the application of the **adverse possession** doctrine, specifically how the elements are applied to "wild" or unimproved land, where the standard for "actual or hostile" possession is lower. It connects to cases like *Howard v. Kunto* by showing that continuous possession does not necessarily mean constant physical presence, especially for certain types of land. The court's emphasis on community recognition of the boundary (the blue paint and fence) highlights the importance of **open, notorious, and visible** acts in establishing notice to the true owner.
2. The chancellor noted that neither party's deed "accurately describe[s] the disputed tract to any certainty." This raises the question of how much ambiguity in original title documents impacts the burden of proof for adverse possession, or if it makes adverse possession more likely to succeed as a means of clarifying boundaries.

**Takeaway:** Adverse possession can be established on wild or unimproved land through public acts of ownership that provide notice to the true owner, even if those acts are not constant physical occupation.
