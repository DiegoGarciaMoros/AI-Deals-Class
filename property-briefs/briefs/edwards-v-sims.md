## Edwards v. Sims (Kentucky Court of Appeals, 1929)

**Facts**
- Lee sued Edwards, claiming that part of Edwards's Great Onyx Cave extended under Lee's land.
- The Edmonson circuit court ordered a survey of the cave, requiring surveyors to enter Edwards's land.
- Edwards appealed the order, but the appeal was dismissed because the order was interlocutory.
- Edwards then filed a petition for a writ of prohibition against Judge Sims to prevent enforcement of the survey order.

**Procedural History**
- The Edmonson circuit court ordered a survey of Edwards's cave.
- Edwards appealed the order, which was dismissed as interlocutory.
- Edwards filed an original proceeding in the Kentucky Court of Appeals seeking a writ of prohibition against Judge Sims.
- The Kentucky Court of Appeals denied the writ of prohibition.

**Issue**
Can a court order a survey of a property owner's subterranean land to determine if they are trespassing on a neighbor's property?

**Holding**
The respondent (Judge Sims) wins. A court has the inherent power to order an inspection of subterranean property, such as a cave, to ascertain the truth of a matter before it, provided certain conditions are met. The writ of prohibition is denied.

**Rules**
- **Writ of Prohibition (Jurisdiction).** A writ of prohibition will issue where an inferior court acts without jurisdiction and there is no remedy through appeal.
- **Writ of Prohibition (Erroneous Exercise of Power).** A writ of prohibition will issue where an inferior court possesses jurisdiction but is exercising its power erroneously, resulting in great injustice and irreparable injury, and there is no adequate remedy by appeal.
- **Ad Coelum Doctrine.** The owner of realty, unless there has been a division of the estate, is entitled to the free and unfettered control of his own land above, upon, and beneath the surface, meaning whatever is in a direct line between the surface of the land and the center of the earth belongs to the owner of the surface.
- **Equity Power to Order Inspection.** A court of equity has the inherent power, independent of statute, to compel a mine owner to permit an inspection of his works at the suit of a party who can show reasonable ground for suspicion that his lands are being trespassed upon.
- **Conditions for Inspection Order.** For an inspection order to be valid, the applicant must show a bona fide claim, allege facts showing a necessity for the inspection, and the party whose property is to be inspected must have had an opportunity to be heard.

**Reasoning**
- The court has jurisdiction over the parties and the subject matter; the question is whether the court is proceeding erroneously.
- The maxim "Cujus est solum, ejus est usque ad coelum ad infernos" (to whomsoever the soil belongs, he owns also to the sky and to the depths) establishes the owner's right to control land above, upon, and beneath the surface.
- There is little difference in principle between caves and mines for the purpose of ordering an inspection.
- Courts of equity have inherent power to order inspections of property, similar to requiring the production of books and papers, to establish justice.
- A temporary invasion for inspection does not deprive the owner of title or permanent use, only a temporary and limited interruption of exclusive use.
- The conditions for such an order (bona fide claim, necessity, opportunity to be heard) were met in this case.
- The survey is imperative and necessary for the administration of justice because opinion evidence was of little value, and a survey will accurately settle the controversy.
- Since the circuit court is not exceeding its jurisdiction or proceeding erroneously, the claim of irreparable injury is not relevant for a writ of prohibition.

**Dissent (Judge Logan)**
- The majority opinion allows for incalculable injury to Edwards without benefiting Lee.
- The age-old maxim "Cujus est solum, ejus est usque ad coelum ad infernos" is not true now and never has been; it should be reformed.
- The rule should be that a surface owner owns everything they can use for profit or happiness and subject to their dominion, but nothing more.
- A man should not own what he cannot use and which is of no benefit to him, but may be of benefit to others (e.g., air for airplanes, vacant spaces in caves).
- A cave should belong absolutely to whoever owns its entrance and has explored and developed it, as Edwards did through discovery, exploration, and exhibition.
- Edwards created value in the cave through his labor and investment, and the survey will destroy that value by making his secrets bare and potentially stopping visitors.
- Equity should not destroy what belongs to one man when the party requesting the destruction is not benefited, even if they have some legal right.

**NOTES:**
1. This case is a classic example of the **ad coelum doctrine** and its limitations. It connects to **Hinman v. Pacific Air Transport** by showing how the "to the heavens" part of the doctrine is limited by practical use, and the dissent explicitly references the airplane analogy. It also relates to **Jacque v. Steenberg Homes** in that it involves a court ordering a temporary physical invasion of land, but here the invasion is for discovery rather than necessity.
2. The tension between the traditional ad coelum doctrine and the practical realities of modern property use (e.g., air travel, subterranean features) is a key point. A hypo could be: If a valuable mineral deposit were discovered deep under Edwards's cave, but only accessible from Lee's land, would the court still order an inspection?

**Takeaway:** While the traditional ad coelum doctrine grants ownership from the surface to the center of the earth, courts retain inherent equitable power to order temporary invasions of subterranean property for discovery in litigation.
