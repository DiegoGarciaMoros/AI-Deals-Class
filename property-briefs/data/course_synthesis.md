# How My Property Casebook Fits Together

## The Big Picture

These 91 cases reveal that owner sovereignty is real but far from absolute. Owners prevailed in only 40 cases, lost in 44, and achieved mixed results in 7. The trend has shifted over time: before 1960, owners won 21 cases and lost 18, but from 1960 onward, they won just 19 while losing 26 (with 7 mixed). The casebook shows property rights constantly negotiated against competing values—productive use appears in 46 cases, public rights in 34, and equity or hardship in 17. Courts justify their decisions through fairness and equity (52 cases), but also through precedent and formalism (42), utilitarian efficiency (38), and settled expectations (38). The right to exclude appears in 41 cases and the right to use in 59, but these "sticks" bend when they collide with necessity, public welfare, or the claims of good-faith possessors. Property emerges not as dominion but as a bundle of rights shaped by context, with courts choosing flexible standards (46 cases) nearly as often as bright-line rules (35).

## Threads That Run Through the Course

### Necessity and the Right to Exclude

The right to exclude—celebrated in *Jacque v. Steenberg Homes* with punitive damages for intentional trespass—gives way when human need is urgent. *Ploof v. Putnam* allows a sailor to moor at another's dock during a storm, treating necessity as a defense to trespass. *Berg v. Wiley* goes further, prohibiting even landlords from using self-help to exclude tenants, requiring judicial process instead. But *Intel v. Hamidi* shows limits: the right to exclude from digital property requires proof of actual harm, not just unwanted presence. These cases trace a line from physical emergency (*Ploof*) to structural power imbalances (*Berg*) to the question of what "exclusion" even means in intangible spaces (*Intel*).

### First Possession vs. Productive Use and Equity

*Pierson v. Post* establishes that mere pursuit doesn't create property rights in wild animals—you need actual capture. But *Ghen v. Rich* carves out an exception when industry custom makes physical possession impractical, protecting whalers who mark their kills. *Haslem v. Lockwood* rewards the labor of gathering abandoned manure, while *Wetherbee v. Green* goes further: a good-faith improver who transforms stolen timber into barrel hoops can keep them by paying for the original materials. These cases show courts moving from formalist rules about possession toward equity-driven concern for labor and investment, though *Anderson v. Gouldberg* reminds us that even wrongful possession beats a subsequent wrongdoer's claim.

### Time Creates and Destroys Rights

Fourteen cases turn on how time shifts entitlements. *Scott v. Anderson-Tully* allows adverse possession to extinguish title when the true owner sleeps on their rights, while *Howard v. Kunto* permits "tacking" of possession periods when good-faith buyers rely on mistaken boundaries. *Songbyrd v. Estate of Grossman* applies similar logic to chattels: open acts of ownership start the limitations clock. But time can also preserve rights: *Carpenter v. Ruperto* denies adverse possession when the claimant knows they're trespassing, requiring good faith. *Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.* creates a "first in time, first in right" system for water in arid regions, making priority itself a property right. *Nebraska v. Iowa* distinguishes gradual change (accretion shifts boundaries) from sudden change (avulsion preserves them), showing that the *pace* of time matters as much as its passage.

### The Transformation of Landlord-Tenant Law

Traditional property doctrine treated leases as conveyances, protecting landlords' rights to rent and limiting tenant defenses. *Blackett v. Olanoff* begins the shift by recognizing constructive eviction when a landlord fails to control another tenant's nuisance. *Javins v. First National Realty* completes the revolution, imposing an implied warranty of habitability and treating leases as contracts for shelter, not land transfers. *Medico-Dental v. Horton & Converse* makes lease covenants dependent rather than independent, allowing tenants to withhold rent for material breaches. Yet *Gotlieb v. Taco Bell* shows limits: landlords who re-let premises may inadvertently accept surrender, cutting off future rent claims. These cases move from property formalism toward consumer protection, though *Jaber v. Miller* suggests courts will still honor parties' intent over rigid common-law categories.

### Public Rights Limiting Private Ownership

*Illinois Central Railroad v. Illinois* establishes that states hold navigable waters in an inalienable public trust, preventing wholesale privatization. *State ex rel. Thornton v. Hay* extends this through custom, recognizing public easements over dry-sand beaches based on long use. *Southern Burlington County NAACP v. Township of Mount Laurel* applies similar logic to zoning, requiring municipalities to provide their "fair share" of affordable housing rather than exclude by income. *Shelley v. Kraemer* prohibits courts from enforcing racially restrictive covenants, finding state action in judicial enforcement. But *Fair Housing Council v. Roommate.com* protects owners' right to discriminate when choosing roommates for shared living spaces, privileging intimate association. The thread runs from natural resources (*Illinois Central*) through community spaces (*Thornton*) to housing markets (*Mount Laurel*), with constitutional limits on how far public rights can penetrate the home (*Roommate.com*).

### Economic Development and the Police Power

*Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty* upholds comprehensive zoning as a valid exercise of police power, even when it reduces property values. *Harbison v. City of Buffalo* allows municipalities to eliminate nonconforming uses through amortization, forcing businesses to relocate after reasonable notice. *Kelo v. City of New London* extends eminent domain to economic development takings, permitting condemnation for transfer to private developers. Yet *Pennsylvania Coal v. Mahon* sets a limit: regulations that go "too far" become takings requiring compensation. *Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council* creates a categorical rule for regulations eliminating all economic use, while *Penn Central v. New York City* adopts a flexible balancing test for partial restrictions. These cases show courts struggling to define when public benefit justifies private loss, with the answer depending on whether courts emphasize democratic process (*Euclid*, *Kelo*) or individual rights (*Pennsylvania Coal*, *Lucas*).

### Equity Tempering Formal Rights

Courts repeatedly use equity to soften property's hard edges. *Spur Industries v. Del E. Webb* enjoins a lawful feedlot as a public nuisance but requires the developer who "came to the nuisance" to pay relocation costs. *Golden Press v. Rylands* denies an injunction for a minor, unintentional encroachment when removal costs would be disproportionate. *Holbrook v. Taylor* transforms a revocable license into an irrevocable easement when the licensee builds a house in reliance. *Skendzel v. Marshall* treats land contracts as mortgages requiring foreclosure rather than forfeiture, protecting vendees' equity. But equity has limits: *Pile v. Pedrick* orders removal of even a small encroachment when the trespass was knowing, and *Warsaw v. Chicago Metallic* grants a mandatory injunction without compensation when the encroacher acted willfully. The pattern suggests equity protects good-faith reliance but punishes deliberate overreaching.

## Rules vs. Standards

The casebook divides almost evenly between bright-line rules (35 cases) and flexible standards (46), with the choice often reflecting competing policy concerns. Courts choose rules when they prioritize administrability and settled expectations: *Pierson v. Post* adopts a clear capture rule for wild animals rather than rewarding mere pursuit; *Pocono Springs v. MacKenzie* holds that fee simple cannot be abandoned, preventing strategic title dumping; *Berg v. Wiley* categorically prohibits landlord self-help evictions. Rules also appear when courts want to limit government power: *Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council* creates a categorical taking when regulations eliminate all economic use, and *Adams v. Cleveland-Cliffs* maintains a strict distinction between trespass (requiring tangible intrusion) and nuisance.

Standards dominate when courts value context-sensitive fairness. Nuisance law exemplifies this: *Hendricks v. Stalnaker* balances the gravity of harm against social utility, while *Jost v. Dairyland Power* allows damages even for socially beneficial activities. Water rights shift from the rigid riparian system (*Evans v. Merriweather*) to reasonable use balancing (*Higday v. Nickolaus*). Takings doctrine after *Penn Central* applies an "ad hoc factual inquiry" considering economic impact, investment-backed expectations, and the character of government action. *Delfino v. Vealencis* uses a flexible approach to partition, preferring division in kind when one co-owner's livelihood depends on continued possession.

The mixed category (10 cases) often reflects courts hedging their bets. *Jacque v. Steenberg Homes* announces a rule protecting the right to exclude but leaves punitive damages to jury discretion. *Edwards v. Sims* applies the ad coelum doctrine but allows equitable exceptions for discovery. *Spur Industries* creates a novel remedy—injunction with compensation—that doesn't fit standard categories. These hybrid approaches suggest courts recognize that neither pure rules nor pure standards adequately capture property's complexity.

## Property Rules vs. Liability Rules

The casebook strongly favors property rule protection (55 cases) over liability rules (28), with property rules allowing owners to refuse any transaction and obtain injunctive relief. *Jacque v. Steenberg Homes*, *Campbell v. Seaman*, and *Pardee v. Camden Lumber* all grant injunctions protecting the right to exclude or preserve property's character. *Clark v. Maloney* and *Anderson v. Gouldberg* protect even wrongful possessors with property rules against subsequent takers. Entity property cases like *Nahrstedt v. Lakeside Village* and *Levandusky v. One Fifth Avenue* use property rules to enforce community restrictions.

Liability rules appear when courts want to permit socially valuable activities while compensating losers. *Jost v. Dairyland Power* allows a power plant to continue operating but requires it to pay for crop damage. *Hinman v. Pacific Air Transport* limits airspace rights to what owners actually use, implicitly allowing overflights without trespass liability. *Golden Press v. Rylands* and *Edwards v. Allouez Mining* deny injunctions for encroachments, leaving plaintiffs to damages. *Spur Industries* creates a hybrid: an injunction (property rule) but with compensation to the enjoined party (liability rule feature).

The choice often reflects power dynamics and transaction costs. *Murphy v. Financial Development* imposes a duty of due diligence on foreclosing mortgagees, using liability rules to protect vulnerable debtors. *Moore v. Regents of University of California* denies property rights in excised cells but recognizes informed consent claims, channeling disputes toward disclosure rather than ownership. *Intel v. Hamidi* requires actual harm for trespass to chattels, effectively creating a liability rule for digital intrusions. Five cases provide no entitlement protection at all, typically when courts reject the claimed property right entirely (*Kelo v. City of New London*, *Charles River Bridge v. Warren Bridge*).

## Time and Possession

Time operates as both sword and shield across multiple doctrines. Adverse possession rewards long possession at the expense of absent owners: *Scott v. Anderson-Tully* transfers title after the statutory period of open, notorious, and hostile use. *Howard v. Kunto* allows "tacking" when successive possessors share privity, while *Carpenter v. Ruperto* denies adverse possession without good faith, showing that subjective intent matters despite the doctrine's objective elements. *Warsaw v. Chicago Metallic* establishes prescriptive easements through similar logic, creating permanent rights from long use.

Statutes of limitations create parallel effects for personal property. *Songbyrd v. Estate of Grossman* starts the clock when a possessor openly exercises dominion inconsistent with the owner's rights, not when the owner discovers the conversion. This rewards diligent owners and punishes those who sleep on their rights.

Time also determines boundaries through accretion and avulsion. *Nebraska v. Iowa* holds that gradual, imperceptible changes shift riparian boundaries, but sudden changes preserve the old line. This distinction reflects the policy that slow changes allow adjustment while sudden shifts would create chaos. *Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.* makes priority in time the basis for water rights in prior appropriation states, converting temporal sequence into a property entitlement.

But time doesn't always favor possessors. *Haslem v. Lockwood* protects a gatherer's claim to manure only for a "reasonable time" before others can take it. *Brokaw v. Fairchild* prevents life tenants from making fundamental alterations that would bind future interest holders, protecting rights that vest only with time's passage. *Harbison v. City of Buffalo* allows municipalities to eliminate nonconforming uses through amortization, using time to phase out incompatible activities. *Skendzel v. Marshall* and *Wood v. Donohue* show that time can create equitable interests even without legal title, as vendees under land contracts gain rights that grow stronger with each payment.

## Tensions to Raise in Class

- **Exclusion as fundamental vs. exclusion as contingent**: *Jacque v. Steenberg Homes* treats the right to exclude as so fundamental that even nominal trespass warrants punitive damages, while *Ploof v. Putnam* subordinates exclusion to necessity and *State ex rel. Thornton v. Hay* allows public custom to override it entirely. Is exclusion the core of property or just one stick among many?

- **Formalism vs. equity in encroachments**: *Pile v. Pedrick* orders removal of even a minor, unintentional foundation encroachment, emphasizing the sanctity of boundaries, but *Golden Press v. Rylands* denies an injunction for a similar encroachment when removal costs are disproportionate. When should courts balance hardships, and when should they enforce rights regardless of consequences?

- **First possession vs. productive use**: *Pierson v. Post* rewards capture over pursuit, privileging clear rules and actual control, while *Ghen v. Rich* and *Haslem v. Lockwood* protect those who invest labor even without complete possession. Should property law reward those who get there first or those who add value?

- **Owner autonomy vs. public welfare in takings**: *Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council* requires compensation when regulations eliminate all economic use, treating property rights as presumptively inviolable, but *Penn Central v. New York City* and *Kelo v. City of New London* defer to government judgments about public benefit. How much can the state diminish private property without paying for it?

## Questions to Ask of Any Property Dispute

1. **What "sticks" in the bundle are at issue—exclude, use, possess, transfer, income, destroy?** The answer shapes both the strength of the claim and available remedies. (*Jacque v. Steenberg Homes*, *Moore v. Regents of University of California*, *Eyerman v. Mercantile Trust*)

2. **Is this a rule or standard jurisdiction, and what does that choice reveal about policy goals?** Rules favor predictability and administrability; standards favor fairness and context. (*Pierson v. Post*, *Hendricks v. Stalnaker*, *Penn Central v. New York City*)

3. **Should the entitlement be protected by a property rule (injunction) or liability rule (damages)?** Consider transaction costs, relative hardship, and whether the activity should be permitted at a price. (*Campbell v. Seaman*, *Jost v. Dairyland Power*, *Spur Industries v. Del E. Webb*)

4. **Has time shifted rights through adverse possession, prescription, accretion, or limitations?** Possession plus time can create or extinguish title. (*Scott v. Anderson-Tully*, *Howard v. Kunto*, *Nebraska v. Iowa*, *Songbyrd v. Estate of Grossman*)

5. **What competing values are in tension—productive use, public rights, equity, reliance, custom?** Courts rarely protect property rights absolutely; they balance against other social goods. (*Illinois Central Railroad v. Illinois*, *Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.*, *Southern Burlington County NAACP v. Township of Mount Laurel*)

6. **Did the parties act in good faith or willfully?** Good faith triggers equity's protection; willfulness invites harsh remedies. (*Wetherbee v. Green*, *Carpenter v. Ruperto*, *Pile v. Pedrick*, *Warsaw v. Chicago Metallic*)

7. **Is this a traditional property form or a novel claim requiring court-made law?** Courts extend property concepts cautiously to new contexts. (*International News Service v. Associated Press*, *Moore v. Regents of University of California*, *Intel v. Hamidi*)

8. **What remedy is sought, and is it adequate to the harm?** The choice between damages, injunction, restitution, or declaratory relief often determines the outcome. (*Pardee v. Camden Lumber*, *Edwards v. Allouez Mining*, *Olwell v. Nye & Nissen*)
