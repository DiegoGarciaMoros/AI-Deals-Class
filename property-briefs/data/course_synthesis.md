# How My Property Casebook Fits Together

## The Big Picture

These 34 cases reveal a fundamental shift in how courts understand owner sovereignty. Before 1960, owners prevailed in 9 of 13 cases, but from 1960 onward, they lost in 11 of 21 disputes (with 2 mixed outcomes). This reversal reflects courts increasingly weighing competing values against ownership claims: productive use appears in 15 cases, public rights in 13, and reliance or repose in 9. While 17 cases still invoke fairness and equity to justify outcomes, courts now balance owner autonomy (invoked in only 9 cases) against settled expectations (16 cases), administrability (16 cases), and utilitarian efficiency (12 cases). The right to exclude—once nearly absolute—appears in only 17 cases, while use rights dominate with 22 cases. Most tellingly, courts chose flexible standards over bright-line rules in 20 of 34 cases, suggesting that modern property law resists the rigid formalism that once protected owners. Property rules still protect most entitlements (19 cases), but liability rules now govern 12 disputes, allowing courts to redistribute losses when exclusion seems too harsh.

## Threads That Run Through the Course

### Necessity and the Right to Exclude

The right to exclude—property's most celebrated stick—bends when necessity or public values intervene. *Ploof v. Putnam* establishes that life-threatening emergencies override exclusion rights, allowing a sailor to moor at a private dock during a storm. *State ex rel. Thornton v. Hay* extends this logic through custom, recognizing a public easement over dry-sand beach areas based on long-standing recreational use. *Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois* goes further, holding that states cannot alienate submerged lands held in public trust, making certain resources inalienable regardless of owner consent. But *Pile v. Pedrick* shows the traditional rule's persistence: even a minor, unintentional foundation encroachment must be removed to vindicate the landowner's exclusion right. The tension between these cases defines modern property: exclusion remains the default, but courts now recognize categorical exceptions when community needs, custom, or emergency override individual sovereignty.

### Time as a Property-Maker

Seven cases demonstrate how the passage of time can create or extinguish ownership. *Scott v. Anderson-Tully Co.* and *Howard v. Kunto* show adverse possession rewarding long-term possessors who use land openly, with *Howard* allowing "tacking" of successive possession periods when parties rely on mistaken boundaries. *Carpenter v. Ruperto* limits this by requiring good faith, but the general principle remains: time plus possession defeats record title. *Songbyrd Inc. v. Estate of Grossman* applies similar logic to chattels, holding that open acts of ownership start the limitations clock even without formal demand. *Nebraska v. Iowa* creates the opposite rule for boundary disputes: gradual accretion shifts borders with the river, but sudden avulsion freezes them in place. *Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.* makes priority in time the foundation of western water rights, replacing riparian ownership with "first in time, first in right." *State ex rel. Thornton v. Hay* uses immemorial custom to create public easements. Across these contexts, time transforms possession into ownership—but only when accompanied by the right kind of use, notice, or community recognition.

### The Productive Use Imperative

Fifteen cases invoke productive use as a competing value, revealing courts' preference for active exploitation over passive ownership. *Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.* explicitly rejects riparian rights in favor of prior appropriation because arid regions require water to be diverted for agriculture. *Scott v. Anderson-Tully Co.* rewards the adverse possessor who cleared and used unimproved land. *Evans v. Merriweather* and *Higday v. Nickolaus* adopt reasonable use standards for water that prioritize beneficial consumption over abstract ownership. *Ghen v. Rich* protects whalers' customary rights because the industry requires investment before physical possession. But *Brokaw v. Fairchild* shows limits: a life tenant cannot demolish a mansion to build apartments, even if more profitable, because waste doctrine protects the testator's intent and the remaindermen's inheritance. *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands* and *Edwards v. Allouez Mining Co.* deny injunctions against encroachments when removal costs vastly exceed the harm, effectively forcing landowners to accept productive uses that slightly invade their property. The message is clear: ownership that blocks productive use receives less protection than ownership that facilitates it.

### Custom and Community Norms

Four cases explore whether local practices can override formal property rules. *Ghen v. Rich* holds that whaling industry customs establish ownership rights when traditional first-possession rules prove unworkable—the whale's body sinks, so the custom of marking it with the killer's identifying mark creates property. *State ex rel. Thornton v. Hay* uses ancient custom to create public easements over private beaches, finding that long-standing recreational use satisfies the requirements for customary rights. *Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.* recognizes mining camp customs as the foundation for prior appropriation water law in the arid West. *United States v. Corrow* enforces federal law protecting Navajo cultural patrimony, acknowledging that communal ownership traditions can make certain items inalienable by individuals. These cases suggest that property law accommodates local knowledge and practice, but only when customs are long-standing, reasonable, and serve important community functions—not merely individual convenience.

### The Commodification Boundary

Five cases ask which resources can be bought and sold. *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* denies property rights in excised cells that researchers transformed into a valuable cell line, reasoning that recognizing such rights would hinder medical research and that patent law already allocates the resulting value. *Hecht v. Superior Court* reaches the opposite conclusion for cryopreserved sperm, treating it as a unique form of property subject to testamentary disposition. *Flynn v. Holder* splits the difference: bone marrow collected through blood filtration can be compensated, but aspirated marrow cannot, based on statutory text distinguishing "organs" from "blood." *United States v. Corrow* holds that Navajo ceremonial masks are cultural patrimony that individuals cannot alienate. *ProCD Inc. v. Zeidenberg* enforces contractual restrictions on database resale, showing that information can be commodified through licensing even when copyright doesn't apply. The pattern reveals judicial caution about expanding property rights into body parts and cultural objects, while readily enforcing contractual commodification of information.

### Equity's Limits on Property Rules

Courts invoke equity to soften property's harsh edges, but not consistently. *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands* refuses to order removal of foundation footings that encroach two inches, finding the $25,000 removal cost grossly disproportionate to the minimal harm. *Edwards v. Allouez Mining Co.* similarly denies an injunction against sand deposits when the landowner's motive appears speculative and damages suffice. *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C.* rejects automatic injunctions even for patent infringement, requiring case-by-case equity analysis. But *Pile v. Pedrick* orders removal of a similarly minor encroachment, and *Berg v. Wiley* awards damages for wrongful lockout even though the tenant breached the lease. *Producers Lumber & Supply Co. v. Olney Building Co.* denies equitable relief to a good-faith improver who demolished the structure he built. *Eyerman v. Mercantile Trust Co.* blocks testamentary destruction of a valuable house as contrary to public policy. The thread connecting these cases is judicial discretion: equity intervenes when strict property rules produce waste or disproportionate hardship, but not when doing so would undermine settled expectations or reward bad faith.

### Bodies, Persons, and Property

Three cases probe the boundary between persons and things. *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* holds that patients lack property rights in cells removed during treatment, even when researchers commercialize them, though informed consent requirements protect patient autonomy. *Hecht v. Superior Court* treats reproductive material differently, recognizing the decedent's right to direct posthumous use of his sperm as a form of property subject to his will. *Flynn v. Holder* interprets organ donation statutes to permit compensation for some forms of marrow donation but not others, based on collection method rather than biological function. These cases reveal deep ambivalence: courts resist full commodification of body parts (protecting research and preventing markets in organs) while recognizing limited property-like rights when necessary to vindicate autonomy, especially reproductive autonomy. The law treats the body as property for some purposes but not others, with no coherent principle explaining which.

## Rules vs. Standards

Eleven cases adopted bright-line rules while twenty chose flexible standards. The choice correlates with the stakes and the need for ex ante clarity. *Nebraska v. Iowa* uses a clear rule distinguishing accretion from avulsion because state boundaries require certainty. *Carpenter v. Ruperto* demands good faith for adverse possession as a bright line to prevent knowing trespassers from gaining title. *Berg v. Wiley* categorically prohibits landlord self-help to prevent violence. *Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.* makes priority in time a clear rule because water rights must be definite for irrigation planning. *Pocono Springs Civic Association v. MacKenzie* holds that fee simple cannot be abandoned, preventing strategic title dumping. *Cowen v. Pressprich* imposes strict liability on bailees who misdeliver to protect commercial certainty. *Intel Corp. v. Hamidi* requires actual harm for trespass to chattels, creating a clear threshold.

By contrast, flexible standards dominate when context matters more than predictability. *Strain v. Green* uses a multi-factor test for fixtures because annexation, adaptation, and intent vary by circumstance. *Scott v. Anderson-Tully Co.* and *Howard v. Kunto* apply adverse possession's open-and-notorious requirement flexibly based on the property's character. *Evans v. Merriweather* and *Higday v. Nickolaus* adopt reasonable use standards for water because competing uses require balancing. *State ex rel. Thornton v. Hay* evaluates custom case-by-case. *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands* and *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C.* apply equitable balancing to encroachments and injunctions. The pattern is clear: rules govern when parties need to plan (boundaries, priorities, abandonment) or when courts fear self-help violence; standards govern when fairness requires weighing competing uses, hardships, or community practices.

## Property Rules vs. Liability Rules

Nineteen cases protected entitlements with property rules (injunctions or title declarations), while twelve used liability rules (damages). Property rules dominate when exclusion rights are at stake and transaction costs are low. *Pile v. Pedrick* orders removal of an encroachment to vindicate the right to exclude. *Berg v. Wiley* awards damages but affirms that only judicial process can terminate possession. *Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois* declares public trust lands inalienable—the strongest property rule. *Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.* and *ProCD Inc. v. Zeidenberg* use injunctions to protect water rights and licensing restrictions. *Pocono Springs Civic Association v. MacKenzie* enforces covenant obligations through damages backed by the impossibility of abandonment.

Liability rules appear when property rules would be inefficient or inequitable. *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands* and *Edwards v. Allouez Mining Co.* force landowners to accept damages rather than removal because injunctions would waste resources. *Evans v. Merriweather*, *Higday v. Nickolaus*, and *Williams v. Ford Motor Credit Co.* use damages for water disputes and bailment because ongoing relationships require flexibility. *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* denies conversion claims for cells, effectively allowing nonconsensual taking with compensation through informed consent doctrine. *Ploof v. Putnam* permits necessity-based trespass but implies compensation for any damage. *Intel Corp. v. Hamidi* denies any remedy absent actual harm, creating a de facto privilege. *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C.* makes injunctions discretionary even for patent infringement, converting property rules to liability rules when public interest or holdup problems arise. The shift from property to liability rules reflects modern courts' willingness to force involuntary exchanges when exclusion seems wasteful or disproportionately harmful.

## Tensions to Raise in Class

- **Productive use vs. testamentary freedom**: *Brokaw v. Fairchild* prevents a life tenant from demolishing a mansion even if apartments would be more valuable, while *Eyerman v. Mercantile Trust Co.* blocks testamentary destruction of the same house type. Both limit owners' use and destruction rights, but *Brokaw* protects dead-hand control while *Eyerman* rejects it—when does the testator's intent govern, and when does productive use override it?

- **Good faith in adverse possession**: *Carpenter v. Ruperto* requires good faith and denies title to knowing trespassers, but *Scott v. Anderson-Tully Co.* and *Howard v. Kunto* grant title based on open use regardless of the possessor's state of mind. Does adverse possession reward honest mistakes or productive use, and does the answer depend on whether the true owner was sleeping on their rights?

- **Encroachment remedies**: *Pile v. Pedrick* orders removal of a minor foundation encroachment at great expense, while *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands* refuses to order removal of similar footings, forcing the landowner to accept damages. Both involve unintentional, minimal encroachments—what explains the different outcomes, and should equity always trump the right to exclude when removal costs are high?

- **Property in body parts**: *Moore v. Regents of the University of California* denies property rights in excised cells to promote research, but *Hecht v. Superior Court* recognizes property rights in sperm to honor reproductive autonomy. Both involve biological materials with commercial or personal value—why does autonomy justify property rights in one context but not the other?

## Questions to Ask of Any Property Dispute

1. **Who had prior possession or priority in time?** *Scott v. Anderson-Tully Co.*, *Howard v. Kunto*, *Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.*, *Songbyrd Inc. v. Estate of Grossman*—time plus possession often defeats record title.

2. **Is this a use dispute or an exclusion dispute?** *Evans v. Merriweather*, *Higday v. Nickolaus*, *Intel Corp. v. Hamidi*—use conflicts get standards and damages; exclusion claims get rules and injunctions.

3. **What custom or community practice governs this resource?** *Ghen v. Rich*, *State ex rel. Thornton v. Hay*, *Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co.*—local norms can override formal rules when reasonable and long-standing.

4. **Would enforcing the property right waste resources or create disproportionate hardship?** *Golden Press Inc. v. Rylands*, *Edwards v. Allouez Mining Co.*, *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange L.L.C.*—equity denies injunctions when costs vastly exceed benefits.

5. **Is the owner using the property productively?** *Scott v. Anderson-Tully Co.*, *Brokaw v. Fairchild*, *Eyerman v. Mercantile Trust Co.*—productive use strengthens claims; waste or destruction weakens them.

6. **Does public policy limit this property right?** *Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois*, *Moore v. Regents of the University of California*, *United States v. Corrow*—some resources cannot be privately owned or commodified.

7. **Did the parties have notice of the competing claim?** *Howard v. Kunto*, *Strain v. Green*, *Carpenter v. Ruperto*—open, notorious conduct and objective manifestations matter more than subjective intent.

8. **What remedy fits—property rule, liability rule, or no protection?** *Pile v. Pedrick* (injunction), *Ploof v. Putnam* (privilege with compensation), *Intel Corp. v. Hamidi* (no remedy)—the entitlement protection determines whether owners can exclude or must accept damages.
